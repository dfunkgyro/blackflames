# CHAPTER 16

## ABBA TALKA

*Lalibela — Low Week, 1326*

The deacon who came running for him at first light gave his two pieces of news in the wrong order. His wife had been delivered in the night of a girl, both of them living, and would Abba Talka come down and bless the child. And then, on the steps, as a thing hardly worth the breath: a Frank had come up with the Egyptians’ caravan at dusk, a priest of the Latins, and had stood at the monastery gate in the dark asking for the priest who kept the Mother’s house.

A man did not refuse a lying-in. Talka went down to the lower town with his mind on the gate behind him, and meant to be back at it before the Frank found anyone else to ask.

The house was round, of the hillside’s grey-red stone, and its lower room was full of women and smoke. The mother lay on hides behind a hanging cloth at the back. Someone had fed the hearth with dung cakes and then, for sweetness, a handful of juniper, and Talka breathed through his mouth. Under the smoke the room smelled of barley porridge and butter, and, faint and iron-sweet, of the night’s blood. Gebre had stopped outside in the lane with the water jar on his hip. He did not come into lying-in houses.

The grandmother had the child.

She was the mother’s mother, married down out of a ridge village fifty years since, and she had brought the hill ways with her. She knelt on a hide by the hearth with the bundle across her knees, and in front of her on the floor stood a small clay lamp, the kind the potters at the market sold ten to a string, with *nug* oil in it. Its flame was no bigger than a lentil. Its smoke smelled of the seed, nutty and close, under the juniper.

“Abba.” She did not get up. “Stand where you are. You’re letting the cold in.”

He stood bent under the lintel with the morning at his back.

“Abba Talka.” A voice behind him, from the lane. It said his name badly and the word after it worse. “*Kahen*?”

It was the plain word for any priest, but he said it the way it stood in the books, every letter given its full weight, as no tongue in Lasta had ever shaped it. One of the women by the hearth laughed, and stopped. Talka turned his head.

The man in the lane wore a long gown of undyed wool, belted with cord, and a round felt hat, and a wooden cross on a thong; and the sun had been at him for months. It had peeled the skin of his nose to a raw pink and left the backs of his hands blotched brown and white, and it had bleached his brows almost to nothing, so that his eyes, which were grey, seemed to have no frame. He was looking past Talka’s shoulder into the room. He was looking at the lamp.

Behind him in the lane Gebre had turned his face to the wall of the next house and stood very still with the jar on his hip.

“Later,” Talka said in Arabic. He did not know why he chose Arabic, except that the man would not have the other. “At the gate.”

The Frank inclined his head, courteous, and did not go.

The grandmother had taken up the lamp. She held it out over the child, low, a hand’s breadth from the small breastbone where the swaddling had been folded back, and she held it there, and asked it in Agaw, in her mother’s tongue and Talka’s mother’s, in the voice she used for asking after the weather:

“Is this one claimed?”

The flame stood on its wick.

It burned a little crooked, because the wick was cheap, and it shrank when the hanging cloth stirred, and it did nothing else. The grandmother counted under her breath, her lips moving, and the flame went on burning yellow and ordinary a hand’s breadth from the child’s heart.

Around the hearth the women breathed out. Talka heard it go round the room, one mouth after another, and someone said *Thanks be to God* to the rafters.

In the doorway the Frank had watched all of it: the lamp lifted, the question asked, the flame held at its distance. His face had not moved once. Now he touched his hat to the room, and turned away up the lane before the grandmother had drawn her next breath. Talka saw him go. The felt hat passed the corner of the next house, and Gebre’s back beside it did not move.

The grandmother did not look up. She set her free hand flat on the swaddling and began to name the dead.

She began with the mother’s line, as it was done in the hills. She said a name, and left a breath before it and a breath after, so that the name stood alone in the room with nothing touching it. Then the next. Her own mother. Her mother’s mother. Her husband, whom the famine had also taken, and Talka knew that one; he had buried him. Then she crossed to the father’s line and named the deacon’s father, and his father, and a brother who had gone to the King’s wars and not come back, each one alone.

The draught came in at Talka’s back. It lifted the hair on his neck and moved the women’s headcloths and set the hanging cloth swaying at the back of the room, and it went across the floor to the lamp in the grandmother’s hand.

The flame did not move.

It stood dead still on its wick in the moving air, straight up and narrow, and from its tip the smoke went up in a single thread, unbroken, to the smoke-black rafters. The headcloths stirred. The cloth swayed. The flame stood.

Last of all, after the longest breath, the grandmother said the child’s own name. *Tsige.* She left the breath after it too.

Then she set the lamp down on the floor and the draught found it at once, and it shrank to a bead and shook on its wick, and its one thread of smoke broke up into nothing, and it was a market lamp in a smoky room. She got up off her knees with her hand on a woman’s shoulder and brought the child across the floor and put her into Talka’s arms.

He made the cross over her and said the words. She weighed nothing. She smelled of butter and of her mother.

He had stood in a hundred doorways while the grandmothers did it, and in all that time he had never once thought of it as two things: an asking, and then a naming. It had been one thing, the way the hill women did a lying-in, as the planting and the threshing are one harvest. The Frank had watched the first half. Then he had put his hat on and gone.

Talka gave the child back.

The caravan had camped in the field below the monastery wall, and the camels lay folded in a row with their loads off and their lips going, and the field stank of them. Over the escarpment the clouds had been building since noon, and thunder walked about behind them, and no rain came.

The Frank was at the gate, sitting on the guest-bench in the shade of the wall with his hat on his knee, and he rose when he saw Talka come up the path.

“For the union,” he said, in Arabic, before anything else. It was a careful, learned Arabic, every word set down whole, the Arabic of a man who had read more of it than he had spoken. Talka had enough of the tongue from his years in the Egyptian bishop’s household to follow it. “I come from the brothers at Cairo; the Patriarch’s people let me travel with them. I am to carry home an account of your faith, so that the Holy Father may know how near it stands to ours.”

He said a name, a Frankish one, two hard syllables, and a city Talka had never heard of. Talka gave him his own.

“I know it,” said the Frank. “My brothers were here. Two years since.” He smiled. His lips were split, and the smile opened the splits. “One of them wrote of you. *Abba Talka, who keeps the house of Mary. He told us that they bury their dead facing east.*”

Talka said nothing.

“Your porter tells me,” the Frank went on pleasantly, “that an old master of this house, Abba Sertsa, if I have his name right, taught that a stranger is a gift of God.”

Abba Sertsa had said so, to the abbot. To the novices, on the floor, with the switch, he had said: when God sends a stranger, count the spoons.

The Frank waited, and when nothing came he smiled again. His eyes went past Talka through the open gate to the lamp a lay brother was trimming in the gate-niche, and rested on it, and came back. They went to the brazier where the porter was roasting barley, and to the coal he carried to it on a sherd. They went to every flame in the court, and stayed with each a moment, and came back.

He smelled of camel, and of the salt-dust of the low country, and of something else that Talka could not place until the wind came round: beeswax. Good beeswax, of the kind no caravan carried for its own use.

“I would like to ask you two things, Abba,” the Frank said. “Then I will not trouble you.”

“Ask.”

“Where does the woman of the burning lie?”

Thunder went along the mountain. The porter at the brazier stirred the barley, and the smell of it came across the court, warm and brown.

“We bury our dead,” Talka said. “Facing east. Your brother wrote it down.”

“He did. He wrote that you said it.” The Frank turned his hat round on his knee, once. “I am told there was a woman in a famine year, carried in dying by pilgrims from the north road, and that she died in your house of Mary and was not buried. I am not a judge. I ask only where what is left of her was put.”

“Who told you that?”

“The north road tells many things.”

Talka did not let his eyes go toward the place.

“Famine women come up that road every bad year,” he said, “and most of them die, and we bury them. If one was not buried, you were told a story. The pilgrims buy them at the gate.”

The Frank inclined his head, courteous, as he had in the lane.

“The second thing,” he said. “The lamp your grandmothers hold to newborns. I saw it this morning. I would like to understand it. What is it that they ask the flame?”

“It is women’s business.”

“I asked the women. They told me to ask the priest.”

“Then the priest tells you it is women’s business.”

The Frank laughed, and while he laughed his eyes went to the lamp in the gate-niche.

Down in the field the camel-men were unloading his mules at the guest-house door. One of them took a pack off by one strap, and it swung and struck the doorpost, and the cloth came open, and something fell out onto the dust: a flat dark thing, the size of a face.

It was a face. It was a mask of iron, plain and close-made, with holes for the eyes and none for the mouth, and it had fallen inner side up. The inside was polished, and the polish was crossed and crossed again with scratches, close-set, row on row: short strokes, longer ones, some cut deep and some scraped almost smooth, so that the iron was rough in long bands and bright in others. Talka had seen a herdsman’s tally cut in a stick like that, and a jailer’s cut in a wall. Near the lower edge the iron had been rubbed bare in a band the width of two fingers, and nothing was cut there yet.

The Frank did not hurry. He walked down and took the mask up out of the dust, folded the cloth back round it, and gave it to the camel-man with a word that made the man duck his head.

When he came back he did not speak of it, and Talka did not ask.

“I will stay a little while,” the Frank said. “Your abbot has been kind. Tomorrow, if he permits, I would hear the morning office in the house of Mary.” He put his hat on. “I am told it is the oldest.”

⁂

That night Talka did not go to his mat.

He sat in his cell with the door open on the court and a tallow lamp in the niche, and the air smelled of dust and of the dung fires of the town and of the dry, stale frankincense that came out of his own sleeve when he lifted his arm.

He lifted it again and smelled it on purpose.

Lalibela’s incense. He had stopped smelling it on himself before his beard came in. But from the second week after Timkat until Hosanna he had sat at Aksum in a mud room where Zion’s smoke came under the door, and Zion’s had a bite in it, some resin he had never put a name to. Lalibela’s was rounder and sweet, and it was in the beard and the wool of every man of this house who had swung a censer for any length of years.

*He smelled of our incense. Lalibela’s.*

He had carried the guardian’s last words south for twelve days beside the woman’s voice at the door, and they would not lie down together. A dead woman’s voice he could believe. A dead woman who smelled of Lalibela’s censers he could not.

So he took the voice away and looked at what was left.

The embassy had gone north by Aksum in June, before the rains. Tamrat had wanted it: to pray at Zion before the long road, so that the King’s letter should go out to the Franks from the house of the Ark.

Four of them, then, at the house of Zion. The soldier, who smelled of mules and iron and the oil he put on his sword. The woman, who carried cloves in her bundle; Talka had smelled them across his own table when she filled his cup. The boy, who smelled of oak-gall and soot-ink and had to be dragged out of the library to eat. And Tamrat, who had stood at the altar of Medhani Alem for forty years with the censer going, and carried its smoke in his beard into every room he entered.

And then Giyorgis, home before the feast of the Cross, telling it at the water jars as a thing to laugh at. At Massawa, on the last night, the brothers of the port had made a meal for the embassy, fish and the white bread of the coast, and the Qess would not sit down to it with anyone. He sat apart with his hands on his stomach and said the road had spoiled his belly, and Desta ate his share and said he would carry that for him too. Giyorgis had laughed telling it. Talka had laughed.

He did not laugh now.

A priest who would not eat at a common table. A finger-long hollow in brown linen, and nothing in it. A smell he knew from his novice years.

It had not been a ghost. He had sat among the stelae with the palm ring in his fist and blamed a woman he had burned for what his own priest had done with his own two hands. It was a man of this house, whom Talka had blessed at the gate on the morning he rode out.

And at the gate, on the morning they left—

It came back whole, the morning itself: the cold of the end of the dry season, the dust lying still and smelling of mule dung and the kolo the women had parched for the road; Tamrat making his speech to the brothers; the boy at the head of the line with his scrip slung low across his hip. Desta at the mule’s shoulder.

*Watch the boy’s bag. Not the boy.* That much he had always kept: the girth tested and the belly slapped, *The boy’s bag*, *Wherever he goes*, the soldier’s joke about the King’s baggage, the hands wiped on the thighs and the long look down the line. *Tell me why.*

Talka had opened his mouth.

He had told him, low, under Tamrat’s speech. He heard himself say it now.

“You have seen the grandmothers hold a lamp to a new child.”

“At my sister’s lying-in.” Desta had his thumbs in his belt. “I trimmed the wick for the old woman. It didn’t lean. She was disappointed; she wanted a saint.”

“It leans to what is claimed. There are men who hold candles to grown men the same way. The Franks who sat at our table watched our women do it the whole of a Timkat morning, and somebody showed one of them with his hands what it was for.”

Desta said nothing, and went on looking at him.

“The boy’s mother died in this house,” Talka had said. “She carried something. She put a thing into his fist the night she died, and the deacon who was nearest said it was nothing.” He had heard his own voice go flat on the word. “I have never believed him. Whatever she gave the boy, he has it still; he has kept that bag on him since he was seven. If any man holds a flame near that bag, on a road or a ship or in a king’s hall—”

“Then what?”

“Then stand where it is looking.”

Desta had turned his head, not to the boy this time but to the gate: to the lamp burning in its niche, and the porter’s brazier, and the candles the brothers held for the blessing of the road. He had looked at each of them, one after another, and measured the ground between each flame and the boy’s hip with his eye. Then he had walked up the line, not hurrying, and taken his place on the boy’s left, on the side the gate was, and stood there with his back to the lamps until the line moved off.

“Where it is looking,” he had said, over his shoulder, in the flat voice of a man repeating an order back to an officer.

That was all of it. For ten months he had not let it run past his own open mouth, and now it ran on to the end.

He had set a soldier to stand between a candle and a scrip. And on the same road he had sent a priest who carried, in his satchel or his clothes or God knew where, a piece of the oldest wood in the world: a thing every lamp in the land would lean to, if the old brothers were right, and the grandmothers. He had sent a candle’s whole attention walking at the boy’s side, and told the soldier to watch the wrong man’s bag.

He knelt on the bare floor of the cell, facing east through the wall.

He prayed for Desta by name, for a living man on a road. He said the name aloud and asked that the road be kind to him and the sea kinder; that his feet find dry land among the Franks and his sword stay in its scabbard; that he keep his eyes open and his counting short. He asked it for longer tonight than any night since June, and when he had finished he began again.

The lamp in the niche smoked. Outside, the thunder had gone round to the south and the dust smell was thick in the court. No rain came.

⁂

He did not eat in the morning. The day was the Frank’s: the Frank in the trenches with a lay brother, the Frank on the guest-bench writing in a little book on his knee, his eyes going up at every lamp the brothers lit. At dusk Giyorgis brought the evening bread across the court, his voice cracking with news.

“The abbot says yes. The Frank can hear the office in the Mother’s house. At the bell.” He set down the bread, and the bowl of shiro beside it, which smelled of fenugreek and the smoke of the kitchen. “He asked if the old paintings could be seen by lamplight. The abbot said they could.”

“Did he.”

Talka set the loaf on the board and covered it with the lid while he said the blessing. It was the lid he always used: woven grass, red and black, in the tight rings of the villages down the escarpment, its rim mended twice with a paler grass. Gebre sat by the door with his bowl on his knee and his right hand in his sleeve. Talka finished the blessing and lifted it off and laid it aside and took up the horn-handled knife.

He set the blade to the loaf, at the end, where the heel comes off, and pressed, and the crust broke under it.

His hand stopped.

The blade stood in the bread a finger deep. He could feel the crumb holding it. He did not know why he had stopped; there was nothing wrong with the loaf, nor with the knife, nor with his hand. It only stayed where it was, a breath, and then another breath, with the heel half-cut and the knife standing in it.

Giyorgis looked at the knife, and then at Talka, and said nothing. Gebre had stopped eating.

Talka cut the heel through.

He got up, and took it out across the court in the dusk to the gate, and laid it on the stone beside the gate, where he laid it every morning. The stone was still warm from the day, and the camels below the wall were groaning as they settled. He stood a moment with the empty hand. Then he went back and ate.

When he had eaten he wiped the knife and did not put it back on the board. He put it in his belt.

“Gebre,” he said. “Bring the oil, and a lamp. And the ladder from the Mother’s house.”

⁂

There was one lamp in the Mother’s house, and Gebre held it.

The ladder stood between the two nearest pillars, where the roof was highest, over a place Talka had not looked at in fifteen years except with his eyes on the floor. He looked at the floor first: bare tuff, red and damp, the rugs rolled back to the pillars by a novice who had been told nothing. It smelled of wet stone and of compline’s frankincense gone stale and sweet, and of the lamp-oil, which was only lamp-oil.

He had to know what was on the roof before the Frank stood under it with a lamp; if her writing named the boy, or his road, he had to know it before the bell.

Gebre stood at the ladder’s foot with the lamp in his left hand. The light went up off it a little way and stopped, and above the pillars’ tops the roof was a dark that might have been anything.

“Your hand,” Talka said.

Gebre gave it. Talka took the right wrist as one takes a pulse, at the place where the blood knocks, and drew the arm out of its sleeve and turned it to the lamp.

The line ran from the root of the first finger toward the heel, her signs, all of them, in their places. He counted them. After them the water, three waves one under another and the long flat stroke beneath that ran away to the edge of the palm. And past the water, where at Aksum there had been nothing, there was a small gap of bare skin; and then one sign more.

He knew it. It was the first letter, the one that stood at the head of the row, the one every child of Lalibela made with a stick in the dust of the court before he made any other. It was not one of her leaning, branching signs. It was his own letter, plain and upright, the first thing on that palm in fifteen years that he could read.

*[ — — — — — ] sea [ — ] አ*

The start of a word, then. A word not yet written. Gebre’s pulse went on under his fingers, quick and steady, and Gebre looked at the floor.

Talka let him go.

“Hold the ladder,” he said. “And hold the light up.”

He climbed. Gebre came up three rungs behind him and stopped there, with his shoulder set against the ladder’s side and the lamp lifted at the full length of his arm, so that the light came up between Talka’s knees and lay on the roof.

The paint was there, the ochre and red and white he had told the painters to lay the year after, the new crosses in their knots and the new beasts in the corners. Fifteen years of candle soot had dulled the white to the colour of old teeth. And through it, grey, row under row, her writing showed. From the floor it had looked faint. At the length of his arm it was not faint.

He took the knife out of his belt.

He chose the place without choosing. It was the end of her last row, where the rows stopped, below the knotted cross; the place where his own knife had squealed in Gebre’s hand that night. He set the edge to the paint and drew it toward him.

The paint came away in a flake, lime under the ochre, dry and white, and it fell past his face and caught in his beard. The soot came off on the blade in a black paste. He worked the edge back and forth over a space the breadth of his hand, and the smell rose round his head in the narrow light.

Lime first. Sharp, and clean, and dusty in the back of the nose: the smell of the painters’ trough, of the year after.

And then, under the lime, where the bare stone came through, another smell came up to meet him, cold, and he knew it before he could have said from where. Cold dust, old hide, ink gone brown. The treasury chest, when the brothers opened it on the great feasts to bring out the oldest books.

Her letters were black on the bare stone. Not grey. Black and fine and clean, the same as on the night.

His blade slipped.

It went in at a bad angle off a ridge of the tuff and skidded and dug, and it gouged straight through one of her letters, a gash the width of a barley straw through the middle of the sign, down into the red.

He held the knife still and watched.

The gouge filled. It filled from its edges, the black coming in from both lips of the cut toward the middle, slowly, and meeting there, as a cut in a child’s lip closes; and when the two edges had met there was no seam, and the letter was whole, and the stone round it was scraped and the letter was not.

He set the point to the same sign, on purpose this time, and drew it through.

It closed.

He stopped, and put the knife back in his belt.

He could read a little of it. Her signs were the grandfathers of his own letters, and here and there one had kept its grandson’s face; and he had sat among the stelae at Aksum and watched these same shapes stand up wet in the dawn under a Ge’ez line that said the same thing. He read with his lips, one sound at a time, with the lamp shaking a little below him because Gebre’s arm was tiring.

*A woman of the [ — ] will [ — ] a priest for the sanctum.*

The first gap would not come. The sign stood there with no grandson in it at all. The second was a root he half knew. It had the bones of a word he had sung nine nights ago at Fasika, in a road-church cut into a ridge in Tigray, among strangers, with the mule tied at the door: *he bound Satan*, the old cry; *asero*. To bind. It had other branches, that root, which he could not follow, and he did not try. Bind, then. A woman will bind a priest for the sanctum.

He thought of a priest bound. He thought of the oath at ordination, the bishop’s hands on the head; of a vow, and a vow broken. He thought of Tamrat, whom he now knew for what he was, in the dark of Zion with his hands in the chest. *For the sanctum.* A priest bound by his vow, and the vow turned. The first gap he left where it was. It would not come, and he did not make it.

There was more after it, and less of it would come. A sign that might be *once*, and further on a verb that might be *break*, and after it the sign for *again*. A priest who has broken once, and can be made to break again.

Again: that was Zion, the chest, the hollow in the linen. But once? He went back through forty years of the man and could not find the first breaking. Unless the first was Zion and the second was still to come, on some Frankish road, with his hands in some other chest. He did not know. He knew only that someone had looked for a priest who would break, and had found one, and that it had been written on his roof since the night of the bell.

At the end of the last row, among signs that gave him nothing, one more verb would half come. *Follow*, it might be, or *hunt*; the root held both. It stood in the row ahead of the woman of the first gap, as he read it, and not behind her.

Had they been on her road before she was ever on it?

The roof did not say. He could not make it say.

He held on to the ladder.

Then, below her last row, the three short lines.

These were not her letters. They were his own, the plain upright letters of the psalter, and they lay under the paint, grey, so that he had to put his face close to the ochre and tilt his head to make them out against the lamp. The first he knew at once. He had written that name himself, in the house’s book, the morning the deacon took the oil of his office.

*Gebre.*

But it did not stop there. After the name, the line went on: and that part of it did not lie under the paint. It lay on the paint, on the soot and the ochre both, black and sharp, with less of the candles’ grime over it than over anything else on the roof. It was new. It was newer than the painting by a great many years.

*opened his hand to the Latin, him with the white scar through the brow, black over white.*

His mind went to the six Franks at the long table two years ago, and to the short one with the burned red neck who had leaned across the bread to ask about women who are burned. A thin white seam had run through that man’s left eyebrow, parting the hair of it. Black cappa over a white habit. He had gone north with the two who were left.

He did not let himself go further. There were two lines more.

The second was short. It held a name he had written himself on the boy’s first slate: *Zoser*. After it the line went on the breadth of a finger, and there was one sign there half made, the stroke of it set down and the foot not yet, and then the bare paint.

The third line was his.

He knew it was his before he read it. The paint lay thick over it, and some of it would not come at all.

*For I have eaten [ — ] For I have eaten [ — — ] …ashes like bread… [ — — ] Talka, who gave his lamp, and with it Elleni.*

The verse he knew. It was the psalm for the dead, the hundred-and-first, the verse he had been singing under his breath at the north wall while he tipped the oil, and the fire had written its first words twice. He had lost his place in it. He remembered the shape where a remembering should be: his mouth open on nothing, and the verse going back to its first word.

*Who gave his lamp.* That was true. He had lit her from the lamp in his own hand.

*And with it Elleni.*

He said it aloud. His voice went up into the painted knots and came back off them, small.

“Talka, who gave his lamp, and with it Elleni.”

He could not think whose name it was. It was a common name; there were Ellenis in every village of Lasta. He went through the women he had known, the benefactors and the widows of the lower town and the mothers of novices, and none of them was it. Then the stool by the bed at Aksum came back to him, and the blind man’s fingers going round the rim of the lid, fifteen days ago: *And Elleni. You have left out Elleni, who brought you bread at the gate.* And his own voice, quite sure: *My father had no daughter.* He had put it with the fever, where it belonged. He looked at it again now, on the ladder, and it was still a sick man’s wandering, and he put it back. He said the line again, slower, and it gave him nothing back.

“And with it Elleni.”

Behind him and below him, Gebre made a sound.

It came out of his chest and through his shut teeth. It was not a word. It went up high in his throat and broke there, and it was the only sound Talka had heard out of him in fifteen years; and then the ladder shifted under Talka’s feet, because the weight had gone off its side, and the light went down.

Talka caught at the painted beast in the corner. The ladder steadied.

When he turned his head from the roof the light was not below him. It was in the doorway, at the far end of the church, and Gebre was there with it, on the sill, where the boy had stood. He held the lamp in his left hand, low, and the light came up off it onto his face, and his face was wet. The tears ran down into his beard and he did nothing to stop them. He looked at Talka on the ladder, at the knife in his belt and the lime in his hair, and then he lowered his eyes.

His right hand had come out of its sleeve. It hung open at his side, palm outward, in the light.

*Opened his hand to the Latin.*

Talka looked at the open hand in the doorway of the Mother’s house, black-lettered from finger to heel, and knew who had told the Latins where to ask; and thought he knew why the man was weeping.
