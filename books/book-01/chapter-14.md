# CHAPTER 14

## TAMRAT

*Castel Sant'Angelo — the night of Low Sunday*

*Four nicks.*

The compline bell came down through the slit from somewhere across the river, one bell and then the others answering it over the city, thin and late and out of time with one another; and before the last of them had stopped, Cencio came along the passage with his sniff.

Angeska was already at the grille. Tamrat heard the coin before he saw anything: the small dry click of a denaro laid on the iron of the bar. The lantern stopped. Its light lay in squares on the ceiling and did not move.

Cencio said something in the Roman speech, not unkindly. He said it again, slower, for the foreigners. Then the coin came back through the bars into her hand, and Tamrat heard that too.

"The steward's order," she said in Amharic, turning from the door. "No light for the condemned after compline. We may keep what we have." She did not soften it. "If nobody sees it."

The sniff went on down the passage, and the squares of light went with it, and the ceiling was stone again.

The candle stood on her ledge in the south-west corner, where it had stood every night since the first: a stump of grey tallow two fingers thick, with the knife-nicks cut down its side a thumb apart. From his straw along the east wall Tamrat could count them. It was the one thing in the cell his eyes were still good for. A psalter in his lap had become a field of ants these five years; but a candle two long paces off and more, standing in its own light, he could read nick by nick. From the pulpit of Bet Medhani Alem he had always known which deacon at the back was asleep.

It stank of mutton, Roman tallow, and of something under the mutton. The moat came in over it through the slit, green water and weed and the town's dung. And under both, in his own corner, the bile in the bucket. That was his.

Under the sash the old cramp lay half closed in his belly, where it had closed on him at sea and never wholly opened since. Under the cramp, low and to the right, the other thing lay where it had lain for a year: a warmth, which he did not name.

At dawn they would come for him. Cencio had told it on Easter morning with his red thumb and his folded fingers, and the octave was out tonight. Tamrat knew the rest without being told. The hangman takes the clothes; it is his fee. They would strip him at the bridge-head before the pitch, an old man naked in front of Rome. And afterwards, in a city that had set a pagan's ash on the point of a needle and charged pilgrims a coin to look at it, men would go through what the fire left of him with sieves. He had stood at Peter's door among the sellers of little lead keys. He knew what Rome did with what it found in the ashes.

He would not be opened for them. That was the whole of what he wanted now, and he wanted it in his bowels, in his knees, in the backs of his hands where the skin had gone thin.

He lay on his back and looked at the dark of the ceiling.

"They say I spoke in the hall," he said.

Nobody answered. The draught came down from the slit across the floor, and the candle shrank into its tallow and smoked, and stood up again.

"After I said yes to the Sicilian. Before the boy was holding me." He turned his head on the straw. "They say I spoke. What did it say with my mouth?"

He could see them both, at that distance, as edges. His wife in her corner, the candle low beside her, the shape of her head against the net of the stones. The boy along the north wall, sitting up, his knees drawn in. Their faces were nothing. In the hall there had been faces to judge by, guards crossing themselves, a prince of the Church gone white to the lips; he had seen all that afterwards, from the floor, without knowing what it was for. Here there was only the outline of two people holding still.

His wife did not move. Eighteen days she had answered every question he put to her, the price of the water, the meaning of the bells, *you were ill, husband*, every one but what the Latin had wanted at the grille on the Wednesday. That time she had put his bowl into his hands. She did not answer this one either.

The boy turned his face to the wall.

So it was bad. He had known that from the guards. He waited, and the candle burned, and the bucket stank, and the silence went on so long that he heard the moat lapping at the foot of the wall under the slit, which in eighteen days he had never heard.

"Clay," the boy said at last, to the stone, "carrying the potter."

Tamrat lay still.

He had said that once in his life. In the middle of a river, with his belly pressed to the back of the boy's neck and his teeth shut, and it had cost him more than any sermon he ever preached. He had not said it again. He had not needed to. The boy had nearly gone down in the black water for laughing, and that had been the whole of the payment, and he had been glad of it on the bank in his wet shamma, and told no one.

He laughed, once, through his nose.

"That one was mine," he said.

Whatever had stood up in him in the Cardinal's hall had turned out his pockets. It had found the one thing he had ever made against himself, and taken it out before the Latins, and said it in his voice.

He found, lying there, that he was not surprised. A year he had carried it. Mornings on the road he had woken with his jaw aching, the ache he had after the Feast of the Cross, when he preached from the third hour to the sixth; and he had preached no sermon. He had told himself it was the fast. He had told himself it was the road, and his age, and the food of the Franks. He had said nothing to anyone.

And every one of those mornings there had been a taste of dust behind his teeth, old dust, that no water shifted. It was there now.

*Three nicks.*

He did not decide to speak. It came up from the same place as the sickness, with as little leave.

"I am sorry," he said.

His wife's outline lifted its head.

"I'm so sorry." His voice went high and broke, and he heard it break against the stone and could not mend it. "I'm sorry… I'm sorry, Angeska… for everything."

He was off the straw and on his knees on the wet floor before he had told his knees anything, his hands out in front of him. He meant to go to her. He had got as far as one shuffle of the knees across the stone toward her corner when she rose.

She came to him across the cell, quickly, her shawl trailing, and knelt in front of him in the middle of the floor, between the bucket and the door, where there was nothing; and she took his two hands out of the air and held them down between them, on her knees.

"Tamrat." Low. Plain. "What did you do?"

He could smell her: the cloves from the little pot in her bundle, the moat in her shawl, her own breath gone sour from eighteen days of water and grey bread. He could not see her face. At the length of his arm, where his eyes were no use even in daylight, there was no daylight; the candle stood behind her in its corner, and she was a dark shape with a thread of yellow round its edge.

"I have no right to ask you to forgive me."

Her hands were still on his. Then they were not quite still.

"Is there…" She stopped, and began again, in a voice he had never heard her use, smaller than her own. "Is there another woman?"

He opened his mouth to say no. It was not what came.

"There was."

Her hands went still again.

"Ilfinesh," he said. "You know the name. Everyone at Lalibela knows the name. She sewed the crosses on my bag, crooked, and laughed when I said so. She is seventeen years by the church wall." The bile was in his throat. He swallowed it. "A priest marries once. Before the bishop's hand comes down on his head. If his wife dies, he lives alone, and says the office alone, and is buried alone. I knew that canon before I had a beard. I could recite you the councils it came down from."

She did not move.

"And I took you. At sixty. No bishop's blessing on it, and no bishop asked. Abba Talka looked at me at the church gate the morning after and said nothing, and I went in and served at the altar that same morning with my hands unwashed of it." His hands shook in hers. "There was a wife, and I buried her. And then there was you. You are my sin. That is the other woman."

In the Cardinal's hall he had told the Latins, through her mouth, that she was his wife and God had blessed it. She had carried that across for him, word for word. He thought of it now, and was ashamed again, in a new place.

She said nothing for a long time. In the corner the candle shrank in the draught and stood up.

He had knelt like this once before, in the rushes of a widow's house, with his thumb in his palm, praying God to hold his hand from her because she had come home with her hair braided by someone else's fingers and her hands smelling of someone else's soap. He thought of that too.

It was his own question. He did not go further into it.

"Are there children?" she said. "Somewhere?"

The word went into him and stopped there, and did not reach the bottom. He had not been asked it in seventeen years. Nobody at Lalibela asked a priest of sixty-four with a young wife whether there were children; they looked at the wife, and then at him, and asked about the harvest.

He looked at her, and at that length he could make out almost nothing. A shoulder. The pale line of the shawl's border. And one hand, which had left his, and lay flat on her own belly. She was tired. Eighteen days on stone. They were all tired.

Children.

Ilfinesh had carried three. Two sons and a daughter, in nine years, and not one had lived to be carried to the font: the boys not to their fortieth day, the girl not to her eightieth. He had buried them himself by the church wall, three small bundles in white cotton, with no name over them but the one their mother called them in the house. After the third, Ilfinesh had not laughed at him for a year. Then she had laughed again. Then she had died.

There would be no one to stand up in any church and say *son of Tamrat*. Tamrat son of Habta Maryam, son of — it stopped with him. It had stopped seventeen years ago at the church wall, and he had gone up to the altar every morning since and told himself that the altar was heir enough for any priest.

And there was the boy. He had written the boy into a widow's book in Rome, at the full stretch of his arm, *the boy Zoser, his scribe*, because the boy was his. That was no answer to give a wife on her knees.

He opened his mouth. Nothing came.

*Two nicks.*

"I was never in a hall," he said.

He said it to the floor between their knees. The stone was wet. It smelled of lime, and under the lime of old earth that had never once been in the sun.

"In Constantinople. You have heard me tell it. Everyone between Lalibela and the sea has heard me tell it. The finest minds of the Greeks, three days, and I found them all wanting." He drew a breath through his nose, and it caught. "I was thirty-four. I had letters from the Abuna under his seal. I went up through that city in my best cope, the red one with the gold border my mother's sister worked, and I had the Word made flesh in my mouth, and Cyril, all of Cyril, ready."

The boy, against the north wall, had not moved. Tamrat could see the long shape of him at the edge of the dark.

"They kept me at the—"

He groped for it. He had said *hall* for thirty years, and his tongue went to the word of itself; and the other word was there behind it and would not come forward.

"The bronze door," said the boy, out of the dark.

Tamrat turned his head.

The boy had not said it unkindly. He had said it as a deacon gives the next line to a priest who has lost his place, low, without lifting his eyes from the book. And Tamrat understood, kneeling there, that the boy had had it since the second Sunday of Lent: since the widow's house and the sermon, when his face changed at the back of the room. He had had it in the friary, behind a barred door with a guard on it day and night. He had had it in the Cardinal's hall. He had had it in this cell on Easter morning, when Tamrat told him he had chosen his fear for him. One word, and it would have stripped Tamrat of Lalibela in front of anyone; and the boy had kept it six weeks and never once taken it out.

It was the first thing Tamrat had ever been able to weigh in him. He weighed it. His right thumb had begun to go round in the hollow of his left palm, hard, and he let it go round.

"The bronze door," he said. "At the Patriarch's house. Three days on the step, in the hollow other men's knees had worn. I slept there. The porter's boy brought me water when he remembered, and I said *please* to him in Greek, and he laughed at the word. On the third day they let me in, and they did not take me to any hall. They took me into a room beside the door, a room for waiting, with a bench and a smell of lamp oil gone rancid, and two deacons turned out my satchel on the floor. They were looking for sorcery. Egyptian sorcery: that was their word for anything that came to them up the sea from Egypt. One of them gave me the kiss of peace, as the Gospel commands, and stepped back, and wiped his hand on his robe. And they laughed at my Greek. I had worked at it two years, by lamplight, with a Syrian monk who charged me a measure of barley a week. They laughed, and the one with the satchel said a word over it, and the other said it back to him, and—"

And he said it.

"*Goês.*"

It came out whole, in the deacon's voice, with the deacon's breath behind it: a light, bored, learned word, the word for a man in the market who does tricks with cups. He stopped with his mouth open.

He had not known he still had it. Thirty years he had not said it aloud; he had not let himself think it. It had lain in him all that while under the hall and the gold ceiling and the finest minds of the Greeks, and it had come up whole, with the deacon's breath still on it.

Across the cell the boy's outline had gone still. Not the stillness of a man listening; Tamrat knew that shape on a man, he had seen it on deacons when the Abuna's eye came down the row and found them. The boy had stopped breathing. He held so against the wall for a long moment. Then he drew his knees closer in and put both arms round them, tight.

Tamrat did not know why. He let it go. He had no room left for it.

"I never debated anyone," he said. "I never spoke to a Greek with a name. They gave me bread and a letter for the road and sent me home, and I walked back up the river saying the hall to myself, and by Aswan I had it by heart." He wiped his face with his palm. "And on the deck the captain's men turned out my bag again. My cross. My censer. The King's letter in the tar. Everyone on that deck was looking at me. The sailors. The captain. I could feel it on my skin." A breath. "Desta never once looked at me. On that deck. Not even when the candle did."

He had taken it for courtesy at the time, a soldier's courtesy to a priest on his knees. And the boy had not looked at him either. He had known that on the deck, kneeling in his own sick: the boy's eyes on the captain's candle the whole length of the Latin, and never once on the man he was saving. He had put that down to pride. Two men on that deck who would not look at him, and he could account for neither of them now.

The tallow smoked in the corner. The smell of it came across to him, mutton and soot, and under it the sourness of his own breath.

"You heard me tell it at Easter," he said. "What he said on the escarpment, with me across his shoulders. *You will not be my forty, old man.*" He heard Desta's dry voice come out of his own mouth a second time in a week, and was ashamed of the theft, and went on. "He kept his word. He went first. And the candle came on down the line anyway, past where he had been, to me."

*One nick.*

"It was at Aksum," he said.

He told it to her hands. They had come back to his, on her knees; he did not remember when.

"Before the rains. You remember. We went north out of our road, so that the King's embassy should pray at Zion before it went down to the sea. I asked to keep the night vigil in the church. A priest of Lalibela, sent by the King of Kings: they could not refuse me. They gave me the place before the last curtain, where the guardian sits."

The bile came up his throat again. He swallowed it down.

"He was an old man. He did not lift his head when I came to the curtain. He sat by the door of the sanctum with his lamp, and he had opened the inner chest that evening for the cloths, because they were to be carried before us at dawn. And in the second watch he rose." Tamrat's voice went down. "He rose, and turned his back on the sanctum, and walked away from it to the door. As if God had called him. I heard nothing. I heard the rain beginning a long way off on the hills. He stood at the door with his face to the dark, and he wept. There may have been words. None that I understood. And I thought: God has called him away. I thought: God has opened the door for me."

Her hands did not move.

"I went in."

He told it plainly. He had no other way of telling left. The lid of the inner chest standing back. The procession cloths folded over its rim, gold-bordered. Under them brown linen, very old, gone soft with age, laid open. And on the linen, apart, by itself, a sliver come away from the old wood: dark, no longer than the top joint of his finger, small, forgotten. Nobody had counted it.

"I put my hand on it, and it was warm." He swallowed. "Warm as a second heart. Acacia. The wood the Law commands for the Ark. I knew it under my fingers. I had handled the thorn-wood of the lowlands every year of my life, and it was that."

He had folded the cloths back over the linen in the dark, quickly, and not well. He had gone out past the old man weeping at the door, and the old man had not turned his head. Beyond the precinct wall his wife had been waiting with the mules, where women must wait; he had told her nothing. He had ridden down toward the sea with it shut in his fist under his shamma.

"Every village in our country has a tabot," he said. "Every church. Every altar at Lalibela has the Ark on it, consecrated, the Law of God; I have carried ours round Bet Medhani Alem at Timkat forty times with the umbrellas over it and the sistra going. It is the Ark. The bishop's hand makes it so. I knew that. I have preached it to the King." His voice cracked across. "I wanted the wood."

The candle shrank. The moat came in at the slit, rank and green.

"I swallowed it the first night on the road down, because there was nowhere on me that a hand could not find. I thought it would kill me. It did not. It passed." He shut his eyes. "And I washed it, and I swallowed it again. At Massawa. At Suakin. At Aswan. In Cairo. In Alexandria, the night before the ship. Over and over. Every time it passed through me I washed it and put it back, so that there would be something holy inside me where no hand could come. Not a Greek deacon's. Not a sailor's. Not the Pope's own." He opened his eyes on nothing. "Untouchable. That is what I wanted. That is what the word means. A place no searcher can reach."

He had said it. He heard the last of it go out of him and fall on the stone between them.

Then, flatly, because it was true and he had nothing left to keep it in:

"And every night it turned in me toward him."

His wife did not look at the boy. Her outline stayed toward Tamrat, her head a little bent, and did not turn.

At the north wall the boy made no sound at all.

Tamrat knelt there, emptied. He was so hungry that his hands had stopped shaking, for want of anything to shake with.

"Give me—" he said, and stopped.

He had not said those two words to a living soul since the bronze door, where he had said them in Greek to a porter's boy for three days. He held his hands out to her, open, and said them again.

"Give me gursha. Angeska. If there is anything."

She did not get up. She put her hand to the knot of her shawl at her girdle, where she had kept something since Easter, and worked the knot loose. It was the last of the Fasika bread, a heel gone hard, wrapped in a corner of the cloth with three cloves. She broke a crumb from it no bigger than a grain of kolo and held it up to him between her finger and thumb.

He opened his mouth.

Her fingertips touched his lower lip. They were cold, and rough at the ends from the needle and the bundle-cords, and they smelled of cloves and tallow and the iron of the grille. She laid the crumb on his tongue and left her fingers there a moment against his lip, not pressing. Then she took them away.

He held the crumb on his tongue. Rye gone stale; the grit of the mill; the cloves through all of it, sharp and sweet, so that his eyes ran. He did not chew. He let it soften there until there was nothing left of it to hold, and swallowed, and it went down into him past the other thing.

*The stub.*

It had to come out.

He knelt in the near-dark with his wife before him, the wet stone under his knees, and knew it. There were two roads, and both were bad, and he made himself read them one at a time: the first lesson, and then the second.

He could keep it. Take it with him in the morning, through the castle gate and out onto the bridge, inside him where it had ridden for a year. They would strip him at the stake, and he would stand before Rome with it in him, and Rome would not know. Then the fire. And when the fire was done, they would come with their sieves. Rome's own Bishop had gone over the mountains to Avignon because Rome had nothing left to hold him. Let such a city find, in an Ethiopian heretic's ash, a little dark wood that was still warm, that still beat. They would carry it to the Pope in gold. He would come home to kneel to it. They would build a church over it, and every tabot in Tamrat's country would become a copy of a thing in a Latin box.

Or he could give it to the boy.

The boy would not burn. Tamrat had heard the notary's Latin at the grille on the Wednesday: his own name in it, and his wife's, and no third. The boy would walk out of this castle. And on the deck—he made himself see the deck. The captain coming down the line with his candle of church wax, the sweet smell of it over the tar. Past the wife. Past Desta. Past the boy, kneeling at his shoulder with his scrip against his thigh. The flame had gone by the boy without a tremor, and come on, and found *him*.

The light had never once turned to the boy. Not on the deck, not at the widow's, not in this cell, three paces from it for eighteen days. The boy could carry it through every search between here and the end of the world, and no candle would know him.

And against that: what he had said in this same hour, on his knees, to his wife. Every night it turned in me toward him.

He knew both. He held them both, and weighed them, and they did not balance; and he chose.

The verse came to him then in Ge'ez, in his own voice from the pulpit of Bet Medhani Alem on the night of the Easter vigil, as he had read it there forty years by lamplight with the people standing in the dark:

*And Abraham took the wood of the burnt offering, and laid it upon Isaac his son.*

The wood. Forty years he had read it, and never once heard what the word was.

The rest of the verse he knew too. He left it where it was.

On the ledge the candle was a stub now, a pool of grey tallow with a black wick standing up in it, and the cell had gone to shapes. His wife before him. The boy against the far wall. And the smell of all of it: hot tallow, the bucket, the moat, damp lime, his own breath gone sour from a year of what he kept in his belly.

For a year he had never once brought it up by the mouth. He had kept his throat shut on it through the worst of the sea, through the Capua bridge with a corporal's hands on his ribs, through the Cardinal's hall with its eight candles. Every night that it rose in him he had swallowed it back down.

He let go of his wife's hands. Her outline drew back from him, and rose, and stood.

He put two fingers into his mouth, to the back of his throat, where the gursha had gone down, and pressed.

His body fought him. It had been taught for a year to keep this, and it kept it: it heaved, and shut, and heaved. The bile came first, hot and thin and bitter, over his fingers and into his beard and onto the stone in front of his knees, and the stink of it filled the cell. Somewhere at the edge of the dark the boy had come off his wall; Tamrat heard his knees on the stone. He pressed again, harder, and held his fingers there. Something moved in him low on the right, under the cramp, and turned over, and began to come up, slowly, against everything the year had taught his throat, until it filled his mouth.

He bent forward and opened his hand under his lips.

It came into his palm small and dark, drenched and slick with what had come up with it: a splinter of acacia no longer than the top joint of a finger. It lay in the hollow of his left hand, where his thumb had rubbed for six weeks.

And it beat.

He knew the beat. He had lain on his back with his hands on his belly every night for a year and told himself it did not beat; and it never once had, apart from him. He did not need to count it now. It was his own heart, doubled, beating in his open hand.
