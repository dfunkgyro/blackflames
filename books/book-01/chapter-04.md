# CHAPTER 4

## ABBA TALKA

*Lalibela — Timkat, January 1326*

The emperor’s officer came down the trench to Bet Maryam in the last hour before the tabot was to be carried out, and Abba Talka did not believe that was an accident.

He heard the man before he saw him. Iron-shod hooves rang on the cut steps where no one rode, a voice rose over the kebero drums at the trench-head, and the small deacons waiting there with their sistrums scattered like hens before a dog. Then a mule’s head came round the bend of the red wall, and on the mule a big man in a lion-skin cape with a spear across his saddle and two spearmen trotting at his stirrups. He brought his own air with him: mule sweat, the smoke of a night camp, rancid butter in his braids, and the dust of four days’ road.

Talka stood in the doorway of the Mother’s house with the brocade folded over his arm and did not go to meet him. The court lay in blue shadow, and the frost that had whitened the tuff at dawn would be back before the stars. He had until the drums changed their beat. Then the Mother’s tabot must come out of that door on a priest’s head and go down to the water with the tabots of every other house in Lalibela, and nothing on earth could stand in its road. Before the drums changed, then, Talka must send him away content, or put him behind the tabot content, which was harder.

And he must do it without telling the man anything that was true.

“Abba.” The officer swung down. He came across the court with the mule’s reins still in his fist, and knelt on the frozen stone, and kissed the hand-cross Talka held out to him. That was correct. Tasfa Mikael commanded a thousand of the King’s spears, and had once been a boy in this court, and he had never been anything but correct. He rose. “The King of Kings, Amda Seyon, asks after his letter.”

“The King’s letter is with Qess Tamrat,” Talka said, “and Qess Tamrat is with God, and between them they will carry it where it is going.”

“Where is it?”

“Farther than either of us will ever stand.”

Tasfa Mikael looked at him the way a soldier looks at a ford that the guide swears is shallow. “Before the Nativity,” he said, “Ifat’s horsemen came up out of the low country into the eastern marches. Three nights’ ride from the King’s tent. They burned a church in a village whose name you would not know. They took the tabot out of it first, and broke it on the steps, and they took the women and the cattle and left the men in the doorway. The King means to march when the new grass comes. He wants to know whether the Franks will be in the Red Sea by then, or whether he fights alone.”

“The Franks,” Talka said.

“Your Franks. They sat in this house. They stood in the King’s tent and said that when Rome moved, Ifat would find a sword at its back and a sword at its throat.” The officer’s hand opened and closed on the reins. “I heard them say it. The tall one, with the cough. He said Rome held the keys.”

*Rome alone holds the keys.*

Talka remembered the tall one at the long table in the refectory two years ago: young as a deacon, with a sun-peeled nose, the injera going cold before him because he would not eat with his hands; the wet, low cough; the key the man had shaped in the air with two thin fingers and turned, as if he were opening a door in the smoke.

“Rome alone holds the keys, Father,” the Frank had said, through the one of his brothers who had some Arabic, and through Tamrat’s young wife, who had more Arabic than the friar, and a few slow words of the Franks’ own tongue to mend it with. “Of heaven. And of the earth, too, if your King will kneel with us.”

“We kneel,” Talka had said. “We kneel a great deal. Ask our knees.”

The young wife had rendered that, and the Frank had smiled the smile of a man humouring a child. The second Frank, the short one with the burned red neck who spoke least and listened most, had not smiled at all. He had leaned across the table, past the bread, and asked a question of his own.

“In the north they told us that your monks burn certain women. Is it so?”

Talka had felt the whole table go still around him, the brothers’ hands stopped over the dishes.

“We bury our dead,” he had said. “Facing east.”

“Not the dead, Father,” the red-necked one had said, gently, through the girl. “The women.”

He had not answered. The cough had taken the tall one then, and in the business of water and cloths the question had been let drop. But at Timkat that year the red-necked Frank had come down to the water with everyone else, and had not watched the tabots. He had stood at the edge of the women and watched the grandmothers with their lamps. He had watched them the whole of the morning, with a great deal of interest, and someone had explained to him with their hands what the lamps were for, since the Frank had no Amharic. Talka had never learned who.

Two of the six had gone before him, to the fever and the bad water at the fords. The tall one died in the month of Megabit, the third, coughing, with the orphan sitting by his mat to learn their prayers off him. The red-necked one had gone north with the two who were left. Talka had not been sorry to see him go.

“Abba.”

The drums at the trench-head had not changed. Not yet.

“The new grass is four months off,” Talka said. “Rome is farther than the new grass. What does the King want of an old priest at Timkat, Tasfa Mikael? Shall I ride out to the marches and wave the Franks in with my fly-whisk?”

“He wants to know what you know.”

“I know that the Mother’s tabot is coming out of that door, and that you are standing in its road with a mule.”

That moved him. A spearman ran up and took the reins, and Tasfa Mikael stepped back off the threshold. But he did not go. He planted himself at the edge of the court and said to Talka’s back, “The Mansa of Mali went through Cairo two years since with gold enough to sink the price of it. Every Muslim between here and the sea feels rich this year, and brave. The King asks what he bought with his letter, Abba. He asks what he bought with your scribe.”

Talka went in.

The cold inside the Mother’s house was older than the court’s, closer, with damp in it. The lamps in their niches made small yellow islands, and the pillars stood between them like men waiting to be called on, and over everything lay the smell.

Frankincense first. It was always first. Then wet tuff, which in this house never dried, even in the dry months. Then beeswax and old tallow and the sweat of priests in heavy vestments. All of that was the church, and he had breathed it every day of his life.

And under all of it, on feast nights, he smelled rancid oil and burning juniper.

There was no such smell in the house. Fifteen years of censers had gone up into that roof since, and the brothers who stood where she had lain smelled nothing but holiness. But on feast nights, when the incense thickened and the lamps burned close, the other smell rose to meet him between the two nearest pillars, faint as a thing remembered in sleep, and his right hand closed on itself. It remembered the horn handle. It remembered turning the knife in his fingers to hold it out hilt first, as one hands a knife to a boy, so the boy would not cut himself. He had still cut bread with that knife the next morning. He had cut it with that knife for fifteen years, and every morning he cut the heel off first and laid it on the stone by the gate; the novices said it was for the birds.

He did not look up.

He knew without looking what was there. The painters had come the year after and laid ochre and red and white over the whole roof, new crosses, new beasts. The grey had come up through it before the season was out. Grey, the way old writing shows through a scraped page held to the light: rows of it between the painted knots, faint as veins under an old man’s skin. Anyone who stood where he stood and tipped his head back would see it. People look up in a church only when they are told to, and he had made sure for fifteen years that no one was told.

He went to the curtain with his eyes on the floor.

The priests of the house were waiting there in their feast copes, gold thread gone brown at the seams, and the bearer who would carry the tabot had already put the cushion on his head. Behind them, by the north wall, Gebre swung the censer.

He swung it well, with that small turn of the wrist at the top of the arc that sent the smoke out even. He had not looked at Talka in fifteen years, and he did not look at him now. His eyes rested on a place on the floor two paces to the left of Talka’s feet, and the censer went out and back on its chains with a small dry clink of brass.

Talka went behind the curtain alone, as was his right, and said the words over the tabot, and lifted it.

It was not heavy. A slab of wood, written on and consecrated by a bishop’s hand long before Talka was born, wrapped in crimson and gold. It weighed less than a lamb. He set it on the bearer’s head, on the cushion, and drew the brocade down over it and over the man’s face, so that the man walked blind under God, and the deacons outside took up the canopy on its poles.

The drums at the trench-head changed their beat.

They went down to the water as Lalibela had always gone down, slowly, singing, the light leaving the sky behind them.

The tabots came out of every house: the Saviour’s under green velvet sewn with silver bells, the Cross’s, Golgotha’s, the Mother’s in crimson and gold, rocking on the bearer’s head above the crowd. Over each one the processional umbrellas wheeled slowly on their staves, red and purple and gold, so that the whole road seemed to turn as it moved. The sistrums shook their iron discs in a dry rain of sound, the kebero spoke under it, and the chant went up in quarter-tones, a long cry that broke and climbed again. The deacons danced backward before the tabots with their prayer-staffs held out level, and the crowd in its white shammas pressed in on both sides with its children held up to see.

Talka walked behind the Mother’s tabot with his hand-cross raised and sang, and watched the weather. The sky was clear and hard and green at the edges. It would freeze. The wind came down off the ridge and smelled of dung fires and tella brewing in the houses. The crowd cupped its candles against it, and he watched the flames anyway. He found that he watched every flame he passed, the way a man who has been bitten by a dog watches every dog.

At the water the tents were up already, white cotton on poles, one for each house, and the priests took the tabots into them for the night and laid them on their stands, and the vigil began.

Talka had kept it more than fifty times, and toward midnight the cold came up out of the ground and seized his left knee, as it always did.

He sat by the fire outside the Mother’s tent on a folded hide, and a boy brought him water.

“Abba. You should drink. Your lips are cracked.”

Giyorgis. He had come back before the feast of the Cross with a trader’s mule, a letter inside his shirt, and his feet in rags, and he had grown since spring. His voice had not decided yet which way it would go, and it went both ways in a single sentence. He knelt and held out the cup, and when Talka had drunk he did not go.

“Did you see it, Abba? Ever? The sea?”

“No.”

“It doesn’t stop.” The boy said it as if it were a sin someone had confessed to him. “At Massawa. It comes up over the sand and goes back, and comes up, and goes back, like a thing breathing in its sleep. Desta made us count. He’d never seen it either.” Giyorgis grinned, and then did not. “He got to thirty-nine and stopped and said the sea had more than he could count, and he wouldn’t give it the satisfaction.”

Talka laughed, because the boy wanted him to, and it hurt his chest in the cold.

“Did the boy count?”

“Zoser? He wrote them.” Giyorgis frowned. “He wrote something in the sand with a stick. He wouldn’t let me see. The sea came up over it.”

Behind the boy Gebre came round the end of the tent with a jar of tella on his hip and a horn cup, and stopped, and came on.

Gebre knelt and poured. The tella was thin and sour and smelled of barley and smoke, and he held out the cup to Talka with his left hand. His right hand stayed in his sleeve. Fifteen years of the right hand in the sleeve, so that the brothers said Gebre had been touched by God in the arm the same night he was touched in the tongue, and was being humble about it.

Talka took the cup with one hand. With the other he took Gebre’s right wrist.

He did not pull. He never had to. He laid his fingers on the inside of the wrist as a physician lays them, at the place where the blood knocks, and Gebre’s whole arm went still, and then let itself be drawn out of the sleeve and turned over in the firelight.

The fire had burned down to coals, and the light came up red off them onto the palm. A line ran across it, from the root of the first finger toward the heel, black and fine, crossing the creases as if the creases were not there. Her leaning, branching letters. The same as at the feast of Mikael in Hidar. The same as the night he had first seen it, in the court, with the bell still going. He could no more read it now than then. He counted the signs, as he always did. They were all there.

And beside them, past the last of them, at the very edge where the palm turned toward the wrist, was a new mark.

It was not one of her letters, or not one he had seen. It lay along the skin in a low curve that rose and fell and rose, like the line a child draws for water.

At Mikael’s feast in Hidar it had not been there. He put his thumb to it and rubbed, gently, and it did not smear. Gebre’s pulse jumped under his fingers and settled and went on, and Gebre looked at the place on the ground two paces to the left of Talka’s knee.

“*It doesn’t stop*,” Giyorgis had said. *It comes up over the sand and goes back.*

Talka let the wrist go. Gebre drew the hand back into the sleeve, and picked up the tella jar, and went away round the end of the tent without a sound, as he went everywhere.

“Abba?” said Giyorgis.

“Go and sleep an hour. You’ll carry the water at dawn.”

The boy went. Talka sat by the coals with the horn cup cooling in his hand and did not drink from it.

The old brothers, when he was young, had spoken of the curse of those who carry in the voice men keep for the names of fevers, and never wrote the words down. He knew that the thing on Gebre’s palm had come from a woman who carried, and that it went on writing, and that it had just written something new. He did not know what, and he sat with that in the cold.

*I did not choose lightly.*

He had said it to the boy in the library in the spring, with his hand on the boy’s shoulder and the boy’s fingers trembling on a page of Coptic. He had meant it.

It had begun at his own table, on one of the nights the Franks were in the house, after they had gone to their beds. Tamrat’s young wife had stayed to clear the cups, which she need not have done. She had stood at his elbow with the jar and filled his cup, and said, as if the thought had only then come to her, that her husband would need a hand to copy for him, if the King sent anyone at all. That Tamrat’s eyes were not what they had been. That the orphan with the good hand was wasted on psalters.

“The orphan,” he had said.

“The one who writes the Coptic. The one who came up out of the ashes.” She had set the jar down. “Forgive me, Abba. It is not my place.”

*Came up out of the ashes.* That was the brothers’ own way of naming the boy, said in the chapter-house and at the night office, not at a market well. He had marked it, and then set it down: she was Tamrat’s wife, and would have it from Tamrat’s mouth. He weighed the suggestion a month, and chose.

He had his own reasons, and he did not take them out by the fire.

He thought instead, because it was easier, of Tamrat’s wife: her seven tongues, her stillness at the edge of the lamplight, the clear way she had of saying a hard thing so that it seemed to have been said by someone kinder. He had long thought that no bishop had blessed that marriage, and that Tamrat, a priest forty years and a widower, could not have found one who would. He had said nothing. Tamrat was useful, and the girl had Arabic, and had sat very still at that table when the Franks talked among themselves.

The chant from the tents rose into its midnight watch. The drummers had found their second wind.

And at the gate, on the morning they left—

It had been cold that morning too, though not like this; the cold of the end of the dry season, with the dust lying still and smelling of mule dung and the kolo the women had parched for the road. The boy had been at the head of the line with his scrip slung across him, riding low on the hip, as he had worn it since he was seven years old. Tamrat had been making a speech to the brothers at the gate. His wife had been listening to it with her eyes down.

Talka had walked down the line to where Desta was tightening a girth, and stood by the mule’s shoulder, and said it low, under Tamrat’s speech.

“Watch the boy’s bag. Not the boy.”

Desta had not stopped pulling on the strap. He had finished the girth and tested it and slapped the mule’s belly, and only then looked up, with the dry, patient look he wore when an officer told him to do a thing and he was deciding which part of it to obey.

“The boy’s bag,” he had said.

“Wherever he goes. Whatever happens to him.”

“Abba, I have watched the King’s baggage for twenty years. Bags don’t wander off to read books; that’s why a man likes them.” He had wiped his hands on his thighs, and looked down the line at the boy, and back. “Tell me why.”

Talka had opened his mouth.

The tella had gone cold in the horn cup. He set it on the ground beside the fire and pulled his shamma up over his head against the frost.

⁂

The frost came, as he had known it would. By the last watch it lay white on the red tuff of the bank, and the breath of the crowd hung over the water in a long low cloud, and the stars went out one by one into a sky the colour of a dove. The air smelled of dead fires and wet wool and the last of the night’s incense, gone stale in the cold.

At first light the priests came out of the tents with their hand-crosses and their candles, and went down to the water.

They had dammed the stream they called Jordan below the fall, as they did every year, so that it spread into a broad pool among the rocks, and the pool lay grey and still under a skin of mist. The priests stood at its edge and sang over it. They read the Gospel of the Lord’s baptism, and then the eldest of them stooped and drew his hand-cross through the water, once, and again, and a third time, and touched his candle’s flame to the surface so that it hissed and went out, and the water was holy.

The crowd broke.

A roar went up from both banks at once. The young men threw off their shammas and went into the pool, shouting at the cold, and the priests scooped the water in jars and threw it out over the people, who pushed forward with their faces lifted to take it. The sistrums went mad. Water flew in the first sun and caught it, and the whole bank steamed.

Tasfa Mikael found him there.

He had spent the night by his own fire ten paces off, near enough that Talka had felt him all night like a draught. Now he came through the press with his lion-skin dark with spray, and Talka turned at the moment he arrived, so that they stood face to face at the water’s edge.

“Abba. I ride today. What do I carry to the King?”

“Carry him this,” Talka said.

He lifted his hand-cross and drew it through the holy water and laid it, streaming, against the officer’s forehead, and held it there.

“The embassy is in Rome.”

He felt the man go still under the cross.

“A pilgrim came up from Jerusalem by the Suakin road before the Nativity,” Talka said, with the water running down between the man’s eyes. “He had it from a Genoese at Alexandria. The black priests of the King of Kings have come to Rome and been received there with honour, and Qess Tamrat has put the King’s letter into the hands that were meant for it. That is the news. The rest is Rome’s to answer, and Rome is slow, and the new grass is four months off.”

Tasfa Mikael’s eyes searched his face.

“Why did you not send to the camp?”

“I am sending,” Talka said. “By you.”

Round them a woman shrieked with laughter as the water hit her, and Tasfa Mikael bowed his head under the cross and let the water run down into his beard.

“God keep them,” he said.

“God keep them,” said Talka.

The man straightened. Something had gone out of his shoulders. He looked about at the water as if he were seeing it for the first time, and wiped his face, and said, in a different voice, “Have you eaten, Abba?”

⁂

They ate on the bank, on cloths spread over the frost-melt, among the families.

There was injera, sour and spongy, cold from the night. There was shiro in a clay bowl, thick with fenugreek, the steam coming off it. There was dabo, the round feast bread, scored with a cross across the top and warm still from the ashes where it had been buried. There was tella, and for the officer a horn of tej, gold and smoky, which he drank too fast and then pushed at Talka until Talka took a mouthful to quiet him. It went down warm. The smell of the shiro and the dung fires and a thousand shammas drying in the sun hung over the bank.

The children came to him, as they always did at Timkat, pushed forward by their mothers, and stood before him with their wet faces turned up, and he dipped his fingers in the jar Giyorgis held for him and touched each forehead and said the blessing over each. One small girl with a cough would not stand still for the water and squirmed and laughed, and he caught her by the ear, gently, and blessed her anyway, and she ran off shrieking. Giyorgis, holding the jar, was grinning so hard that he spilled it.

Tasfa Mikael sat on the cloth with his cape off and watched, and ate, and was quiet, until a mother called her boy back to wipe his nose and the line stood still a moment.

“The red-necked Frank came to the camp after he left you,” he said then, wiping his fingers on the injera. “He asked the King’s chaplains whether it was true that the monks of Lalibela burned a woman once, in a church. The chaplains laughed at him.”

He watched the children at the water, not Talka. “He did not laugh. He wrote it down.”

The shiro smell was thick and good, and under it, for the space of a breath, Talka smelled juniper.

“Then the chaplains answered him,” he said.

“Did they answer him true, Abba?”

“Tasfa Mikael.” Talka reached across and took a piece of the dabo, and broke it. “When you were nine you stole oil from the Mother’s lamps to grease your sling, and I never told the abbot. Shall we talk about what is true?”

The officer stared at him, and then laughed, a great bark that turned heads along the bank, and let it go. The boy came back with his nose wiped, and Talka blessed him.

Farther along the bank the hill families had their own fires. Talka watched one of the grandmothers now, an old woman wrapped to the eyes in a shamma gone grey with washing, kneel by her fire with a bundle in her arms and a small clay oil lamp on the ground before her.

It was a hill custom, not a valley one. The women of those families did it at the lying-in, the first night: the grandmother held a lamp to the new child, close, and watched the flame. If it leaned, they said, *the child is claimed*, and the family wrapped it up and carried it down to the priests to be blessed. The families who had been late with a lying-in did it again at Timkat, by the water, where the priests were near. He did not ask the women what they believed the flame saw. They did not ask him what he believed.

The grandmother took up the lamp and held it to the bundle.

Talka found that he had stopped with his fingers wet over a child’s head. He did not look away. The small yellow flame stood on its wick in the still air, a finger’s breadth from the baby’s face, and the baby blinked at it, and the grandmother held it there.

It did not lean.

It stood straight up, as a flame should, and burned, and was only a flame.

Talka let out his breath. He had not known he was holding it until it went out of him, long and slow, and smoked in the cold. His shoulders came down. He put his wet fingers on the waiting child’s forehead and said the blessing, and said it badly, and had to begin again.

The grandmother had seen him. She lowered the lamp and laughed at him across the frost, showing three teeth.

“Did you think it would lean, Abba? This one?” She hoisted the bundle. “She is as plain as bread.”

“Bring her to me,” he said. “I’ll bless her anyway.”

When the sun was up and the frost gone off the rocks, the tabots began their slow way home.

He stood on the bank with Tasfa Mikael and watched them go back up the road under their turning umbrellas: the Saviour’s under green and silver, the Mother’s under crimson, and after them the small tabots of the hill villages, each going back to a church of mud and timber that would hold forty people standing.

The last was the smallest, from a ridge village whose name Talka had forgotten, under an umbrella so faded that its red had gone the pink of a healed scar, carried by a priest who could not have been twenty. The brocade had slipped. Talka could see the sweat standing on the young man’s neck in the cold, and the way he held his head, rigid, as if the wood on it weighed what a man weighs.

The Franks had not understood that. They believed the Ark lay under one altar, in their church of the Lateran at Rome, and that Rome held the keys to it; they had looked at these slabs of wood under their umbrellas and seen copies. His people believed otherwise. The Ark slept at Aksum in the sanctum of Zion, under its guardian, as it had since Menelik brought it south; and it was also here, on that boy’s head, and in the Mother’s house, and in every mud church on every ridge where a bishop had laid hands on the wood and it had been written and wrapped and sung over. It had been given out, as bread is given out at a table. No thief could steal it, because a thief who came at the chest in Aksum would still have to come at every altar in the land.

He thought that, watching the pink umbrella sway up the road, and for a moment he felt the whole of it in his chest, warm, like the tej.

Then his eyes went, without his leave, to the end of the bank, where Gebre knelt among the jars with his right hand in his sleeve; and his own hand, hanging at his side, closed on nothing.

“Your scribe,” Tasfa Mikael said beside him, mildly, with a full belly. “The orphan. He’ll make something of himself, among the Franks.”

“He will,” Talka said.

The road behind the last tabot was emptying. The drums went up the hill. Giyorgis was carrying the water jars back to the Mother’s tent, two on a yoke, the way he had carried them to Massawa, and singing something under his breath that Talka did not know.

A horse was coming down the road against the crowd.

A poor horse, its head low and its legs trembling, and the rider on it mud to the thighs: the grey-brown mud of the northern crossings, as if he had come through the Tekeze at the ford and not stopped. The crowd parted for him with a mutter. He came down off the horse at the water’s edge, and his knees gave, and he caught himself on the saddle.

He wore a priest’s cross and a priest’s turban gone brown with dust, and his face was grey under it. He smelled of horse and fear-sweat, and of the incense of a church that was not Lalibela’s, sharper and older, still clinging to his clothes.

“Abba Talka?” he said. “They told me at the Mother’s house. The priests of Zion send to every house that keeps a tabot.”

Talka went to him. Behind him he heard the officer get up off the cloth.

“Sit,” Talka said. “Giyorgis, water.”

“No.” The rider had his hand on Talka’s sleeve. His fingers were shaking. “Twelve days before Timkat the guardian of the sanctum must open the inner chest, Abba, for the cloths of the procession. Before witnesses. It is the rule. I have been on the road since that morning. This year he could not find the lock.” He swallowed. “He could not find it with his hands. They held a lamp to his face and his eyes did not follow it. The guardian of the sanctum of St Mary of Zion is blind, Abba, and the inner chest has been disturbed, and he has been hiding it since before the rains.”
