# CHAPTER 7

## TAMRAT

*Rome, by the Tiber — the second Sunday of Lent, 1326*

The guide wanted another coin at the bridge.

He stood in the roadway with his palm up, a small man in a hood of greasy felt, and under him the river went by brown and stinking, carrying a dead dog with its legs raised to God. Angeska put the coin into the palm without a word.

Tamrat listened to the purse as she drew it closed. A week ago it had chinked. Now it ticked.

“He says it is the angel’s toll,” she said in Amharic. “There is no angel’s toll. He says it anyway.”

“Then tell him the Ethiopians pay the angels directly.”

She did not tell him. The guide smiled with three teeth and walked on.

It was the second Thursday of the Latins’ Lent, and Rome smelled of salt fish: stockfish stacked in doorways, eels stiff in brine-tubs, and over all of it the river and the tallow of a city that could not afford wax. Under Tamrat’s arm, in a cloth, was a loaf bought at a price that had made the baker look away while he took it. It weighed what a stone weighs. It smelled of rye and ash, and his mouth ran with water, and he swallowed and walked.

Low under his ribs, on the right, the old fist lay half closed and waited.

He was going to the church of Peter. If the Bishop of Rome sat in this place called Avignon, Peter did not; Peter lay here under his own altar, and Peter’s canons would know what it meant that the King of Kings had sent his priest across the width of the world.

The canons were not there. The church stood up out of a field of mud and broken marble, its forecourt empty but for beggars and pigeons and a man selling little lead keys on strings. The guide went in at a side door, came out, and talked with his hands.

“They have gone to the station,” she said. “To another church across the city, for the Lenten Mass. The porter says, if the Ethiopians wish to see the canons, the Ethiopians may write.”

“Write.”

“He says that is how it is done here.”

Tamrat stood on the steps with sleet in his beard and looked at the shut door. His hand had gone of its own accord to the place under his sash. He took it away.

“The guide says there is a wonder round the side,” said Angeska. “The tomb of Julius Caesar. For one more coin.”

He went, because the beggars on the steps were watching him stand before a shut door.

⁂

It stood on the south flank of the church, past a small round chapel, in a yard of frozen ruts: one stone needle, square and tapering, taller than any tree in Lasta, without a joint from its foot to its point. On the point, black against the sleet, sat a ball of bronze. The yard smelled of wet stone and the guide’s garlic.

The guide planted himself before it and chanted. It had the cadence of a thing said ten thousand times for money, the way the lesser deacons at home gabble the genealogies.

Angeska rendered it flat. “This is the Needle of Caesar. In the apple of bronze at its top his ashes lie, so that as the whole world lay under him living, so it lies under him dead for ever.”

Tamrat looked up.

His eyes could no longer read a psalter in his lap, but at a distance they were as good as ever. He knew that red speckled granite from the quarries above the Cataract. The Copt on the salt wagon had talked all the road from Cassino of the stones up the river past Akhmim, covered with birds and eyes and little men walking, that no one living could read. Tamrat looked for the birds. He looked for the eyes.

There was nothing. From foot to ball the faces of the Needle were smooth, and the sleet ran down them in clean lines. The Romans had carried the Egyptians’ stone across the sea and set a pagan’s ashes on it for a relic, and the stone said nothing about itself.

At Aksum the great stones stood in the grass with doors carved on them, storey above storey, doors that did not open, and at dusk their shadows lay out across the grass toward the church, and between them an old man’s lamp—

He put that down. He put it down hard.

*They have not come for ours.*

The guide added something quick, with a jerk of his chin to the north and west, and in it Tamrat caught the one word of the Latins he had learned in Rome, from a guard with a ring of keys: *Avignon*. Angeska did not render it. She had already looked that way, once, over the roofs toward the mountains, before the guide’s chin moved.

Then she held out another coin, and did not look that way again.

⁂

The innkeeper by the Jews’ bridge, who had taken their silver for eight nights, wanted the ninth in advance. Angeska turned the purse out on his board. He laughed, not unkindly, and pointed to the door.

That night a cheaper house behind the fish-market took the last of the silver for straw under its stair. The reek of eels came up through the straw till morning, and the boy slept with his hand over the pouch at his breast.

On the Friday they went out with the coppers.

The first inn stood under a ruin of old brick, its back wall built into the arches. Tamrat went in ahead of his wife, because he was the priest. It was a low room stinking of wine-lees and a goat stabled under the stair, and he stopped inside the door before he greeted anyone, and counted. One lamp on the board, a clay dish of tallow, smoking. One in the niche by the stair, before a saint. A fire under a pot.

He took the bench by the door. From there the board’s lamp was more than the length of his arm away, the niche further, the hearth across the room. He sat. Then he lifted his hand and blessed the house.

He did not ask himself why he had counted first.

The innkeeper, a man with a wen beside his nose, looked at their faces and his lip went down; he looked at the coppers Angeska held up, and his lip came up again. He held out his hand.

A man came in out of the sleet behind them.

Tamrat saw his clothes before his face. The man wore a short coat of good wool slashed from shoulder to hip in bands of silver and red, and at the shoulder a rose was stitched, red on white. He did not look at the Ethiopians. He put his mouth by the innkeeper’s wen and said something short.

The innkeeper’s hand closed on nothing.

“He has no room,” Angeska said. “He did not know it until now.”

The silver and red was already going off along the lane, unhurried.

At the second inn, under a hill crowned with towers, it was a window with a shutter propped above it. The woman inside had the coppers in her palm when the silver and red came round the end of the lane: another servant or the same, the same coat. She gave them back so quickly she dropped one, and Angeska stooped for it in the mud, and the shutter came down with a slam of wet wood that threw drops on Tamrat’s face.

He stood in the lane with the drops running into his beard.

“It is our faces,” he said. “Every hand in this city goes to its purse when it sees a face like mine.”

Angeska put the copper away. “Yes,” she said.

He had heard it all the way from Naples: *Saraceni*, *heretici*. He did not need a servant in a coat to tell him what Rome thought of his skin. But he remembered the coat.

⁂

They lay that night in the porch of a little church by the river, under three arches of old brick, with the rain coming in under the middle one.

A lamp burned in a niche above the door before a saint whose face the damp had eaten. Tamrat measured it from the step and lay down at the west end of the porch, where it was further from him than he was tall. When the wind came off the river the little flame shrank down into its oil and smoked, rancid, and when the wind let go it stood up again, small and yellow.

The boy lay at his left hand against the west wall, between the old man and the weather: Desta’s place, now. Angeska lay at Tamrat’s feet with her shawl over her face. Something swam in the river, heavily.

Tamrat lay on his back with his hands flat on his belly.

The warmth was there. It had been there since the sea, and he did not think about it: a small steady heat low under his ribs, below the fist. It did not beat. On the cold nights in the stables of Italy he had been glad of it, and had not asked himself what kind of gladness that was.

Tonight it moved.

It went the breadth of a finger, slowly, toward his left side, toward the place where the boy lay breathing. It went as a sleeper turns toward a fire, without waking. And there it stayed.

He lay very still. Then he took his mat across the porch past Angeska’s feet and lay down against the east wall, the whole length of the porch from the boy. The niche lamp was nearer here. He measured it. It was still out of reach.

“Qess?” said the boy, out of the dark.

“The rain comes in there.”

The boy said nothing more.

Tamrat said the Psalms of the night under his breath. Before he came to the ninth the warmth had begun to turn in him again, back across his belly, slow, patient, until it lay against his right side, toward the far end of the porch, where the boy slept.

He did not move again. He lay till the bells with his hands one over the other on his belly, and he did not give the thing a name.

⁂

The boy was eating.

It was the Saturday, grey and late, and they had been shut out of every door along the river since dawn. Angeska had broken the stone loaf into four. One quarter she had wrapped in a cloth for her husband, who did not eat before others, and he had carried it all day in the breast of his shamma and not eaten it.

He had laid it on the church step while he said the ninth-hour prayer with his face to the wall. When he turned, the cloth lay open, the heel of the bread was gone, and the boy sat on the step below, chewing, his eyes on the river and one hand closed at his breast over the little pouch of Desta’s grain.

All night the thing in Tamrat had turned toward that boy. All night.

He did not remember crossing the step. He remembered his open hand coming down across the side of the boy’s head with all the weight left in his arm, and the flat wet sound of it, and the crust jumping out of the boy’s mouth onto the stones. Then the strap of his satchel was in his fist. It came down across the boy’s back, once, twice, and the boy did not lift his hands. He bent forward over his knees with both hands over the pouch, and took it on his neck and shoulders, and made no sound at all.

“Thief,” Tamrat said, in a voice gone high. “Thief of a priest’s bread. In the fast. In the fast—”

“Tamrat.”

Angeska did not raise her voice. A woman had stopped in the lane to watch, and a man in a tavern door with a cup.

The strap hung from his hand. His palm stung and smelled of greased leather and the boy’s hair.

The boy sat up slowly, a weal rising along his neck. He picked the crust up off the stones, wiped it on his shamma, wrapped it back into the cloth with the rest, and laid the cloth where it had been. Then he put his hand back over the pouch.

A whole pouch of grain at his breast, carried from the plank. He would steal a heel of rye from a sick old man rather than open it.

Tamrat went along the river wall until a buttress stood between him and the lane, and the wind came up off the water smelling of weed and sewage. There he put his forehead against the stone and wept with his mouth shut, so that it came out through his nose, ugly, and the strap knocked against the wall.

When it was done he wiped his face with his palms. Then he rubbed his right thumb into the hollow of his left palm, hard, and did not notice that he did it.

⁂

The door the Romans forgot was across the river in Trastevere, behind the boatmen’s quays. After a second night under the arches, Angeska found it on the Sunday at first light, and came back to say, in the voice she used for prices, that there were Egyptians by the river, and the woman who kept their house had not shut it in her face.

Tamrat smelled it from the lane: beans simmering since dawn with cumin and garlic; under the beans, the bitter clean lime of whitewash; under the lime, faint, Coptic incense, the heavy sweet resin he had last smelled at the Patriarch’s door in Cairo. His eyes filled. It was the beans, he told himself.

It was one long whitewashed room with the river at its back. He stopped inside the door and counted. One lamp: a small clay lamp on a bracket low on the east wall, at the height of a man’s belt, below an icon of Saint Mark and his lion. A hearth at the far end. A board, benches, rushes. Grey light from a window on the river.

He took the bench under the window, the whole width of the room from the lamp. Then he blessed the house.

The woman who kept it came from the hearth wiping her hands: perhaps fifty, small and broad, in widow’s black, a blue cross inside her wrist, with the long-boned face of the river towns above Aswan. She looked at the old priest, the woman, the boy with the weal.

“Peace to you,” she said in Arabic. “You are the Abyssinians the canons of Saint Peter turned off their step.”

“We are the embassy of the King of Kings of Ethiopia.”

“Then they turned off an embassy. Sit down.”

There were four others in the room: two old men of the Fayyum, a woman with a sick child in a shawl, and by the hearth a narrow bald man with a pilgrim’s leaden badge, already up and coming down the room with both hands out.

“Cousin,” said Bishoy of Akhmim, to the boy.

He took Zoser by the shoulders, and saw the neck, and his hands stopped. He looked once at Tamrat. Then he embraced the boy anyway, and held him off again by both hands. “Since Friday I have been asking for you at every inn by the bridges,” he said. “They told me the Abyssinians were lodged with a cardinal.”

The widow was Dimiana, of Aswan. When her husband died she had brought his ferry-silver to the tombs of the apostles to pray for him, found that the Copts of Rome had nowhere to sleep but the porches of the Latins, and stayed. Eleven years she had kept a door open here for the people of the Nile.

“Two pennies a night,” she said, “and the pilgrim brings his own bread. Those are the rules of my house.”

Angeska showed her the purse. Dimiana looked into it, and did not laugh.

“I am a priest forty years at the altar of Bet Medhani Alem in Lalibela,” said Tamrat. “I have preached before the King of Kings.” His voice filled the room to its rafters, and the sick child turned its head. “It is the third Sunday of our fast, and your house has heard no sermon in its own tongue today. I will give it one.”

Dimiana considered him a long time, her head on one side.

“If it is a good one,” she said.

⁂

He preached at the ninth hour, before the meal, with the light going and the beans breathing in the pot.

They sat on the benches: the Fayyum men, the woman with the child, Bishoy, Angeska by the door. The widow sat on a stool against the east wall, beside the icon and its lamp. The boy sat at the back on the rushes, his knees drawn up.

Tamrat stood in the middle of the floor, two strides from the lamp, and began in Arabic.

He gave them the rich man clothed in purple and fine linen, and the beggar laid at his gate full of sores, desiring the crumbs; and the woman of Tyre and Sidon who was told, *It is not meet to take the children’s bread, and to cast it to the dogs.* The Fayyum men nodded; they had heard both since they were boys.

Then he told them how it was at the gate.

“The gate,” he said, “was a door of bronze.”

At the back of the room, on the rushes, the boy’s face changed.

Tamrat’s eyes were good at a distance. It was nothing, a lift of the head, a stillness, but it went through the whole face at once and stayed. The boy had Arabic. He had heard two hundred times how Tamrat of Lalibela had debated the finest minds of Constantinople and found them wanting. He had never heard of a door.

Tamrat’s mouth was open. The next word was not in it.

The pot ticked on the fire. The sick child sniffed. Three breaths, four, before he found where he had been; and he went back and began the sentence again from its first word, slowly, and did not look toward the back of the room.

“The gate was a door of bronze, and before it there was a step, and the step was worn hollow in the middle by the knees of other men. Many had knelt there before him. The stone had given way under them. He knelt in the hollow they had made.”

Nobody in the Gospel kneels on a step. The Fayyum men had leaned forward.

“He knelt there one day. He knelt there two days. He knelt there three days, with the cold of the step coming up through his garment into his knees, and he heard them inside at the feast, and once they laughed. And on the third day the door opened.”

The widow had put her hand to her mouth.

“And they took him in, and turned out his bag before him on the floor: his bread, his cross, the letter of his master, which they held up to the light and laughed at. They put their hands into his garment. Into his sash. Under his arms. To see what a beggar keeps.” He heard his own voice from far off, steady, a good preacher’s voice giving a parable. “And then a deacon of that house came and gave him the kiss of peace, as the Gospel commands, on the one cheek and the other. And stepped back, and wiped his hand on his robe.”

He showed them. He drew his right palm down the front of his shamma, slowly, from breast to thigh.

The widow was weeping openly, after the manner of the churches of the Nile, the tears running into the lines beside her mouth.

Something rose in him at the sight of her, from the same place as the weeping at the buttress, and he did not want to stop it. His sermon had gone into a woman and come out of her eyes; she was the first soul in Rome who had received him. He went to her. One step toward her stool, then a second, his hands going out open to take hers, his mouth saying, “Yet the dogs under the table, daughter, the dogs under the table eat of the—”

The bracket stood out from the wall at the height of his sash, and the second step had brought his sash to a hand’s breadth of it. The flame stood on its wick small and yellow below the lion of Mark; and its point, its very point, began to come round toward him, as a dog lifts its head at a step in the yard.

He stopped.

He stopped with one foot set down and his weight not yet on it, his hands in the air above the widow’s, the word *crumbs* unspoken. One breath he did not take. A second. A third. He did not look at the flame.

He stepped back, both steps, slowly, and set his heels in the rushes where he had first stood, an arm’s length and more from the lamp, and folded his hands over his belly.

“—eat of the children’s crumbs,” he said, from there. “And the Lord said unto her, For this saying go thy way.”

His voice did not shake. He finished from where he stood, the beggar carried by angels into Abraham’s bosom, the daughter healed from afar without a hand laid on her, and the room said *Amen*, and he did not know what he had said between.

Dimiana came to him with her wet face and took both his hands before he could prevent her, and kissed them, the backs and then the palms.

“The Spirit came down on you,” she said. “In the middle. I saw it. You stopped, and it came.”

Over her bent head he could see the lamp. It stood straight and small and yellow on its wick, smoking a little at the tip, and there was nothing in it.

“There is no fee in my house for a sermon like that,” said the widow. “Not while you are in Rome.”

She let go of him and went to stir the beans. He stood where she had left him, his right thumb rubbing round and round in the hollow of his left palm, wiping it.

⁂

The widow’s cat came in at the river window as the bowls were set out, a long yellow beast with torn ears and a fish-head in its jaws, and went under the board with it. The fish-head stank under the board against the cumin.

Dimiana went after it with the ladle, scolding. Not in Arabic. It was a quick tongue that rose and fell over itself, and Tamrat knew it without one word of it: the tongue the boatmen shout at the Cataract above Aswan, hauling the boats up the white water. He had heard it last autumn, from the Nubian bank, with his lips split.

From the back of the room someone answered her.

One word. It came out of the boy on the rushes with no more effort than a breath, in the same tongue and the same quick music, and the widow turned with the ladle raised and her face alight, and said something back to him in it, three or four words, a question.

The boy opened his mouth.

Nothing came. Tamrat watched him look for it, lips parted, eyes gone inward, groping for a door he knew was there and finding the wall, and the wall again. The colour went out of his face from the brow down, until under the brown he was the grey of cold ash.

It was a word, Tamrat understood, that the boy had not said since the ashes.

Dimiana said something else in it, gently. The boy shook his head and went out by the river door without a word in any tongue.

He had come up out of the river country in the famine year with ash in his hair; Tamrat had said so at Abba Talka’s table and the imam’s and on the dock at Alexandria, to make men laugh. *Wet clay.* The mud of the Nile, with no shape in it until a master gave it one.

The clay had had a tongue. Someone on that river had spoken to it in this tongue, and it had answered, before Tamrat ever laid a hand on it. He had called it clay because he did not know its mother’s word for it.

His face was hot. He did not look at the river door.

⁂

Before anyone ate, the widow brought him her book.

It lay in her palm: two small boards bound in black leather worn soft, tied with a thong. Inside, leaf after leaf of names in Arabic, in many hands, and against many of them a small red cross. It smelled of old leather and of the hearth-smoke in her black sleeve.

“My dead,” Dimiana said, “and the living I have promised to pray for. At every tomb of the apostles I read them all.” She turned to an empty leaf. “I cannot write, and the priests here are Latins. Write my husband for me, father. Here, at the apostles.”

His eyes would not do it in his lap. He held the little book out at the full stretch of his arm toward the window, and Angeska, unasked, brought the boy’s reed and his shell of soot-ink from the satchel and held the shell for him; and he wrote at that length, in the Arabic of the imam’s table, large and careful.

“Pakhom son of Shenouda,” said Dimiana. “Ferryman at the Cataract. He fell asleep in the year of the low river.”

She touched the letters she could not read with one finger. Then she gave him the book again, shy now.

“And yours, father. So that someone prays for the father who prays for us.”

Nobody in Rome had asked his name. The reed shook once at the end of his arm. He wrote his name and his father’s, and what he had done in her house, because she would read it aloud at the tomb of Peter. He wrote *his wife*. He wrote the boy, because the boy was his, and he wanted the apostles to hear that Tamrat of Lalibela kept a scribe.

“And write that you paid,” said Dimiana. “So the fathers at the Hanging Church do not think I took silver from a priest.”

> FROM THE DIPTYCH OF DIMIANA OF ASWAN, WIDOW, IN ARABIC
>
> Pakhom son of Shenouda, ferryman at the First Cataract, her husband, who fell asleep in the year of the low river.
>
> Tamrat son of Habta Maryam, priest of the Ethiopians, who preached to us on the third Sunday of the fast; his wife; the boy Zoser, his scribe.
>
> They paid with a sermon.

She blew on the ink, tied the thong, and put the book into the breast of her black gown.

⁂

At the meal Angeska moved the lamp.

The widow had set it in the middle of the board and lit a twin for Saint Mark. Tamrat came last, counted the two flames, and took the end of the bench by the window. The board was short.

Angeska rose, lifted the lamp in both hands, and carried it down to the far end, past the Fayyum men, past Bishoy.

“So the old man’s eyes can rest from it,” she said to the widow, in Arabic, pleasantly. “He has read too long by bad lamps on the road.”

Tamrat looked at his wife breaking her bread across from him. She had seen his eyes were tired, and spared him. Something tight in him since the shutter came down eased a little, and he let it.

The beans came to him hot in a wooden bowl, with oil and a little cumin, and the steam came up into his face, and his belly turned over under the sash with longing. He measured the board. The lamp burned at the far end of it.

He did not lean.

“I keep the fast,” he said. “I have eaten.”

Nobody argued. Tamrat sat with his hands folded before the steaming bowl until it was taken away, and the smell of the cumin stayed in his beard.

The boy came in at the river door as the bowls were cleared, the cat under his arm. Dimiana put a bowl before him without a word, and he ate every bean, and wiped the bowl with his bread.

“After the Easter of the Latins,” Bishoy said, “when the sea opens, we go home together, the Fayyum men and the woman and I. Ostia, Damietta, then up the river to Cairo. If the Abyssinians have letters for the Patriarch, or for anyone in Egypt or beyond it, I will carry them.”

Tamrat inclined his head. The boy said nothing. His hand had gone to the scrip at his thigh.

⁂

When the others had gone to their mats, Tamrat stayed at the board. There was a lamp, and there was a table, and he had sat at neither since the Jews’ bridge.

“The questions at sea,” he said, in Amharic. “You wrote them down.”

The boy was at the far end, by the lamp, the psalter open before him. “At Benevento, Qess, when I had ink again. At the back of the psalter.”

“Read me the record.”

The boy turned the leaves past the friar’s Latin written out in Ge’ez letters, and tipped the leaf to the lamp. The sea had swollen the vellum, and it smelled of damp hide; under the window the Tiber went by in the dark. Tamrat sat at the other end in the dark, where his eyes were good and his belly was far from the light. The boy read in a plain voice, with no colour and no pause.

The boy’s voice stopped at the foot of the line. Then it went on in the same plain register.

“The priest did not answer.”

One of the Fayyum men snored. Tamrat heard it spoken in the voice of a register, among the dead, and his face went hot, and then cold.

“Strike it,” he said.

The boy looked up. With the lamp at his hand and his face turned toward the dark, he was a shape only; but Tamrat saw the shape hold still a long time, and look down at the leaf, at the reed, at the leaf.

The boy dipped the reed. He set its point to the leaf, slowly, drew it across once, and lifted it. From where Tamrat sat it was the motion of a hand in a small light, and he could not see what line it left and did not try. He had said *strike it*, and the hand had gone across.

Angeska sat halfway down the board, nearer the boy, nearer the lamp, with her chin on her hand. She looked at the leaf for as long as it takes to read a line, and said nothing.

The boy did not explain himself. He laid the reed down and read on, in the same voice.

“That is all,” the boy said. “He asked nothing else.”

“It is enough.”

The boy closed the psalter.

Angeska got up and went down the room to the door onto the lane, where the widow had dropped the bar for the night, a beam of old oak lying in two iron brackets. She laid her palm flat on it.

“*Ostium*,” she said.

The boy looked at her. She had not held out her hand for the psalter, and she did not hold it out now. She waited with her palm on the barred door.

“Door,” said the boy.

“*Ostium*.”

“*Ostium*,” he said. “Door.”

She lifted the bar a finger’s breadth and let it fall. It went home in the iron with a short heavy sound, the sound of a key gone all the way round in a lock; and Tamrat, at the dark end of the board, felt the hair rise on his arms and did not know why.

⁂

On the Monday his wife was gone before the light, her shawl from the peg and her shoes from the door. She had gone out when the bar came off at the first bell, the widow said, and said nothing of where.

He did not wait for her. Twelve days in Rome and no one had received him. He went to the Lateran with Bishoy for his tongue, past the great round of the old games, where goats cropped the terraces among the wet ash.

The Lateran was a palace and a church run together, with grass in its gutters. Two canons came out in grey fur, going to the station of the day; they looked at Tamrat, and one said something to the other, who laughed, and then they spoke to Bishoy.

Bishoy answered them. His face had gone stiff.

“What do they say?”

“That the Lord Pope is in Avignon, father, and his vicar sees no one in Lent without letters, and letters should be shown to a notary.”

“They laughed.”

Bishoy did not look at him. “They asked where the Ethiopian’s woman is. The one who speaks for him. They said it as Romans say such things.”

“What else?”

“Nothing for a priest’s ear, father.”

The canons went in, and a breath of Latin incense came out at the door, thin and sweet, before it shut.

⁂

She did not come back all day.

He sat under the window with his hands on his belly and watched the door. The boy copied the widow’s reckoning at the end of the board, and did not look at him. The weal on his neck had gone dark.

She knew this city. She knew roads to it that were not on the captain’s map. She had told him once, the first winter, by his fire at Lalibela, that her mother’s father had been a merchant of Aksum who traded in Rome, and that she had learned her first Latin at his knee. The old merchant would have known men in Rome. Those men had sons.

*The Ethiopian’s woman.*

He thought of her voice on the deck above his head the night Desta died, running soft and fast in the language of Rome, and her low laughter. He thought of Abba Talka at the church gate the morning after the wedding no bishop blessed, looking at him a moment too long and saying nothing. A priest of the Tewahedo marries once, before he is made priest; if his wife dies, he lives alone. He had married Ilfinesh, who bit her thread with her side teeth, and buried her. And then, at sixty, a woman thirty years younger with seven tongues in her head. His mother on the lake road used to say that what a man takes without a blessing he has no right to keep.

The light went out of the window. The widow lit her lamps.

His wife came in at dusk.

She came down the room toward him through the rushes, and he looked at her with his good far eyes, and saw.

Her hair had been braided again. She had gone out in the loose braids of the road, full of the dust of Italy; she came back with it combed out and put up in close fine rows tight to her head, a feast-day braiding, another woman’s fingers. As she passed him to the water jar the air off her hands smelled of soap: not the grey lye of the widow’s tub but something sweet, with oil in it. Someone else’s soap.

She dipped a cup and drank a little, and set the cup down half full, and turned and looked at him.

For twelve days he had seen the city’s hunger in her eyes, and in the boy’s: they went to a face and then to its hands, to see if there was bread in them. Her eyes came to his face and stayed there. She had eaten. Somewhere in this city, today, a man had fed his wife.

The fist closed under his sash and held.

He said nothing. He got up off the bench without his hands and went to the east wall, stopped an arm’s length and more from the lamp before Saint Mark, and went down on his knees in the rushes.

He prayed in Ge’ez, without sound, his forehead almost to the floor, his right thumb working in the hollow of his left palm. *Lord, who stayed the hand of Abraham over the boy, stay mine. Hold my hand from her. Let me not strike her as I struck the boy, in this house, before these strangers, under this lamp.* The rushes smelled of the river and the cat. Above him at its distance the lamp burned small and yellow and straight.

When he raised his head she was standing beside him.

She held out a paper folded twice, sealed in red wax with a shield of bands and a rose above them. Under the Latin of the outer fold someone had written a line in small careful Arabic.

“A summons,” she said. “For Thursday week, the twenty-seventh day of February in their reckoning. An audience before the Cardinal Giovanni, at San Francesco a Ripa.”

He took it. It was heavy paper, and smelled of wax. He held it out to the full length of his arm, and at that distance, where everything came clear for him now, the Arabic came clear.

*To Angeska, the woman of the Ethiopians, who speaks their tongues; and those of her household.*

He read it twice. He looked for his own name, for the King of Kings’, for the word *priest*. They were not there. There was her name, and after it her household: the boy at the end of the board, and an old man on his knees in the rushes, holding her summons out at the full length of his arm so that he could see it.
