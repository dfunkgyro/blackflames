# CHAPTER 2

## ANGESKA

*Alexandria — Advent 1325 (three weeks earlier)*

“Tell him,” Tamrat said, “that I have debated with the finest minds in Constantinople.”

The Catalan shipmaster was not looking at any of them. He stood at the foot of his gangplank with a tally stick and a knife, cutting a notch for every bale of pepper his porters carried down off the cog and into the warehouse. Above him Latin pilgrims back from the Sepulchre, with no passage home now until spring, lined the rail and looked down at the four Ethiopians with the interest Lalibela kept for a hyena near the houses, and from a height.

“He has said no, my lord,” Angeska said in Amharic. “Twice. His ship is going nowhere.”

“His ship is full of Franks.” Tamrat drew himself up inside a shamma that had been white at Lalibela and was the colour of the road now. “Tell him who asks. Tell him I have stood among the finest minds of the Greeks and found them all wanting, and that a man who has done that does not wait on a quay while a sailor counts sacks.”

She had heard the story perhaps two hundred times, at Abba Talka’s table and in the Nubian sand with his lips split, always whole and in the same words, a triumph worn smooth by handling. In two hundred tellings he had never named one of the finest minds, or said who had spoken last. She had noticed it the first time, when she was a girl at the edge of the lamplight, and she put it again where she kept such things.

“The holy father thanks you for your patience,” she said to the Catalan in the mixed Frankish of the ports, “and asks whether any master in this harbour sails west, now that the season is closed.”

The knife stopped on the stick. The man looked at her for the first time, and at her mouth, which he seemed to suspect of a trick with a coin. “After Saint Andrew? Nobody who loves his hull, lady. We were the last, and we’re laid up.” He jerked his chin along the quay. “Ask the Genoese. They know who’s mad this year.”

He cut his notch. The next bale went past in a hot brown cloud of pepper and sweat, and under it lay the harbour’s own smell, nine days in her clothes: tar softening in the sun, fish guts by the steps, cumin from the cookshops, and from behind the customs house, absurdly, roses, where a boy was sprinkling rosewater on bales of silk to lay the dust.

“What does he say?” Tamrat asked.

“That no one sails in the closed season but madmen, and the Genoese know which.”

“He said more than that. I heard him laugh.”

“He laughed at the season, my lord.”

He had not laughed at all. Angeska let her sentence stand, as she let a hundred stand each day, small and smoothed, as the tide smooths the stones it has finished turning over. Tamrat’s eyes went past her to Zoser, who stood under the packs with the satchel of the embassy’s books in his arms. Tamrat’s own bag, with the King of Kings’ letter in it, he carried himself. The boy chose that moment to let the satchel’s strap slip.

“Hold it properly. God give me patience.” Tamrat did not lower his voice; he never had. “A mind like wet clay, requiring constant shaping by a master’s hand.”

The boy flinched: a tightening at the corner of the eye, a drawing-in of the shoulders under a hand that had not come down. He was twenty-one, a man by any count the mountains kept, he had heard the sentence as often as she had heard the one about Constantinople, and he flinched every time. A man could get used to being struck, she had thought once. It seemed a man could not get used to being called soft.

Desta, beside the boy, looked up at the gulls that turned and screamed over the fish-steps. “Camels,” he said to no one. “A camel lets you know when it means to bite.”

From the minaret by the Pharos road a muezzin began the call to the noon prayer, and a breath later, quarrelling with him over the hour, a Coptic bell answered from behind the customs house: a small bell, rung low and behind walls, as bells had been rung in Egypt since the burnings four years ago. The two sounds climbed over each other above the harbour, and neither stopped for the other.

Saint Andrew was two days gone, and with him the last keels that sailed by daylight law. If they did not find a master who sailed outside it they would winter here, in a khan whose master already counted their bowls, and by spring the purse would be gone. In Cairo the changers had sworn that the king of Takrur had passed through with his slaves and his staffs of gold and given gold to every hand that opened, until a dinar bought half what it had. They said *half*. She had done the sum against the silver dirham three days running and made it nearer an eighth; but a changer who swears that gold is worth half will give you half. The highland gold in Tamrat’s girdle had come down the mountains as wealth and arrived in Egypt as a disappointment. The incense was worse; Egypt had incense. And the salt, the long grey bars from the Afar caravans that bought a goat in any highland market, money you could break and eat: a changer on this quay had licked his thumb, tasted one, and set it down with the care a man gives to a child’s toy he does not wish to be rude about.

She had brought the cargo across the salt and the sand and down the river, and she would not lose it on a quay to the calendar of the Franks.

*My husband*, she thought. *I have brought my husband.*

“The Genoese, then,” she said.

⁂

The Genoese factor kept a counting-room in the upper storey of the fondaco by the Pepper Gate, and the stairs to it smelled of everything the Genoese bought in Egypt: pepper and ginger, alum and sugar-loaf, and ink. He was a heavy man with a cold, and he did not rise. He spoke the quick, flattened Arabic of the wharves, and she watched Tamrat’s Arabic, which was the Arabic of books, lose his first sentence and run after the second.

Tamrat began with Constantinople. Angeska rendered it as “the holy father has travelled far in the service of his Church,” which was true, and the factor wiped his nose and said that he did not doubt it.

While they spoke she read his table. She had learned it at her grandfather’s elbow: words upside down are only words. Under the factor’s elbow, half-folded, a letter in a good curial Latin from his principals, which gave its place and day at the foot in a clerk’s round letters: *Datum Avinione, in curia domini Pape.* Given at Avignon, in the court of the lord Pope.

It was not news to her. It was confirmation, which is a different thing: a coin come back from the changer, and bitten.

“I go to lay the letters of the King of Kings before the Holy Father in Rome,” Tamrat was saying, in Amharic, “and to show him a Church that was old when—”

“The holy father journeys to Rome,” Angeska said, “with letters for the Pope.”

The factor’s eyes went to the letter under his elbow and came back to her face, and she gave him nothing: a woman’s face, attending to her husband. He shrugged.

“Four of you,” he said, and wrote it on a scrap, *iiij*, and drew a line under it that closed the account. “Moors.”

“Ethiopians. Christians.”

“To a shipmaster in the closed season, lady, it is four Moors.” He blew his nose. “Every Christian keel in this harbour trades under the Church’s curse and buys its absolution at home, so it goes home before the weather does. One master holds a clean licence, with a seal on it. The *Santa Maria*, Venetian-built. He can winter in the Sultan’s ports and run in the closed season and fear neither the Sultan’s customs nor the Pope’s. He sails when he likes. He carries what he likes.” He wrote a name under it and pushed the scrap across. “Humbert. Tell him the factor sent you, and don’t tell him I said he was mad.”

⁂

The *Santa Maria* lay at the far end of the mole, a high-sided round ship with two masts, her hull stained black with pitch. Her men sat on the bulwark in the sun eating raw onions, and watched the four of them come all the way down from the first step. The onion reached her before their faces did, and under it the pitch, and under that, from the open hatch, the bilge breathing out, slow and sour, in its sleep.

Her master waited at the foot of the gangplank, a man the sea seemed to have carved and left out to dry, his beard a tangle of iron wire.

“Four passengers to Rome,” Tamrat said in Amharic, and held out the little pouch of stones from the mountains, the last thing they had that anyone had admired.

The captain did not take the pouch. He drew out one stone with two fingers and turned it over in his open palm, and Angeska looked at the hand.

The nails were pared short and clean and rounded: a canon’s nails. There was no tar under them. On the forefinger he wore a signet of dark iron, flat-faced, and cut into its face, small and clear, was a key standing upright inside a circle.

*Good*, she thought. *Then it is him.*

He dropped the stone back into the pouch, wiped the two fingers that had held it on his sleeve, one and then the other, and looked them over one by one with the attention a man gives a bill of sale. “Moors,” he said in Arabic, setting the word down whole, to be counted. “I was told Christians.”

His voice did not belong to his face: dry and exact, a clerk’s voice, a voice that had read aloud in cold rooms and been listened to.

“We are Christians,” Tamrat said, before she could speak, in the Arabic he believed to be good.

The captain did not look at him. He said to Angeska, in Latin, pleasantly, trying the door to see whether it was locked: “And you, lady? Have you the tongue of the Church, or only the tongues of the market?”

She chose to halt. She had chosen it on the river, before she knew whose ship it would be.

“Christians,” she said, in Latin, slowly, fishing each word up from the bottom of a jar. “We are. Of... Alexandria. The faith of.”

The captain listened with his head on one side, as a master of grammar listens to a pupil pretending to parse worse than he can.

Tamrat’s hand closed on her arm. “You have their Latin?” he said in Amharic, low.

“A little of the Franks’ Latin, my lord,” she said. “Market words. Enough to ask the price of bread.”

He let go of her arm. She let the sentence stand.

“Heretics, then,” the captain said, in Arabic again, for all of them. He crossed himself, broadly, for the men on the bulwark. “There is one Church. My agreement is to carry Christians.” He looked past her to the first mate, a long-armed man leaning in the shadow of the stern-castle, half-smiling. “Unless they can prove themselves.”

Then they laughed, the captain and the mate, and after them the men along the bulwark with the onions in their fists. It was not a laugh at anything that had been said. It went down the rail from man to man, and Angeska had heard its like once, at dusk on the moors above Lalibela, when the red wolves found a kill and called to one another that there was enough for all.

⁂

In Cairo, in the courtyard of a house behind the spice market, the imam had asked it gently, turning a fig in his fingers. “And how does one distinguish between divine truth and human pride, honoured priest?”

His beard was hennaed the red of a fox. The lamps hung high in his fig tree, well above the heads of his guests. Rosewater stood in a copper basin for their hands, the lamb had been cooked with cumin, and Tamrat, five months on the road and keeping the Fast of the Prophets, had left the lamb on the dish and eaten bread and lentils with a great deal of noise about the lamb.

Twelve days ago. She had translated every word that night.

What Tamrat had said, before the question, was: “When one possesses absolute truth, one need not bend to accommodate error.” What she had said in Arabic was: “One who believes he holds the truth does not easily bend to what he thinks mistaken.” It was the same thought with its knife taken away. The imam had heard the knife in the place where she had taken it out, and asked his question into the gap.

She gave it to Tamrat in Amharic exactly as it had been asked. That was her rule with questions. Answers she softened; questions she let him feel.

He felt it. The blood came up into his face under the grey stubble, and his hand closed on the bread. “Through decades of study and contemplation,” he said, “which reveal truth to those worthy of receiving it.”

“Through long study and prayer,” she said to the imam, “which reveal the truth to those who seek it.”

The imam inclined his head. He said nothing about the word that was not there. He looked at her, though, a moment longer than he looked at Tamrat, and she understood that he had counted it.

It was the guest beside him who answered, a tall man in the white of the Malian scholars, whose classical Arabic made the imam’s sound like the market. Al-Hajj Mahmud of Timbuktu had walked to Mecca behind his king. His party would follow them down the river, two days behind, to the same khan in Alexandria, to wait like them for a ship.

“Your Christ and our Prophet,” he said to Tamrat, through her, “are they not both lights from the same divine source, leading their followers toward the same truth?”

“Both guide the faithful through darkness,” Tamrat said, slowly, testing each word before he put his weight on it, “though naturally the path of Christ represents the fuller revelation.”

She left out *naturally*. She left it on the floor between them, a dropped crumb, and Al-Hajj Mahmud smiled. He had seen it fall.

The Malians had brought their own man to the imam’s table, and he had not eaten. He sat a little back on a rolled mat with a *ngoni* across his knees, a long-necked lute with a skin belly and a few strings of gut. He was older than Mahmud, with a grey fringe of beard and a scar that pulled his lower lip, and when Mahmud named him he put his palm flat on the lute’s belly to quiet it.

“Fodé Kouyaté,” Mahmud said. “A jeli of his people. He keeps the names.”

Angeska greeted him in Mande, a market Mande that put its words down in the wrong places and knew it. The jeli’s eyebrows went up. He answered slowly, as a courtesy, so that she could follow.

“You tell the histories of your king,” she said.

“No.” He moved his thumb along one string without sounding it. “Men in the market tell. Tellers sell. I carry.” He touched the strings one after another. “This is my father’s line. This my mother’s. This the house my fathers have served since before the empire had a name. A man’s blood is strung like this, daughter. If one string goes out of true, the whole song lies.”

Beside him a younger man shifted on his mat. He was perhaps twenty-five, his forearm welted with the pale scars of a man who has held up an arm against an edge, more than once. He had been listening with his jaw working, and at the fuller revelation he had made a sound in his throat.

The talk went on past the night prayer, and the smell of the lamps came down out of the fig tree, sesame oil and burnt wick.

The young man stood up.

“Enough talk,” he said in Arabic, loud, to the courtyard. He spat beside the dish. “The infidel should kneel or bleed.”

Then he said it again in Mande, to his own people, which was worse, because in Mande it was not a slogan; it was an instruction.

Every Malian at the table went still. And Desta, who had sat all night with his back to the wall, with a soldier’s handful of Arabic and none of this table’s, put his hand on the hilt of his sword.

She did not decide what to do. She found herself doing it, in the order the danger lay.

“Desta,” she said in Amharic, without turning her head, “take your hand off it, and look at the bread.” She heard the leather creak as he obeyed.

To the imam, in Arabic, she said: “Shaykh, we have eaten salt at your table.” She let the words sit. “Is the bread of this house to be paid for in our blood?”

The imam put down his fig and spoke a sharp word to the young man, and the young man did not sit, because the imam was not his elder and this was not his house.

Mahmud was beginning to rise, and a young man in a rage would have heard his reasonable Arabic as one more voice telling him to be ashamed. She turned to the man with the lute instead, and said in her poor Mande, in the form a stranger uses to ask a favour of an elder:

“Father of words. Who is this young man? I do not know his people. Will you tell me whose son he is?”

Fodé Kouyaté looked at her, and something passed over his face that was close to a smile and was not one. He sounded the strings once, a low, dry chord, and began.

He did not raise his voice. He gave the young man’s name, and then his father’s, and his father’s father’s, and the names went back along the line as the weft goes back through the warp, each pass beaten home against the last, each with its sound and its pause after. She followed enough. They were a house of words, not of blades. A man who had carried a mansa’s message across the river to his enemies, and brought peace back in his mouth. A man who had sung at Niani at the naming of a king’s son. A man who had kept the names of a whole province through a year of hunger. Then the jeli’s hand moved to another string, and he named a woman: the young man’s mother, Nyuma, a Kouyaté, *my sister*, who had married into that house of words as her own mothers had, and had given water to anyone who came to her door, Muslim or pagan or Christian, and said it was the house’s water and not hers to refuse.

The young man’s face did not soften. It went heavy, a graveside heaviness, with the weight of all those people standing behind it who could not be argued with: men who had made peace with words, and whose blood had reached for an edge. Then he sat down, and looked at his knees, and did not speak again.

Al-Hajj Mahmud, half-risen, sat back. The imam picked up his fig.

“What did he say?” Tamrat asked her, low and furious. “What did you say to them?”

“I asked an old man about his family, my lord.”

Desta took his eyes off the bread. “Remind me,” he said, “never to let an old man count my grandfathers at supper.”

⁂

“Prove ourselves how?” Angeska asked, in Arabic, when the laughter had died among the onions. The wind had shifted and brought the ship’s full smell down the plank, bilge and old wine and wet hemp and the sweat of men who had slept in their clothes since summer, a smell she would live inside for a month. She did not look at the iron on the captain’s hand again.

“Work,” he said. “There are no freeloaders on my ship. Your stones buy you a place to lie down below and a share of the water. The soldier and the boy work the ropes and the pumps when they are told. The woman mends sail and keeps the pots. And the old man—” he let his eyes rest on Tamrat, who stood a pace ahead of them all, the quay his “—scrubs. Every day. On his knees, as a Christian should be.”

The mate laughed again, alone this time.

“What does he say?” Tamrat asked, though she had seen in his face that he had understood the knees.

She told him all of it, the ropes and the pots and the knees, in plain Amharic, without taking the knife out of a single word, because she wanted him to say yes.

Tamrat’s lip drew back from his teeth. For a moment she thought he would take the embassy back up the mole into a winter of debts and changers. Then he turned his face away from the captain, slowly, until there was no captain in it, and said nothing at all.

“He agrees,” Angeska said in Arabic.

The captain took the pouch, weighed it without opening it, and put it inside his coat. “We sail when my lading is aboard,” he said over his shoulder, already on the plank. “Ten days. Twelve. When you see them boiling pitch on the mole, come down.”

Tamrat had walked ten paces up the mole and stood with his back to the ship, so that it should be seen he had not been spoken to; Desta and Zoser were bent over the packs. The mate came down to take their names for the master’s book. He wrote *iiij*, and after it *Mauri*, Moors, and nothing else; she read it upside down, as she read everything. Then she took from her sleeve a small square packet, tied with thread and sealed in plain wax, with no device in the wax at all.

“For your master,” she said in Latin, quietly. “From Alexandria.”

The mate looked at the plain seal. He looked at her. Then he put the packet inside his shirt without a word, as if he had been told to expect it, and went back up the plank.

She had promised it, and it was given. She turned to the others with her hands empty.

The last thing she had sealed before it was Tamrat’s letter home, at Massawa, on a night when the wind off the sea smelled of salt pans and dead weed. She had written it for him in Ge’ez, and they had given it to the novice Giyorgis, who was perhaps twelve and had carried water for them from Lalibela to the coast, two skins on a yoke. He had wept to be sent home; he had wanted to see the Pope. He had put the letter inside his shirt exactly where the mate had just put hers, and gone back up into the mountains behind a trader’s mule, and she had watched him out of sight.

⁂

The Advent days went by, one fast-day after another, and the *Santa Maria* lay at the mole taking in bales by night. The Malians’ ship came first. Their dead were named on the last night before it sailed.

The jeli did it in the courtyard of the khan, around a fire of dried dung that smoked and stank, inside a ring of clay lamps on the paving. Mahmud had asked the embassy to sit with them, and Tamrat had come because he would not be seen to refuse a courtesy he did not understand. Angeska seated him by the gate, out of the light, where he would not have to smell the dung, and sat down herself between him and the ring.

All evening the sea wind had come over the roofs in gusts and worried at the lamps. It tore the smoke off the wicks in rags, and the flames shrank down almost to nothing and swelled again, and twice a lamp blew out and a Malian boy got up without being told and lit it again from the fire. The dung smoke went flat along the ground and into everyone’s eyes.

Fodé Kouyaté stood up with his lute. He did not play it. He held it against his chest and let it sleep.

He began with a man who had died at Walata, before the desert crossing. Then a woman who had died of a fever at Tuat. Then a boy who had fallen from a camel in the night. He named them where they had died and what they had been to the people in the circle, and he did not hurry. Angeska understood perhaps one word in three. The names she understood all of. They did not need translating; they were the thing itself.

She began, from habit, to count them. She lost the count somewhere after the dead of Cairo, when he came to the men who had died in the desert when the water failed, and she could not get it back. In all the time he spoke he did not once give a number.

At some point she saw that the Malian boy had not had to get up again.

The wind had not stopped. She could hear it in the palm-fronds over the gate, and the dung smoke still crawled flat along the stones. But the little flames in the ring had stopped shrinking and swelling. They stood on their wicks in the wind, every one of them, still as painted flames, and the smoke went up from them in threads that did not break. None went out. She did not look away from them until he had named the last man, a Fulbe porter who had died two streets from where they sat, three nights before; and when the jeli lowered his lute and sat down, the wind came back into the ring, and the first lamp went out.

Nobody in the circle remarked on it. Perhaps it was always so.

She was afraid of him. She sat with her hands folded and her face composed, more afraid of the old man with the scar on his lip than she had been of the young one with the scars on his arm.

⁂

The night before they sailed she lay on her bedroll on the khan’s flat roof, because the rooms below stank of the cookshop, and looked at the Egyptian stars.

Tamrat slept beside her with his knees drawn up and his mouth open. He had been silent for days, as he was when a day had shamed him. His feet stuck out from under the blanket. He was her husband by a marriage no bishop had blessed, and his feet were always cold. She drew the blanket down over them, carefully, and kept her hand on his ankle a moment longer than she needed to, the bone of it under her palm, old and thin and warm. Then she lay down again.

A little way off, along the parapet, Desta and Zoser were not asleep.

She could hear something being chewed, and smell it: roasted barley and chickpea, the kolo the women of Lalibela parched for anyone going down the mountain. Desta had carried a pouch of it from home, and every night he held out a handful to whoever was beside him. Tonight it was the boy, and the boy was not allowed to eat it yet.

“Count it back to me,” Desta said. “Grain by grain. Then it’s yours.”

“You gave Abba Tamrat a whole handful at Aswan. He didn’t have to count it.”

“Abba Tamrat can already count. A man who can count kolo in the dark can count sentries in the dark. Go on.”

“Twenty-six,” the boy said, patient. “Twenty-seven.”

There was a pause while he ate. The air smelled of salt and fish-scale, and the stars were very bright and very many, as they never were in the highlands, where every evening the cloud came down and shut them away.

“Now,” Desta said. “Look where I’m pointing. No. Follow my finger, not my face. There. The little cluster, close together, like a hen with her chicks round her. You see her?”

“I see her.”

“When she’s up over your head like that, the night’s half gone. If you’re on watch and the man who relieves you is late, you look for the Hen, and if she’s past the top you go and kick him.” His arm moved. “Now down there, lower, east of her. Three in a row.”

“Three. Yes.”

“The Spearmen. They come up in the first watch and they follow her across all night. When the Hen is going down in the west and the Spearmen are going down after her, it’s near enough dawn that you can stop pretending to be awake.” A grain crunched. “You put your finger on each one. Go on. Don’t just look. Touch it, like you’re counting your brothers.”

Against the stars she could see the boy’s forefinger moving from point to point with the care he gave a pen on a leaf, and Desta’s arm beside it, guiding.

“How many men did you have on watch,” Zoser asked, “in the war?”

Desta’s voice did not change. “I don’t count the living. I count the other ones. Since Ifat. Every man who went down beside me, I gave him a number, so as not to lose him.” Another grain. “I stopped at thirty-nine.”

“Why did you stop?”

“Because the war stopped. Mostly.” The finger came down. “Thirty-nine. I don’t do forty.” And then, in a tone she had not heard from him on the whole road, lighter, a man setting something heavy down on a high shelf, out of the boy’s reach: “You’re too young to be my forty. So don’t get any ideas on that boat.”

The boy laughed. It was a startled sound, and young, and it went out over the parapet into the dark above the harbour.

Angeska lay with her eyes open and filed it away.

She did it without deciding to, as she had with the factor’s letter and the flinch: a thing set down in its place, to be found when needed. A man who would not let a boy be his fortieth was a man who would stand in front of the boy. A man who would stand in front of the boy could be put where the boy was, and he would stay there. She noted that. She noted, too, that she had thought it, lying on her back under the Hen and the Spearmen with her hand still warm from her husband’s ankle, and that it had not cost her anything to think.

The talk ran down. After a while the boy was asleep, and Desta was a dark shape against the parapet that did not lie down.

She drifted. At the edge of sleep she saw Desta get up, quietly for a man his size, and cross to where the boy lay. He did not touch the boy. He reached past him for the scrip, ran his fingers along its strap from end to end, tested the buckle, and set it down exactly where it had lain, its flap toward the boy’s hand.

He did not touch his own kit, or look at it. He made his round of the boy’s scrip as he had made his rounds of the King’s baggage for twenty years, and went back to his place against the parapet, and sat down facing the stairs.

⁂

In the morning the harbour smelled of tar. Two men were boiling pitch on the quay beside the *Santa Maria*, and the black smoke hung over the mole in the windless air so thick she could taste it. The water by the steps was the colour of strong tella and full of fish-heads.

Desta went up the plank first, with his sword on his hip, and spoke to no one. Zoser went after him with the book-satchel, his scrip against his thigh and his eyes on the masts.

Tamrat stopped at the foot of the plank.

He stood there for three breaths, looking up at the black hull, and she saw him lift his right hand and lay it flat against his belly, under the shamma, below the ribs. It was not the gesture of a man with a cramp. It was slower, and more careful: a man’s hand over his purse in a crowd. He held it there. He did not seem to know that he had done it.

She came up beside him, close, where the deck could not hear.

“Is it heavy?” she asked softly.

He turned on her. The colour came up into his face as it had come up at the imam’s table. “You will not speak to me of pride,” he said, low and shaking. “Not you. Not here, in front of these. I have stood before the finest minds in Constantinople, woman, and I will not be lectured on the weight of my own soul by a—”

He did not finish it. He took his hand off his belly, and gathered his shamma, and went up the plank with his back as straight as a processional cross, and did not look down.

Angeska stood a moment longer on the quay. Then she looked down between the black hull and the stones of the mole, at the brown water slapping there with the fish-heads turning in it, and smiled at it.
