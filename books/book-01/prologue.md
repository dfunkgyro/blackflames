# PROLOGUE

## GEBRE

*Bet Maryam, Lalibela — c.1310*

Abba Talka put him at the woman’s feet and pressed his hands down onto them, one on each, the way a man shows a boy how to hold a kid still for the knife.

“When she stops, we burn her,” the old priest said. “Here. Before the bell.”

Gebre held the feet. They were hot with fever, hotter than anything living had a right to be in that church at that hour, and the heat came up into his palms as if he had laid them on a hearthstone. Under his knees the floor was cold enough to ache. The rugs had been rolled back to the pillars, and the bare tuff beneath them was red and damp and smelled of a quarry after rain. Over that lay the frankincense from the censer by the north wall, thick and sweet, and under both, near the woman’s mouth, something sour that Gebre did not want to name.

*We burn her.*

Let it be done before the bell, he thought. And in the same breath, before he could stop it: let her live.

He knew the law as he knew the first psalm. The dead went into the earth, wrapped and sung over, their faces to the east. Fire for the dead was a heathen thing, from the stories told to make boys shiver. And nothing was let die inside a church if the brothers could carry it out in time; a church that so much as bled was shut, its lamps cold, until the Abuna’s word came to hallow it again. This was the Mother’s house. Her painted face looked down from above the north door, and the woman lay on the bare stone beneath it with her head toward the curtain that hid the altar’s heart and her feet toward the west door.

Gebre held her feet, and did as he was told.

The kebero hung silent on its peg by the curtain; no one would beat it tonight. The bell would ring when the brother who kept it woke, and the cold woke him every night at the same depth of the dark, as faithfully as if the bell rang itself. Gebre knew how far the first stroke carried in still air, and how long it took a sleeping man to find his sandals, come down the trench with his prayer-staff and his sleep still in his mouth, and file in at that door to stand where the woman lay. Through the small window high in the east wall he could see no stars. Cloud, then. The cold in the stone had not yet hardened. There was time, or there was not.

“Abba,” he said.

“Hold her.”

Abba Talka did not look at him. He was at the north wall, bent over the bundles Gebre had carried in at dusk: juniper split from the woodstore, the oil jar from the lamp-niche. He had carried them in thinking they were for a fire in the court, to warm her.

He did not want her to stop.

He set it before God in the plain words of a prayer, and was ashamed, because it was a sin against obedience. Let her breathe until the bell, and past it. If she was breathing when the brothers came, they would find a sick pilgrim and a deacon chafing her feet, and that was mercy. They would carry her out to the pool in the court and give her the water to drink, and some who drank it lived. And if she died after that she would die outside, with her face to the east, and no one would burn anything.

He wanted that. He also wanted, with a fear that loosened his bowels, the thing Abba Talka meant to do done and swept and gone before the first stroke. He could not imagine the brothers’ faces if they came in on it.

So he held her feet and counted her breaths and wanted both.

Her breaths came long apart. Each went out in a rattle he felt through her heels. He counted the silences like the beats of a chant, and every time he reached a number that seemed too long, she dragged in another.

He had washed a great many pilgrims’ feet, at the door, in a clay basin. He knew feet better than faces. He could have told from hers, with his eyes shut, that she had walked a long way on stone, that she was not old, that she had not always been thin. The soles were split. One toenail was gone, and the bed of it was black with old blood and road.

He moved his thumbs a little, to keep the heat from settling in one place, and felt the lines.

They were raised, just. They ran across the top of the left foot below the ankle bone, a thumb’s breadth apart: a short ridge, a short ridge, a long one, a short. He went still. Then he moved his hand up onto the shin, slowly, and they went on there, row above row the way lines go down a page, short and long, some so old they were only a smoothness under the skin. A few had a small stroke across one end. None of them was crooked. Whoever had cut them had cut them slowly, with a steady hand, over years, and had meant every one.

He thought of the signs the teachers wrote above the words in the chant-books, that told the voice where to rise. He had never learned to read those.

Someone counted something here, he thought. Then: no. Someone said something here.

He took his hand from her shin. It felt like reading over a man’s shoulder.

The boy was in the doorway.

He had been there since dusk, since the pilgrims who carried her in had gone out muttering, crossing themselves. Abba Talka had twice told him to go and sit in the court, and the boy had stood where he was on the sill, half in the rain-smell and half in the incense.

He was six, or seven. His legs were dusty to the knee. He had not cried where Gebre could hear, and he did not come in. He held his right hand pressed against his belly in a fist so tight the knuckles stood out pale, and from between two of the fingers a thread of dark cord hung down and swung a little when he breathed.

The woman’s breath dragged in, and this time something came with it. Her eyes opened.

They went to the door. Not to the painted Mother, not to the deacon at her feet. Straight to the boy, as if she had known all along where in the room he stood.

“My lion,” she said.

The boy came off the sill then, two steps, three, onto the wet stone, until he stood just behind Gebre’s shoulder and his dusty knees were a hand’s breadth from his mother’s burning feet. His fist pressed harder against his belly.

Then she spoke again, in a voice no louder than her breath, that went no farther than her feet, and it was in the tongue of the altar.

Gebre’s hands jerked. It was the tongue of the psalter, the tongue the brothers sang the night office in, a tongue for God and the dead. Her mouth made it strangely, the way one speaks a tongue learned from a single teacher, for a single purpose. But Gebre had sung it every night since he was eight, and he understood her as plainly as if she had spoken in his ear.

“Let no priest see it.”

The boy’s eyes came off his mother’s face, slowly, and went to Abba Talka’s back, and then down to Gebre.

Across the church the old man had not turned. He was bent at the north wall, the juniper rustling under his hands, humming the psalm for the dead under his breath, the way he hummed when he trimmed the lamps.

Gebre was not a priest. He was a deacon; he swung the censer and washed the feet, and could not consecrate so much as a crumb. But he wore the white of the church and a cross at his neck, and to a boy of six he was a priest the way the hewn churches were mountains. He felt his face go hot. She had said it in a breath meant for her son alone, with the old priest’s back turned, and she had said it over Gebre’s hands, and he did not know whether she had forgotten he was there or had meant him to hear.

Her eyes stayed on the boy. Her lips moved again without sound, and then the breath went out of her in its long rattle and did not come back.

Gebre counted.

He counted past the place where she had always dragged in another. He pressed his thumbs into the hot arches of her feet as if he could push the breath back up through her from below, and he counted on, and at his shoulder the cord swung once, twice, beneath the boy’s fist and was still.

The heat was slow to leave her feet, as a hearthstone is slow. He could not have said when it began to go. That was the worst of it: that he could feel no moment at all.

A hand came down on his shoulder, dry and light as a leaf.

“She has stopped,” Abba Talka said.

The boy went back to the sill without being told, and stood there.

They lifted her together, Abba Talka at the head and Gebre at the feet, and she weighed less than a sack of grain. The juniper lay in a bed on the bare floor between the two nearest pillars, where the roof was highest, and they set her down on it in her own white cloth. Abba Talka drew the cloth up over her face. He did not draw it over her legs. Gebre saw that, and did not ask.

The old priest’s hands were steady, long and brown and spotted. They tipped the oil jar along her length, without haste and without waste, the way they broke the bread at the altar, and the smell rose up rancid and heavy and fought the incense and lost.

“Abba.” Gebre’s voice cracked. “It is the Mother’s house.”

Abba Talka took the tallow lamp from its niche, and the light ran up the underside of his face and made it a stranger’s.

“It was done so in my master’s time, and in his master’s,” he said, very low, as if the painted saints might hear. “To such as her. Not often. Never written. Never spoken after.” He looked at Gebre then, for the first time since dusk. “Never spoken after, Gebre.”

He began the psalm again, aloud now, softly, and tipped the lamp to the oil at her feet.

The oil took, as oil does. A yellow tongue ran along the wood, quick and ordinary, with the stink of tallow and resin and a hiss where it found a wet splinter, and it threw its light up onto the Mother’s face, and Gebre’s whole body leaned away from it.

Abba Talka’s lips stopped in the middle of a verse. They hung open a moment on nothing. Then they went back and began the verse again from its first word, as a man does who has lost his place on the page.

Then the yellow went out of it.

It did not die. It went on burning, and the burning went dark from within, from where she lay, out toward its edges, the way a cloth darkens as it drinks water. Only along the underside of the juniper, and at the split ends of the sticks where they stood out past her, a rind of yellow clung, thin as a thread, and it stayed there, low and small, as if the wood would not go over. Everything that was her went black, and the wood where she lay on it. What stood up from her was not a light. It was a shape cut out of the air, a long tongue of nothing, with a rim around it the colour of a bruise. It climbed as fire climbs. It made no sound at all: no crackle, no hiss, no roar of air drawn in. Gebre heard his own breath. He heard the small wet click of Abba Talka’s throat as the old man swallowed.

And it was cold.

He felt it on his face first, like the air of a high pass before sunrise. On the damp red floor around the juniper a whiteness crept out through the grain of the stone. Frost. It made a ring around her a hand’s breadth wide, and the ring did not melt.

The candles leaned.

There were two before the painted Mother and one in the niche by the curtain, beeswax all three, and as Gebre watched they bent toward the floor between the pillars. They tipped, each at its own pace, the way a man’s head tips when he hears his name spoken across a crowded court and turns to find who said it, and having turned they held there, listening. Their tips went blue. Then one after another they sank into their wicks and went out, and their three threads of smoke bent, also, toward the fire.

A sound came out of Gebre’s mouth.

The smoke of the pyre did not rise. It poured. It slid off the black flame and down over the juniper and spread across the floor, grey and slow and heavy, out over the frost and around his knees and his bare feet, colder than the stone. It smelled of the treasury chest when the brothers opened it on feast days to bring out the oldest books: cold dust, old hide, ink gone brown, and under that something like the sea he had never seen. He could not smell her at all.

The cloth had not covered her legs, and the dark took them first. The fire was black and her shins inside it were black, and yet the lines on them showed. They showed darker. Short, short, long, short: the rhythm he had felt under his thumbs, row beneath row, cut out of the dark in a deeper dark. The fire went along each row the way a finger goes along a line on a page, and when it had gone along one it went down to the next, and Gebre found his lips moving with it, as they moved when the teacher read.

Then the smoke rose.

It lifted off the floor all at once, the way a flock lifts, and climbed the pillars and gathered against the roof. The roof of Bet Maryam was hewn smooth and painted, ochre and red and white, with crosses in knots and beasts that walked in the corners, and the smoke spread among the painted crosses and did not stain them. It settled. It thinned. Where it thinned it left lines behind, black and fine as hair.

Letters.

Gebre was on his feet. The letters went across the hewn ceiling in rows, one under another, between the painted knots, and they were not letters he knew. They had the look of the ones he knew, as a grandfather has the look of a grandson, but they leaned and branched and hooked. And they came in a rhythm: a short sign, a short sign, a long, a short. He had held them in his hands.

Below, the black flame sank into what had been her, and grey ash began to settle where she had been, falling not in a heap but in lines. The yellow thread still ran along the wood’s underside, small and stubborn.

“Get the ladder,” Abba Talka said.

Gebre carried it out from behind the curtain past the black flame with his face turned away, and the cold of the flame stood against his cheek like a hand. He set the ladder’s feet beside the frost. Abba Talka held out his knife hilt first, a plain horn-handled knife, the one he cut bread with.

“Scrape it off,” he said. “Before the bell.”

Gebre climbed. At the top his head was among the painted beasts, and the smell was stronger there: old ink, old dust, and the oily sweetness of incense pooled under the roof. He set the knife to the end of the first row and drew it across.

The paint went. The tuff went, a little, in a fine red grit that fell in his eyes and on his tongue and tasted of the quarry. The letters went with it. He drew the blade back and the stone was bare and pale and clean for the length of three signs.

Then the letters were there again.

They came up through the scraped place the way water comes up into a footprint in wet ground, and filled their own shapes, and were black, and were exactly what they had been.

He scraped them again. They came back. He scraped faster, and the knife’s edge squealed on the stone, the only loud sound in the church, and each time he lifted the blade the bare place darkened from below and the signs stood up in it, unhurried, as if they had the whole night and he had none of it.

“Again,” said Abba Talka from the floor.

He went down her rows one by one, because there was nothing else to do, the first and the second and the third, scraping each to the bare stone and watching each fill again behind his blade. Through the high east window the dark had gone a shade thinner, the grey of ash and not of soot. The cold was coming up out of the floor now and through the ladder’s feet into his own, hardening as it did each night before the bell-keeper woke. He moved the ladder. By the time the knife was under the last row his breath was sobbing in his nose.

It was then, on the fifth stroke or the tenth, that he saw something moving below the last row of her letters, where there had been only painted ochre.

A line was growing.

It grew from the left, under the point of his knife, one sign and then the next, black out of nothing. These were letters he knew. He had learned them with a stick in the dust of the court; they were the first letters he had ever made mean anything. He watched the first take its shape. He watched the second set down its foot. He watched the third.

*Gebre.*

His own name, in the fire’s black, on the roof of the Mother’s house, under hers.

He made a noise in his throat and put the knife to it. The red grit sheeted down. His name went. He held his breath. The bare stone darkened from below, and the first sign came up through it, and then the second, and then the third.

He scraped. It came back. He scraped with both hands on the knife and his whole weight behind it, until the ladder walked on the floor and he clutched at a painted beast’s leg, and it came back. Far off, outside, across the court, a sandal scuffed on stone, and stopped, and did not come on. Out at the edges of the writing there were other short lines, two of them, that he was almost sure had not been there before, and he did not look at them. He looked at his name and scraped it, and it came back, and his arm began to shake from the shoulder down, a long shaking he could not stop, and still he scraped. His lips were moving. They shaped the three sounds each time the three signs stood up again, as they had moved at the teacher’s knee, and he could not make them stop either.

He had been written down.

The ash had been lifting from the floor all the while, a little at a time, in the cold breath of the black flame. Some of it had come up as high as the roof. A flake settled on his lip.

He tasted salt.

It was so strong and so clean that it stopped his arm. It was the salt of tears. He thought of the woman’s face on the stone and her eyes going to the boy, and he thought, she wept, she must have wept; and then he knew that she had not. He had been at her feet the whole time, and her face had been dry.

He wiped his mouth with his right hand, and his palm came away grey, and he climbed down.

On the floor he looked at his hand.

There were letters on it. A single line of them ran across the palm, from the root of the first finger toward the heel, black and fine, crossing the creases as if the creases were not there. He spat on it and rubbed it on the stone until the skin burned, and when he lifted his hand the line was whole, unblurred, not a stroke out of place. They were her letters, the leaning, branching kind, not his. He could not read one of them.

Behind him Abba Talka had a broom of twigs and the water jar from the court, and was not hurrying. The black flame had gone down to nothing. In the juniper’s place lay a bed of grey ash in rows, and beneath it, at the charred ends of the sticks, the last thin rind of yellow still crept. Abba Talka swept the rows into a cloth and tied it, and poured water after them across the stone, and the yellow rind hissed under the water and went out, the last ordinary flame in the church, and the frost with it, and the floor was only wet. He threw a fistful of frankincense on the censer, and the sweet smoke went up straight, as smoke should. He lit the candles before the Mother again from the tallow lamp, one and two, and the one in the niche, three, and they stood up yellow and did not lean.

The floor was clean. It was done before the bell. Gebre felt the relief go through him like sickness, and knew he would have to confess it, and knew he could not.

He looked up.

The ceiling was not clean. Her rows ran across it between the painted knots, and under them, in the letters he had learned in the dust, his name. He could not stop looking at it, and then at the rest, at her shapes, trying as he had tried as a child to make even one of them sound. And near the end of the last row, among signs he did not know, were three he did.

They were the plain letters of the psalter, like a word of his own tongue heard in a foreign market. He read them one sound at a time, his lips moving.

*Amara.*

He had not known her name. The pilgrims had not known it. The boy had not said it, and she had not.

Far off, over the hewn roofs and down into the trenches, the bell began.

Abba Talka stood in the doorway with the bundled cloth in his arms and the boy beside him on the sill. He did not look at the roof. He looked down at the boy’s fist, pressed white against the boy’s belly, and then across the wet floor at Gebre.

“What did she give him?”

Gebre looked at the boy’s fist, at the thread of cord that hung from it. Then he looked down at his own right palm, at the line of black letters he could not read.

“Nothing, Abba.”
