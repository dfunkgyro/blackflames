# CHAPTER 9

## ANGESKA

*San Francesco a Ripa — that night*

The chamberlain left her at the head of the stair with one lamp and a sentence. “His Eminence is at his office.” Then he went down again, and his slippers went away across the stone below, and Angeska stood listening to the part he had not said, which was *how long*.

She had until the bell for prime. Before that bell, while the house was still dark enough for a guard to be asleep on his step, she meant to be back up the dormitory stair to the north rooms with bread in her arms and a road in her sleeve, and to have paid for both no more than she had decided to pay on the walk across the cloister. Tamrat had not eaten in her sight for longer than she liked to count. Tomorrow at the ninth hour the fast would let him eat, and there would be nothing to eat, unless she came down this stair with it.

The antechamber was a passage that had been given a bench and a door and called a room. It smelled of cold plaster and of the olibanum that came under the door in a thin dry sweetness, and below that of the friars’ beans, hours old, rising from the refectory by the stair. A draught came up the stairwell steadily, the breath of the whole stone house going out through this one gap toward the roof. It moved the edge of her shawl. It moved the leather curtain on the door so that the curtain sighed against the jamb and fell back, sighed and fell back. The lamp stood on a stool beside the bench, a little clay thing with a spout and an oil-blackened lip, and its flame shrank in the draught and swelled and shrank again, and its smoke tore off the wick in rags.

Above the bench someone had scratched into the plaster, at the height of a seated man’s shoulder, a long column of short upright strokes in groups of five, each group crossed through: some petitioner’s days, counted while he waited at this door, and stopped. She read the strokes as she read everything, and found no name at the head of them and none at the foot. The count simply ended.

She did not sit at once. She stood in front of the door and looked at the grain of it, and found that her hands had folded themselves at her waist as they had folded for three hours that morning on the marble, and she unfolded them.

She was hungry. That was a fact, like the draught, and she set it down beside the other facts. The friars’ beans came up the stair. Her mouth ran, and she let it run, and swallowed. Hunger shortened a sentence. It made a woman agree a half-breath early. She would have to listen for that in herself. She listened for it in every man she rendered.

Then she sat on the bench beside the lamp and began, under her breath, to say the names.

She said them in the old tongue, in which the dead are spoken of as though they had gone ahead on a road and could still hear their names called after them. She did not move her lips more than she had to.

“Ashta.”

“Bihon.”

“Yemrut.”

“Sela.”

“Gadar.”

“Nehet.”

“Owara.”

“Kebet.”

She left a breath after each, the length of a breath and no more, as she had been taught to leave it. A name needs room on both sides of it. Her teacher had said that a name said quickly is a debt paid in bad coin.

“Hadera,” she said, and that was her teacher’s.

Somewhere before Hadera the lamp had stopped moving.

The draught had not stopped. She could feel it on her ankles and at the back of her neck; the curtain still sighed on the jamb and fell back. But the little flame stood upright on its wick as if set in a closed room, and did not shrink or swell, and the smoke went up from it in one thread, unbroken, straight to the black soot-stain on the plaster above: a single warp strung on the loom before any weft has touched it. She did not look away from it, and she did not look at it longer than at anything else.

She drew a breath and let it out. Then she said one more name, which was not a Warden’s.

“Desta.”

She left the breath after it, the same length. The thread of smoke stood. When she closed her mouth the draught came back into the flame all at once, and it shrank and fluttered and tore its smoke along the wall, and was an ordinary lamp in an ordinary passage again.

She sat with her hands open on her knees.

Below, the night office had begun in the choir; she heard the hebdomadary’s voice, thin through two walls, and the friars answering him like a tide coming in over flat sand, all at once and not quite together. She counted the psalms by their endings. On the fourth, the curtain was drawn back from the inside and the chamberlain stood there, with the smell of the brazier at his back.

“His Eminence will see you.”

The chamber was warm. That was the first thing, and she had not been warm since Naples. A brazier of charcoal stood in the middle of the floor on three iron feet, and the heat came off it in a wall she walked into, and her face, which had been cold for a month, began at once to ache. The second thing was the incense. He kept a dish of olibanum by the brazier and fed it himself, grain by grain, from the dish to the coals, and the smoke went up white and sweet and hung under the beams, and lay in the folds of the hangings, and in the wool of his gown.

Giovanni Orsini sat at a table with his back to a shuttered window. He had taken off the red. He wore a gown of dark wool lined with fox, open at the throat, and his hands lay on the table in front of him on two papers, one folded in three and one folded in six. On the table also stood a jug, and two cups, and a lamp of good brass with three spouts, all three lit. On a stool against the wall, by itself, was a small round loaf of dark bread with a rose pressed into its crust, and nothing else, set there to be seen.

She did not look at the loaf.

She had noted it as she noted a word missing from a line: by the space where it should have been.

He turned the letter round on the table with two fingers and pushed it to her side, and she read it herself, the right way up, by his three flames. Six lines. A small hurried hand that leaned forward as if it had somewhere to be. The wax at the foot bore a key standing in a ring.

She read what it said, and then she read what it did not say. It gave the order. It gave the reward. Between them, where a reason should have stood, it gave nothing. A clerk would have filled that space without thinking. The man who wrote this had left it empty, as a weaver leaves a gap in the warp for a pattern that will be put in later by another hand; and she knew what pattern went there, because she had been hungry on the road from Capua and had watched the faces of men who were hungrier. A hungry man notices bread. He notices the hand that holds it out. He remembers that hand longer than he remembers his own name.

She told the Cardinal so.

He wrote it there, at the table, on a half-sheet of the cheap paper she had used for her note, which he took from under Matteo’s collation; she saw him notice which paper it was, and use it anyway. His hand was quick and square, a lawyer’s hand, and it did nothing at all that she could see. It wrote *four loaves at first light* and *the north rooms* and *by the woman’s hand*, and under that *bread at noon and oil at evening, from the friars’ store, at my charge*, and his name; and he sanded it and folded it and held it up between two fingers for the chamberlain, who came out of the dark by the door where she had not known he stood, and took it, and read it there, and looked once at his master before he went.

Then the Cardinal took a small purse from the breast of his gown and pushed it across the table to her with the backs of his fingers, over Humbert’s letter, as a man pushes a counter across a board. It was leather, tied at the neck, the size of a child’s fist. She took it without looking at it and put it by feel into the flat purse at her girdle, and closed her hand over both. Oil, then, and a candle for the old man’s psalter, and a pair of shoes for the boy before Avignon. He laughed. It was short and surprised and it was the first time she had heard him do it, and with the laugh he leaned across the table toward her, and the incense came off his gown into the warm air between them, and into her shawl, and into her hair, and stayed there.

The night had gone on without them. Some time since, the office below had ended; she had heard its last bell go through the floor and had not turned her head to it. Lauds was sung. Between lauds and prime the house would sleep again, and the dark she had left was shorter than the dark she had spent. The charcoal had sunk to a red crust with grey ash on it, and the room was cooling from the corners inward. He had not fed the coals for some time. He sat back with his split hands flat on Humbert’s letter and Matteo’s sheet, one under each palm, holding them down in a room with no wind, and looked at her across the three flames for a long while without speaking. She let him look. She had been looked at by better men than this, and by worse, and she knew the difference between a man deciding whether to strike and a man deciding which question was the last one.

The bread was heavy. There were four loaves in a cloth, still warm at the heart from some oven that had been fired in the dark, and she carried them up the dormitory stair against her chest with both arms. The smell came up under her chin, crust and rye and the floor of an oven, and her belly turned over on itself. She had eaten nothing since before the hall. On the third step she found that her thumb had worked into the cloth and was resting on a crust, and she took it away, and went up the rest of the stair with her hands flat. The stair was black, and the bell had not rung. The guard on the north rooms had a stub of tallow on the step beside him and was asleep sitting up, with his chin in the red and silver of his collar, and he woke when her shadow went over him, and saw what she held, and looked at it a long moment. Then he got up without a word and drew the bar. It scraped in its staples the whole length of the door, and she went in under the sound of it, and heard it come down again behind her.

The outer room was hardly wider than a man lying down. It held a pallet, and a board on trestles under the one window, which was barred and gave onto the guards’ court; and through the bars came the first grey of the day and, low down, the last red of the watch-brazier by the gate, an eye nearly shut. In the corner a low door stood on the privy closet, and the closet breathed out the cold stink of the friary ditch. The boy lay on his pallet with his face to the wall and his blanket over his head. She stepped past him to the narrow doorway of the inner room. In the niche cut into its post, at the height of her hip, the night-lamp stood in its clay boat; they had been given water only, and the boat was dry, and the wick had burned down days ago to a black crust that smelled of nothing now but char. She went past it within a hand’s breadth, and through.

Tamrat lay on his mat against the far wall of the inner room with his knees drawn up. He was awake. He was always awake at this hour now; the fast had taken his sleep before it took his flesh. The grey from the barred window came through the doorway behind her and laid her shadow across him, and his eyes moved to it, and then to her arms.

She knelt by him and set the cloth down on the stone and opened it, and laid the loaves out on it one by one.

Then she bent over him and put her mouth to his ear. She kept it low, and she kept it there; her voice went from her lips into his ear and nowhere else. She told him in the order in which it had been said, as much of it as she had carried up the stair, and he listened with his face turned up to the beams. Halfway through, the bell for prime began over the roof of the choir, and she did not wait for it to finish but went on beneath it, and under the bell even her breath was not a sound. When she came to the bread he turned his head and looked at the loaves. The nearest lay with its stamped face upward, and the rose on it was clear and sharp in the crust, a bloom of five petals pressed into the dough by a wooden seal.

He did not reach for it.

“Whose rose?” he said, as low as she had spoken. His voice was a dry thread; he had not used it since the hall.

“His house’s. The Orsini.”

He looked at it a long time, at the distance where his eyes were good. “Then he has bought us,” he said, “for four loaves.”

“He has fed us. For four loaves.” She turned the nearest a little, so that the rose faced him. “It is not the hour. I know. It will keep until the ninth hour. I wanted it here before.”

He said nothing. His left hand lay open on the stone by his face. His right came up off his belly from under the cloak she had bought him at Naples, and found the left, and its thumb began to move in the hollow of that palm, slowly, round and round.

She drew the blanket down over his feet, which had come out of it in the night and were grey with cold, and kept her hand on his ankle a moment, the thin warm bone of it. She counted, while her hand was there, the hours to the ninth, and the paces from this mat to the dry lamp in the doorpost, and how many of the four loaves the boy would need to be made to eat before he would believe they were safe to eat. Then she leaned down over her husband again to tell him that she would wake him at the ninth hour.

His breath came up into her face.

Under the sourness of the fast, under the old-man smell of the gums and the stale wine of the Lenten water, it was not sickness. It was cold dust, and old hide, and ink gone brown.

She knew that smell.

She moved the bread an inch nearer to him, and rose, and went out.

The boy was awake.

He was sitting up on his pallet in the outer room, where she had stepped past him a little while before, with his knees drawn up and his blanket round his shoulders and his psalter shut on his knees. He had not been asleep then either. She saw that now: the blanket had been over his head so that he need not be seen to watch. Perhaps he had lain so since the guard let her out after compline. The grey came in through the bars of the window and lay across his feet in stripes, and the brazier’s eye in the court had gone out.

He had heard the bar, then. He had heard her feet go past him, and the cloth opened on the stone, and after that the bell; and under the bell, nothing.

He looked at her. Then she saw his nostrils move, once, and she knew what he had smelled, because she could smell it too now that she was out of the warm room and in the cold: olibanum, sweet and dry, in the wool of her shawl and in her hair. It had been put there in a warm room, slowly, over a long time. A boy who had lain awake since compline could count that time as well as she could.

“Where have you been?” Zoser said.

She showed him her hands, empty, with crumbs of crust in the creases.

“Finding us bread.”

He looked past her through the narrow doorway, to where the loaves lay on their cloth by the old man’s mat. He looked at her face, and she let him; she gave him her face as she had given it to the factor in Alexandria and to the Cardinal all night, attending, composed, a woman who had been out on her husband’s business and come back. She watched him read it as a child reads a line of a new tongue that he has only half learned: slowly, lips moving, and where the letters failed him, putting in the word he wanted to be there.

He wanted to believe her. It was in his hand. It came out of the blanket, palm up, and lay open on his knee in the grey light, the way Desta’s had come out every night on the road to whoever was beside him, full; this one was empty, and waited for her to put something into it.
