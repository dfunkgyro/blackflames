# CHAPTER 11

## ZOSER

*San Francesco a Ripa — Passion week, the second audience*

> MINUTE OF THE AUDIENCE BEFORE THE LORD GIOVANNI, CARDINAL DEACON, IN THE WEEK OF THE PASSION, 1326
>
> *In the year of the Lord 1326, in the week of the Passion, before the Lord Giovanni, Cardinal Deacon … there appeared: Tamrat, priest of the Ethiopians; Angeska, called his wife; Zoser, his servant and scribe; and the said Lord's notary Matteo, to render the Arabic.*

The blood would not stop.

It had begun on the dormitory stair, one dark drop on the stone that the old man looked down at with mild surprise, and it had not stopped since. It did not run. It seeped. Tamrat walked the cloister between the guards with a corner of his Naples cloak pressed under his nose, and every few paces he took the cloth away and looked at it at arm's length, where his eyes were good, and folded it to a clean place. The blood made small, neat squares on the brown wool, ruled one beside the next.

Zoser walked behind him and counted the squares, because it was something to count.

He knew what he wanted from the next hour: to come out of the hall with three people, back up the stair to the north rooms and the bread with the rose on it. It was not much to want. In Alexandria he had wanted more. He had wanted, God forgive him, to be the one who could read what no one else could read.

Under his tunic the strip lay against his skin, across the little scar below his left collarbone. Every dawn since the search he had read the line again under his breath, and meant to sew it back into the lining, and had not.

The guard in front knocked at the refectory door with the butt of his staff. Through the wood came the smell of the hall: cold stone, cold wax, and under them the sweet dry smoke the Cardinal burned in his own rooms, which Zoser had smelled once before, in the wool of Angeska's shawl at dawn.

She walked beside him with her hair braided tight and covered and her eyes on the old man's back. She had not spoken since the summons.

"His nose," Zoser said, low, in Amharic.

"I know."

"It was the cold of the stair."

"Yes," she said, and the door opened.

⁂

They had moved the candles.

At the first audience the eight stands had flanked the Cardinal's chair. Now the chair stood on its step with nothing beside it but the cold, and the candles had come down the hall. They stood on the marble in two rows a long stride apart, making a lane the width of three people shoulder to shoulder, and at the end of the lane, before the step, a strip of red cloth lay on the stone. That was where they were to stand. There was no other way to it.

The candles were beeswax, a forearm tall, on iron stands chest-high. They burned straight and filled the hall with hot honey and hot wick, a feast's smell in a cold room.

On the long wall, where the friars' Christ broke bread with His twelve, a hanging of plain purple cloth fell from the beams, and the frame of the painting pushed through it at the corners. The Latins hid their images in the last two weeks of their fast. Beside the step an empty cage hung from an iron hook with its door open.

Tamrat had stopped on the threshold. His shoulders had come up under the cloak, as they had on the deck when the captain lit his candle, and his free hand had gone to his belly and spread there. At that distance he could see every wick.

"Go on," said the guard behind him, in the Roman speech, and put two fingers to the small of his back.

Tamrat went on. He gathered his cloak into his fist so that no fold of it swung, and walked in the exact middle of the lane. It was three people wide. He made himself the width of one. The candles burned straight on either side of him.

Then it was Zoser's turn, and he found that he was afraid of the same thing.

*He died because the light wanted me.* He had thought it in the north rooms with the night-lamp bending at the doorway and the ink stirring on his knee, and he had believed it every hour since. Now he stood at the head of a lane of eight flames with his mother's letters against his skin, unsewn, read aloud every dawn; and in the doorway a hand's breadth had been enough.

He measured it; he had ruled too many pages not to. Half a stride from the middle of the lane to either flame. More than a hand. He walked with his eyes on the cloth and did not breathe for the length of the lane.

Nothing leaned.

He stood on the red cloth at the old man's left and let his breath go, and felt the sweat cold under his arms. Angeska came last and stood at Tamrat's right. The candles burned upright behind them and threw their three shadows forward up the marble toward the empty chair, long and pale and side by side.

⁂

There was a desk beside him.

A writing-slope on a trestle, a pace from his left hand, inside the light; and on the stool behind it, already sitting, the notary. He had come down from his place below the step and brought his pens, the reed behind his left ear and the goose quill behind his right. His skin had the colour and grain of a fig dried on a roof. He smelled of gall-ink and of the fennel seed he was chewing with his front teeth.

On the slope lay a sheet of vellum pinned with four lead weights. The Latins' small upright book-hand: he could not read a word. But he could read a page without its words. Eight or nine lines of a head stood at the top, and below them the vellum had been ruled with a dry point and waited. He had kept the abbot's chapter minutes at Lalibela for three years; he knew the shape of a minute. And the ink of those lines was dull. It had sunk into the vellum and gone flat. Gall-ink goes so when it has stood a night.

Nobody in the hall had said a word yet. The Cardinal was not in his chair.

The notary saw him looking.

"You are the scribe," he said, in Arabic.

It was not the Arabic of Alexandria or of the imam's table in Cairo. It was older and drier, every word with all its letters in it.

"I am the priest's scribe. Yes."

"Matteo. Of Mazara, in Sicily." He looked at the ink ground into the side of Zoser's middle finger, and seemed to read it. "His Eminence will speak Latin. I will put it into Arabic, which the priest has, and you. The woman"—he did not look at her—"will render nothing today."

"Why?"

"Because His Eminence has said so." The jaw went round on the fennel. "So I sit where the Arabic is, and nothing is lost on the way across. Speak slowly. My Arabic is older than yours, and it comes from an island. I would not like to mistake you."

He laid the reed on the slope exactly parallel to its edge, and the quill beside it.

Zoser could not tell whether he had been given a courtesy or shown a trap. A man who sits beside you in your own tongue, near enough to hear your breath change, is not there to carry anything.

"Is it the custom," he said, "for the notary to sit with the witnesses?"

"No," said Matteo of Mazara, and turned the quill a hair's breadth so that it lay truer.

⁂

The Cardinal came in from the cloister with the cold on him, up along the wall, not down the lane, pulling off his gloves, and Zoser watched his hands come out of them: split at the knuckles with frost, flour in the creases of the right palm, a blue stone turned inward on the third finger. There was rye on him under the sweet smoke.

He sat, and the chamberlain put a cup into his hand, and he drank. Then he looked down the hall at Angeska, first and longest. It was not a judge's look. Zoser had seen factors at Alexandria look so at a bale already paid for, to see whether it had been wet in the hold.

He spoke in Latin, not loud.

"His Eminence greets the envoys of the King of the Ethiopians in the week of the Passion of our Lord," said Matteo in Arabic, close on the Latin's heels. "The notary will render, the woman being a party to the matter before him. Let the head of the minute be read."

He put his finger on the dry lines and read them out in Latin, fast, in a mutter, and Zoser understood nothing of it but the names: *Tamrat*; *Angeska*; and his own, *Zoser*, flat, the stress on the wrong half. He had never heard his name in a Latin mouth. It sounded like the name of a man he did not know.

The Cardinal drew from his right sleeve a paper folded in three, its folds furred with handling, and smoothed it on his knee with his floury palm, and spoke.

"His Eminence has before him the collation of the King's letter," said Matteo. "The King's words, from the Arabic copy; and the words of the woman, who rendered the letter into Latin in this hall and affirmed that her rendering was the whole of it, word for word, as near as Latin will carry it. Which affirmation is in the minute. His Eminence will have the second row read, in Arabic, so that the envoys hear it."

He did not need the sheet. He had made it. He shut his eyes.

"The King's words: *And if the Sultan does not cease to afflict our brothers, we will turn the river from Egypt, and his land will thirst.*" He opened them. "The woman's words, which I put back into Arabic as near as Arabic will carry them: *The waters of our country go down to Egypt each year, a gift of God to all peoples.*"

One of the candles behind them ticked, a little spit of wax.

Zoser did not look at Angeska. He looked at the old man.

Tamrat had carried that letter from the mountains against his chest. On this floor he had listened to his wife give it to the Latins in Latin, which he did not have, and inclined his head to the long rising sound of it. Zoser watched the knowledge go into him, in at the face and down. The cloth came away from his nose, and a thread of blood ran into the white of his beard, and he did not feel it. He did not turn to his wife. He looked at the air above the Cardinal's chair, a long way off, where his eyes were good.

The Cardinal spoke again, in the reasonable voice of a man laying out a matter for a clerk.

"The other rows are of the same kind," said Matteo. "A grievance made a greeting. A command made a courtesy. Hostages made into perils. A threat made into a hymn." The fennel had stopped. "His Eminence has sat as an auditor of causes. He knows a rendering that blurs from a rendering that has been made. This one has been made. If the woman made it of herself, she has falsified, under minute, a Christian king's letter to the Apostolic See: a forgery of the Holy See's own record, which the canons answer with excommunication. Then the city's judges will ask what else a foreign woman's seven tongues can do, and if they find sorcery in it they give her to the secular arm. And Rome in Lent, with no Pope, will ask for fire in the Campo." He did not change his voice for any of it. "His Eminence asks: who ordered this?"

⁂

The word came to Zoser in the notary's island Arabic, *nar*, fire, and a picture came with it that he had carried fifteen years without looking at: a woman's hair going up all at once like a page held to a lamp. He shut it fast, with the flat of his hand.

Two roads, as on the plank.

He could say nothing. In a moment Matteo would put the question to the old man, and Zoser knew the answer. Tamrat son of Habta Maryam had told every table from Lalibela to Cairo how he confounded the Greeks of Constantinople, and had stopped dead in a sermon at the word *bronze*, and begun the sentence again as a parable about somebody else. He would not tell a Latin prince, *I made my wife sweeten my King's letter, because I was afraid.* He would say the truth, with his staff up and the blood in his beard: *She did it. I did not know.* And they would take her to the Campo.

Or he could speak first, and give them a man to hold instead of a woman to burn: a coward, perhaps, a fool who had softened his master's threat for fear of a Latin frown, but no forger. Envoys were not burned for cowardice. They were sent home with it. Zoser did not know that. He believed it because the other thing could not be borne.

And it would be a lie told against the old man. On the plank he had lied for him, and even under the spite it had been a gift. This would put into a Cardinal's minute, for ever, that Tamrat of Lalibela had been afraid: before the Latins, what the Greeks had made him on their step, a large voice with nothing in its hands.

And under that, small and hard, in the place he did not show confessors, something lifted its head and tasted the air. *Wet clay.* Let the clay say, for once, what the potter was.

He hated it. It warmed him all the same.

Matteo had begun to turn on his stool, and the fennel came with him, green and sharp across the cold.

"My master ordered the softening," Zoser said.

He said it in Arabic, to the notary, low and clear and slow, as he had been told. His voice did not shake. It never had. Matteo's head stopped in its turn and came back, and the dark eyes rested on him and read him.

"Again," said Matteo. "Slowly."

"My master ordered it. The priest Tamrat. He had the letter read to him in Ge'ez before the audience, and he was afraid how it would sound to the Pope's men. He told her to make it soft. She did what her husband told her. She is his wife."

The last was true, at least. He set it at the end, where it would be heard.

Matteo turned to the step and spoke in Latin, every word laid down separately; and in the run of it Zoser heard *Tamrat*, and *presbyter*, and a word like *iussit* that he did not know and knew exactly, because he had made it. The Cardinal's face did not move. Only his eyes went, for a breath, to Angeska, and came back.

He said something short.

"His Eminence says the priest will answer for himself." Matteo turned on his stool all the way this time and looked up past Zoser's shoulder. And he did not put it in the Cardinal's Latin. He put it in his own slow Arabic, plainly.

"Is this true, father?"

⁂

Zoser did not look at Tamrat. He could not. He looked at the floor, at the red cloth and their three shadows lying up the marble toward the step; and at the edge of his eye, where he could not stop it, he saw the old man's hands.

The left had opened at his side. The right had let go of the cloak and come across, and its thumb had gone into the hollow of the left palm and begun to move there. Round, and round. Pressing.

Zoser had seen that thumb in the widow's hostel, after the sermon, while she went to stir the beans. He had seen it in the rushes under Saint Mark's lamp, when the old man knelt at the east wall and prayed without sound. He had seen it in this hall, while Angeska answered the Cardinal's question for her husband. It was not prayer. It was a man rubbing at a mark that would not come off.

Round, and round.

The blood had soaked through the fold of the cloak under his nose; Zoser could smell it from where he stood, iron and wet wool, under the honey of the wax.

The old man was not looking at Zoser, who had just sold him, or at the Cardinal, or at the notary who had asked. For the first time since the second row was read, he had turned his head to his wife.

Angeska did not look back. She stood with her hands folded and her eyes on the marble before her feet. Zoser could see only the side of her face. It was perfectly still. It did not ask anybody for anything.

Tamrat looked at her a long time. She was too close for his eyes. Zoser wondered what he saw: a shape in a dark shawl, who had made his King's threat into a psalm and let him nod at it.

The thumb stopped.

"Yes," said Tamrat.

And every candle in the hall leaned toward him.

Zoser saw it on the floor first, because he was looking at the floor. The three shadows that lay side by side up the marble swung, all of them, a little, at once. He looked up.

The eight flames had turned. Each bright tongue had tipped from the upright and come round on its wick, and pointed, and held. There was no draught, and they did not flutter. They stood bent as a scribe's head bends over a line he has not finished, all eight points turned to Tamrat. And at the root of each, where every flame keeps a barleycorn of blue under the wick, the blue woke and climbed, until what had been yellow was only a thin gilt edge round it, the gilding round a painted page.

The heat came onto the back of Zoser's neck, and the smell of hot wax rolled over him, thick, honey and smoke.

*Him*, Zoser thought, stupidly. *Not me.*

The old man had not seen; the light was behind him and beside him. His *yes* had not been the Arabic of his sermons. There was no pulpit in it. It was the Arabic a man uses to a carter about the price of a load.

"I ordered it." He said it into the bent light without knowing it was bent. "Write that I ordered it."

Matteo's hand had gone to the quill, and stopped on it. He was not looking at the old man. He was looking past him, at the candle nearest his desk, and the fennel had stopped in his jaw.

Tamrat did not see that either. He bent his head a little toward Zoser, as he bent it at the altar to give the deacon his part, and spoke in Ge'ez, very low, in the tongue nobody in that hall had but the three of them.

"Now you have lied for me twice." He put the cloth back under his nose, and above it his eyes were red and dry and almost amused, lit blue from both sides. "Do not make it a habit, it is my office."

Then he made a sound in his nose. The blood was not seeping now. It ran from under the dark cloth, over his knuckles and down his wrist, and dripped from his elbow onto the red cloth on the floor, where it did not show.

⁂

Somebody by the door said a word in the Roman speech, and then nobody said anything.

The light was all on him, eight flames bent to one man, so that he stood lit from every side at once, his beard white where it was not wet and black where it was. A man so lit throws no long shadow; the pillars of Bet Medhani Alem at noon on a feast stand in their own small pools. The old man's shadow should have drawn in close round his sandals and lain there.

It did not.

It went out from him across the marble toward the candles, toward the light, against the light, as water runs uphill in a dream: a dark tongue sliding unhurried over the grey stone to the nearest stand on the left of the lane. It went up the iron shaft, darker than the dark metal, past the drip-pan, to the cup where the candle stood bent and blue; and there it hung, folded over the rim. The flame burned on beside it, bent toward the old man, and did not light it.

Then it came back.

It came down the stand and over the marble to the old man's feet, and did not stop there. It went up him, against the fall of the light: ink climbing a thread laid in the inkpot. His cracked heels. His knees. His belly, where his free hand lay spread. His fist with the cloth in it. His beard.

His face.

It came up over his face as the purple had come down over the friars' Christ, and when it had covered it, it was a veil.

Tamrat stood up.

He had been standing already. He stood up now inside himself. The bend went out of his back that had been in it since the Afar escarpment, and he was taller than Zoser had ever seen him, and still. The veil lay over his face from brow to beard, close as wet linen, and through it, where his eyes were, two lights came up slowly from behind: gold, the gold of metal in the crucible, liquid, moving, too bright for the face they were in.

And across the veil there were letters.

They were scars, or they were writing; on that dark linen they were the same thing. They stood up from it in pale ridges, and they moved: came up and ran together and parted and sank and rose elsewhere, too fast to read. Zoser knew them. Not their sense: their shapes. He had sat over those shapes every dawn since the search. Two strokes and a hook surfaced at the corner of the mouth, and sank. A roof, and a small man standing under it, ran into a cord tied in two knots and came apart from it again. And across the brow, held there longer than any of the others, one stroke rising from a short bar and parting into three, the middle tallest: the sign he had sat over longest and at last chosen to read as *burn*. Then three others came up in its place and went down again, fish turning in brown water.

The smell changed. Over the wax and the blood came something cold and sweet and grave: a church's incense, but not the Cardinal's dry sweet smoke, and not the frankincense of home that lived in the beams of Bet Maryam, warm resin over honey and old soot. This was colder, with something green under it, crushed leaves, and under that the dust that comes off cut stone. It was the smell of a church he had never been in.

Behind him a guard dropped his staff. It rang on the marble and rolled.

The thing in Tamrat's body turned its veiled head and looked at Zoser.

⁂

It spoke in Desta's voice.

"I was never good at counting."

Amharic, low and dry and level, with the little drop at the end where the joke would be if he had let it be one. Zoser's knees went, and locked, and held. Not a man doing Desta's voice. Desta: the rasp at the back of it, the way he let a sentence leave his mouth and did not watch it go.

Under the veil the old man's lips had moved.

Then, before he could breathe:

"I am no better at it than you."

It was his own voice: a little hoarse, pleased with itself and ashamed of being pleased. Said low, almost not said, to the dark under the eaves of a fish-loft in Naples, with the nets dripping and twelve grains of barley and seven of chickpea in rows on his knee. He had said it to Desta, three weeks dead, with nobody near but the old man asleep on the nets an arm's length away. He had never said it again, not to Angeska, not to God. It was the one thing he owned that no one had ever heard.

It had been heard, and kept, and given back to him out of the mouth of the man who had been asleep.

He understood it then, all at once. Every tender thing said within an arm's length of the old man since the quay at Alexandria. Desta at the plank. Whatever Angeska had said to him at night in the hold, where Zoser could not hear. The ford. The loft. *Clay*— His mind stopped on the word and would not go past it. Something in the priest had been listening, and it had kept everything, and it was not done.

The veiled head turned on the old man's neck, slowly, to the step, to the man in red with a paper folded in three on his knee. And in a voice that was not Desta's or Zoser's or the old man's, a deeper voice, smooth and Roman and patient, it said in Latin:

"*Estne uxor tua, Pater, an peccatum tuum?*"

The Cardinal's voice.

Zoser had heard those sounds once before, in this hall, and kept them by their sound, without their sense. Now he understood two words. *Pater*: father. And *peccatum*. *Agnus Dei, qui tollis peccata mundi.* The dying friar had shown him at last, with his hands, what *peccata* were: he had laid them on Zoser's shoulders, one, two, three, a weight, and lifted them off and set them on his own. Sins.

At the first audience Angeska had given her husband the Cardinal's question in Amharic, mildly, and Zoser had heard what she gave: *He asks whether I am your wife.* The old man had answered, content, with his hand on his heart. There had been a second half. She had carried the first half across and left the other lying on the marble.

*Is she your wife, Father, or your sin?*

The Cardinal had gone white, the white of the flour in the creases of his hand. He had half risen with one hand on the arm of his chair, and the folded paper had slid off his knee onto the step. His mouth was open, and he did not close it. It had his voice. Zoser thought it must be the voice the man heard inside his own head when he chose his words, and that he had never before heard it come at him from outside.

And Zoser looked at Angeska.

She had lifted her eyes. Not to the veil: to the Cardinal, who had turned his white face from the veil to her. Zoser saw her hear it, the half she had left on the floor picked up and said aloud in the voice of the man who had asked it, before the guards and the notary and the minute. Nothing in her face moved. But the colour went out of her lips, and her folded hands pressed together once, hard, so that the knuckles stood up; and Zoser, who had watched her give the factor at Alexandria a face, and the Cardinal a face, and himself a face, saw that for the space of that breath she had no face to give anyone.

The veil turned back to the old man's own body and looked down at it: the brown cloak, the blood on the wrist, the hand spread over the belly. Then it spoke in the old man's voice. Not his pulpit voice: his voice in the middle of the ford at Cassino, with the stream roaring and his belly pressed to the back of Zoser's neck, strangled, through the teeth, each word drawn out of him as if with tongs.

"Clay," it said, "carrying the potter."

It patted the belly, twice, gently.

At the ford Zoser had laughed without breath, and nearly lost his feet in the black water. It was the only joke the old man had ever made against himself. Now the thing in him had taken it, and turned it round, and was wearing it. The words were not about the clay any more.

⁂

Then the veiled head came round to Zoser, and came close.

The gold was a finger's width from his eyes, and the cold green church-smell was in his mouth, so that he tasted it. It did not speak into the hall. It spoke into the little space between its mouth and his face, so low that it was hardly a sound at all: a weight on his ears from both sides, the weight that comes on them going down the cut steps into the rock churches at home.

It was Latin; but not the Cardinal's, and not the dying friar's. This Latin opened its vowels wide and sang them, even breathed.

"*Custodes*—"

He knew the word. It had come through the doorway on the night of the search, out of the old man's sleeping mouth, and he had taken it for the guards' broken Latin of the city. It was not the guards'. The guards, and the friar before them, struck the sound at the head of that word hard, a pen-knife's click against the teeth. This mouth breathed it away, so that the word came out of the dark on a sigh; and nobody in Rome whom Zoser had heard ever said it so.

It went on, quick and low. *Nomina*: he had that, *in nomine Patris*, with the sign of the cross. *Nomina eorum*: their names. Then a word he did not have, with a fall in it, a lid coming down.

The *custodes*, whoever they were. Their names, and something shut down over them.

At the edge of his eye Matteo had lifted the quill and was holding it over the vellum, his head turned toward them, waiting for a word to write. None came to him. By the door a Roman guard was saying the same few words of his own speech over and over, fast and low. To the hall, Zoser thought, it must be nothing but a hiss behind a veil.

The gold eyes looked down, at his chest, at the place where his mother's letters lay.

The old man's hand came up, blood dried brown in the creases of its knuckles, forty years' ink grey in the side of the finger. It stopped a finger's width from his breastbone and pointed at the place. And the weight on his ears grew, pressing in from both sides until something rang inside his own head; and nobody else in the hall so much as turned.

"*Tu*—"

*Tu* he had: the friar said it to God every morning, *tu solus sanctus*. You. The rest went by him. He had only the finger, and where it pointed: the place where every dawn he had said aloud what his mother had sewn shut so that no priest would see. His own heart gave him the rest, in Amharic, before he could stop it. *You dared.* He knew what he had dared. Every dawn, under his breath.

And every candle in the hall swung.

All at once. The eight flames that had stood bent toward the old man since he said *yes* came round together in one movement and pointed at Zoser. At his chest. They held, their blue tips toward him from either side of the lane, and the light that had been all on the old man was all on him, the heat of eight flames on his face and his hands and the side of his neck. Under his tunic the strip had grown warm, the warmth of a hand laid flat on him.

He did not look down.

He knew what the old man's shadow had done. He thought of the floor round his own feet, and what might be lying there, or going out from him toward the light; and he could not make his eyes go to it. He looked at the gold in the veil. It was easier.

Behind him somebody was praying in the Roman speech, very fast. Somebody else was being sick.

The veiled face did not draw back. It came nearer, until the lips under the dark linen were almost on his own, and he felt the shapes of the words against his mouth before he heard them. The breath that carried them was cold.

"*Excommunicatus es.*"

The hard sound in the middle went out on a breath, like the other. But that word he had. The friar had said it once, gently, between coughs, of Zoser's own church and its Patriarch in Egypt; and when Zoser asked what it meant, the friar had closed the cell door between them, softly, and opened it again. Shut out.

"*Non ab Ecclesia*—"

Not from the Church. Every Latin had *Ecclesia* in his mouth twenty times a day.

"—*sed ab ipso ordine creaturae.*"

The last word he took by its root, as he took a hard Ge'ez word by the three letters under it. *Quis te creavit?* The friar's first question: who made you? *Deus me creavit*, and the friar's thin finger pointing up through the cell roof and then down at Zoser's chest, at the very place the old man's finger had pointed.

Not from the Church, then. From what God made: so he read it, and could not tell how much of the reading was his own fear. He knew only that something had been done, and had taken. Something had shut behind him, softly, as the friar's door had shut.

A pace away, Matteo's quill still hung over the vellum with nothing on it.

The old man's hand went down and spread flat on his own belly, over the place that same hand had guarded on the deck and in the hold and on every road from Naples, and pressed there. Then it lifted and turned over and held itself out toward Zoser, palm up and empty, as Desta's had come out sideways every night on the road, full.

"*Portabis*," the cold mouth breathed against his, "*quod portavi.*"

Two words with one root under both, he could hear that much: once turned toward him, once turned back. The hand on the belly; the hand held out empty. Whatever the old man had kept behind that hand was being given across. That was how Zoser read it. He had no other way to read it.

⁂

Then it went.

The gold went out of the eyes from the edges in, a coal greying when the bellows stop, and the letters slowed and lay down and were only ridges on a dark cloth, and then not even that; and the veil was a shadow on an old man's face, and then it was on the floor, where it belonged, lying round his sandals in a small close pool. The candles stood upright, all eight, yellow to the tip, smoking a little.

The incense was gone. There was wax, and blood, and somebody's vomit, sour, by the door.

Tamrat swayed.

The stoop had come back into him all at once: an old man of sixty-four with a nosebleed, who had stood too long on a cold floor, blinking at the step.

Two guards were coming down the lane with their staves held crosswise, their faces the colour of tallow. The first was a boy younger than Zoser, the rose stitched at his shoulder, his lips moving. He came up on the old man's left, and his hobnailed shoe came down square and heavy on the small dark pool by the old man's sandal.

On the shadow.

Tamrat flinched.

It went through the whole of him, a jerk and a hiss. The boy, who had touched nothing but the floor, leapt back and crossed himself.

The old man's knees went.

Zoser caught him. The weight came down into his arms, sticks in a wet cloak, lighter than at the ford, and he went down with it onto the marble and held him there, half in his lap, the white head against his chest. Against the strip. He felt it under the old man's cheek and did not move.

Tamrat's eyes opened. He looked past Zoser at the beams, far off, where he could see them well, and frowned the frown he wore when a deacon lost his place in the hymn.

"Did he write it?" he said in Amharic. His voice was thin and puzzled and ordinary. "Zoser. I told him to write it. Has he written it?"

He did not know. For him, he had said *yes* to a Sicilian notary a moment since, and lain down, and that was all.

"He has written it, Qess."

"Good." The eyes closed. "Good."

All round the hall men were crossing themselves: the guards; the boy, three times, four; Matteo on his stool, once, small and exact. Zoser's own hand had made the sign over the old man's head before he knew it had moved.

Angeska stood where she had stood, her hands hanging unfolded at her sides. Zoser looked up at her from the floor with her husband's blood on his shamma, and waited for her hand to go to her brow.

It did not move.

⁂

On the step the Cardinal had sat down again, the paper picked up from the floor and held in his lap in both hands. His face was still white. He looked down the lane of upright candles at the three of them, and his lips moved once before any sound came, trying the words for weight. Then he spoke in Latin, quietly, to the captain of his guard.

Zoser heard only the sound. *Parate*—

Beside him Matteo said in Arabic, after his master, in the dry voice he had used all morning, because the words had to go across to someone and he was the one sitting where they could go:

"His Eminence says: Prepare the stakes."

Then the guards had them.

They took Angeska first, a hand on each arm; she went without being pulled. They lifted the old man out of Zoser's lap and stood him between two of them, his feet trailing, one small dark drop from his nose on the marble and then another. They put Zoser on his feet and held him by the notary's desk, near enough to the door that the sour smell of the sick came to him there, and under it, closer, the gall-ink in the horn at Matteo's elbow, sharp as split green walnuts.

He did not look at the floor. He looked at the desk.

Matteo had taken up the goose quill and dipped it. His hand was steady, or nearly. He said the words under his breath as he wrote them, a clerk's habit, and Zoser watched the quill go along the ruled line under the dry head, the fresh ink lying black and wet behind it, and the mutter going with it: something long; and *Tamrat*; and *presbyterum*; and *Angeskam*; and *et*—

"*Zoser*," said Matteo, under his breath, and set the pen to the vellum.

The ink did not take.

It touched the vellum and drew itself up into a bead, round and black and shining, and would not lie down. The slope of the desk was gentle. The bead went down it all the same, leaving nothing behind it, and dropped off the foot of the sheet.

Matteo wiped the nib on the rag at his wrist and dipped and wrote the name again, a little to the right, with care. It beaded. It slid. It fell. He made a small sound with his tongue and said in Arabic, to the desk, "Bad skin. Campagna calf. They never pumice it." He wrote it a third time, pressing, the nib splaying under his hand; and the ink gathered and stood and ran, and went off the edge after the others.

The captain said something sharp. On his step the Cardinal said nothing at all.

Matteo looked at the three clean places where a name had been written and was not. Then he looked up at Zoser, and for the first time that morning there was something in the dried-fig face that Zoser could read, and did not want to.

The notary wiped the quill on his rag until there was no ink left in the slit at all. Then he set the dry nib to the vellum and wrote the name a fourth time, hard, with nothing in the pen: a furrow scored into the vellum and holding nothing. And he went on to the end of the line.
