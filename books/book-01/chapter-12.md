# CHAPTER 12

## ABBA TALKA

*Aksum — Hosanna, 1326*

The azaj wanted a lamp lit in daylight. So a deacon of Zion stood in the full sun of Hosanna morning with a clay lamp burning between his two hands and held it before the blind man’s eyes, and the whole court pressed in to watch.

“Closer,” said the azaj from his stool. “So the court may see he does not see.”

The deacon brought it closer. Abba Talka stood at the head of the litter with his hand-cross against his chest and watched the flame, not the eyes.

It was a small flame in a great deal of light. The sun stood a hand above the precinct wall. The frost had gone off the flagstones an hour since, and the air was so still that the dung smoke from the town went up beyond the wall in grey threads and stayed there. The lamp burned yellow in that stillness, smoked a little at the tip from the cheap oil, and did nothing else. Talka had watched every flame he passed for fifteen years. He watched this one until his eyes ran, and it stood upright on its wick, a lamp in a frightened deacon’s hands near a sick old man who carried nothing but his fever.

He let his breath out into his beard.

The guardian’s eyes did not follow it. The deacon moved the lamp to the left, and the eyes stayed where they were, filmed and pale, fixed on the church door or on the sky above the church door. The deacon moved it to the right. He brought it so near that the heat must have touched the lashes, and the lids did not close.

A sound went round the court. It was not a word. It was breath let out of many mouths together, and under it the dry clink of a sistrum that someone had forgotten to hold still.

The court was full for Hosanna. Townsmen stood in their white shammas with green fronds over their shoulders, waiting for the procession. Boys sat on the church steps weaving the young palm into rings and crosses, their fingers quick and stained green, and handed them up to anyone who would take them. Deacons stood with sistra at their belts and their chins on their prayer-staffs. On the top step the priests of Zion waited in their copes, a row of old faces under white turbans, and not one of them looked at the litter. At the gate the azaj’s spearmen held the horses, and the horses stamped and blew and smelled of the road.

The guardian smiled toward the sound. There were palm rings in his lap, five or six of them; the boys had pressed them on him as he was carried out, and he turned one now in his long fingers, feeling for the place where the weave began.

“Enough,” said the azaj. The deacon pinched out the lamp and backed away with it.

The King’s officer was a lean grey man in a red-bordered shamma over quilted cotton, with the dust of the plateau on the boots he had not taken off to stand in the court of Zion. He sat with a riding whip across his knees, and since he rode in at dusk he had told everyone who came near him that his horses would be saddled at first light.

“Now,” he said. “Before the palms go round the church, I will have it written. What was done to the chest, and by whose hand. I do not carry a story to the King of Kings, Abba. I carry a finding, or I carry a man.” He turned the whip over on his knees. “The house says you will speak for it.”

The eldest of Zion’s priests had come to the lodging before cockcrow and asked it of him, his breath smoking in the doorway, and had not quite said why: that a priest of Lalibela might put his name where no priest of Zion would put his own.

“The house is generous with my mouth,” Talka said.

“Then use it. Is anything wanting?”

On the top step the old faces studied the stones.

Talka had not seen the chest. No one went in past the last curtain of the sanctum but its guardian, and the guardian had gone in alone every morning of the rains and every morning of the dry season after them with his hand on the wall. Talka had it from the eldest, who had it from the witnesses of Gena, twelve days before Timkat, who had seen it by a lamp the guardian could not see. The lock the old man’s fingers could not find. The cloths for the procession folded wrong, the gold edge turned in. And under the cloths, in the place where it had lain since before any keeper’s memory, a wrapping of brown linen lying open, and in the linen a hollow pressed the length of a man’s finger, and nothing in the hollow.

“Who came to the house,” said the azaj, “between one Nativity and the next? I am told the King’s own embassy prayed here, before the rains, on its road to the sea.”

The sun had found the back of Talka’s neck, and the cold of the stones came up through his sandals, and he knew the hour by both.

There were two ways down from where he stood. He had walked each to the bottom in the dark, lying on his mat by the guardian’s bed, and neither had grown easier.

If he said a thing was wanting, the scribe would write it and the azaj would carry it, and before the new grass the King would send after his own embassy. Talka had stood in the water at Timkat with his cross on Tasfa Mikael’s forehead and said that embassy was in Rome. Rome was a year’s road. Letters would go after it, a year out and a year back, and all that while the King’s letter would lie in the Franks’ hands as a letter carried by thieves. Whatever swords the coughing friar had promised at Ifat’s back would stay in their scabbards. And the court’s men would come south to Lalibela, because that was where the embassy had been chosen, and they would ask who chose it, and who chose the scribe, and why. The chaplains in the King’s tent had laughed once at a Frank who asked whether the monks of Lalibela burned women. They would not laugh at the King’s riders.

And the boy— He did not finish that, even to himself.

If he said that nothing was wanting, the chest still lay disturbed before witnesses, and a disturbed chest must have a cause. There was one to hand. It sat in the litter with palm rings in its lap.

He looked at it.

Fifty years ago and more, this man had knelt beside him on the cold floor of the novices’ house at Lalibela, learning the night office by one lamp, putting an elbow in his ribs whenever he nodded. He had gone north to Zion at twenty with his whole life tied in one cloth, and had come south since only twice, for consecrations, and stood both times at the monastery gate with his arms out, laughing. The roll of the keepers of Zion was read aloud in the church every year at the feast of Zion in Hidar, from the first keeper down to the living one, and the living one stood and answered to it. This one had answered to it for more than thirty years.

The guardian hummed. Talka knew the tune: the Hosanna psalm, the one the children sang, and the old man was a beat behind the deacons who were practising it by the wall.

“Abba,” said the azaj.

“Bring the scribe,” said Talka.

The scribe was a deacon of the house, younger than his beard wanted to be, with ink in the creases of his knuckles. He knelt on a mat beside the litter, set a board across his knees and a leaf of vellum on the board, and cut his reed fresh while the court watched. At dawn Talka had set a broken pot of coals by the litter’s foot, with rue on the coals and a split stick of olive-wood, against the fever, and the smoke came up between them green and bitter and went in at the nose and stayed. The guardian turned his face toward it. He liked it. He had liked it as a boy, when the old brothers burned rue in the infirmary, and had held his head in it until he sneezed.

Talka gave the words in the tongue of the church, slowly, a clause at a time, and the reed squeaked after him across the skin.

> FINDING OF THE HOUSE OF ZION, IN THE DAYS OF HOSANNA

> In the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, one God. In the year of mercy ፲፫፻፲፰, in the days of Hosanna, before the azaj of the King of Kings Amda Seyon, the elders of Zion being present, Talka, priest of Lalibela, speaking.
>
> Item, the keeper of Zion, Abba Hiruy, by the will of God is made blind, and he hid it from his brothers from the season of the rains until the Nativity.
>
> Item, at the Nativity, when the inner chest was opened before witnesses for the cloths, it was found disturbed, by reason of a keeping without eyes.
>
> Item, the lock of the chest is sound.
>
> Item, nothing is wanting.
>
> Item, the name of Hiruy is taken from the roll of the keepers of Zion. Let his brothers pray for him.
>
> Whoever adds to this finding or takes from it, let him answer for it before the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit. Amen.

At the fourth item the scribe lifted his reed and asked, without looking up, whether he should write *nothing is wanting* or *nothing is known to be wanting*, and Talka said, “The first.”

The reed went on. It squeaked at every down-stroke, a small sound and a stubborn one, and in the quiet of the court it seemed the loudest thing there. The rue smoke drifted across the vellum. When the scribe came to the last item the guardian had a palm ring between his finger and thumb and was working it round and round, and Talka watched the green ring turn and did not watch the reed.

The scribe sanded the leaf and carried it to the stool. The azaj read it with his lips moving. He read the fourth item twice.

“A blind keeper,” he said. “The King will be sorry to hear it. He likes his keepers to see.” He looked over the vellum at the litter, and then at Talka, for a long moment, and Talka gave him back his own face and nothing else. “Nothing is wanting.”

“Nothing.”

“Then the house has lost a keeper and kept everything else.” The azaj rolled the leaf and put it inside his coat. “It is a cheap year for Zion, Abba.”

On the steps the eldest lifted his hand, and the drums began.

The palms went round the church. The deacons led, dancing backward before the cross with their staffs held level, the sistra shaking, and the townsmen came after with their fronds high, singing *Hosanna to the Son of David*, three times round the old walls. They parted round the litter and closed again behind it. A boy who could not have been seven stopped long enough to put a cross of palm on the blind man’s chest, and ran on.

The guardian’s lodging stood inside the precinct wall on the north side, a single room of mud-brick under a roof of poles and blackened thatch, with a door so low that Talka bent his head at it every time and had cracked his skull on the lintel twice in the first week. There was one window, shuttered with acacia boards on leather hinges; a bed of hide thongs on a frame, a stool, three hearthstones, a niche for the lamp, a water jar sweating in the corner. The room smelled of sickness, of old sweat and old fever, and of the dung cakes on the hearth; and through the cracks of the door, whenever the wind came from the church, of Zion’s incense, which was not Lalibela’s. It was sharper, with a bite of something resinous under the frankincense that Talka had never been able to name.

He had sat at this bed since the second week after Timkat. He had counted the days at first, up to forty, the day at home the dead are let go; the guardian was not let go, and Talka stopped counting.

Mornings, a deacon of the house brought a round of barley bread to the door. Talka took it from him on the threshold. He sat on the stool and cut the heel off first with the horn-handled knife, and stepped out, and laid it on the flat stone beside the door; then he cut the rest into pieces a sick man could hold, and covered them against the dust with the lid. It was a mesob lid of woven grass, red and black, worked in tight rings in the weave of the villages two days down the escarpment, where his mother had been born. Its rim had been mended twice, each time with a paler grass than the first, so that it wore three colours round its edge. It had gone with him on every road since the famine year, and he had never asked himself where it came from. By noon the heel was always gone from the stone. He had never seen what took it.

That evening he set the guardian’s remedy going. More rue on the coals, and more of the olive-wood, split thin so it would smoke and not blaze; and a fistful of the bitter leaf steeped in a pot of water by the fire until the water went brown and the smell of it made Gebre turn his face away. Gebre fed the fire. He did it with his left hand, a dung cake at a time, his right hand in his sleeve. At dusk Talka closed the shutter against the cold and set the bar across it, and the room drew in round the hearth and was still.

He gave the bitter water from a horn spoon, a little and then a little more, and held the old head up to swallow it. He had done it every night since he came. On most nights the guardian swallowed and slept and woke talking to men who had died before either of them took the cowl. Tonight he swallowed, and coughed, and lay with his eyes open on the roof-poles while the fever went out of him in a long sweat, and somewhere in the second watch he said, in his own voice, “Talka.”

“I am here.”

“I know you are here. You have been breathing through your nose at me since Timkat.” The pale eyes moved, though they did not find him. “What day is it?”

“Hosanna. The palms have gone round.”

“And the King’s man?”

“Rides at first light.”

“With what?”

Talka told him. He had decided, while the fever broke, not to make it smaller than it was. He said the finding over item by item, and the guardian listened with his hands open on the blanket. When Talka came to the last item the hands did not move.

“So,” the guardian said. “I am off the roll.”

“Yes.”

“And nothing is wanting.”

“Nothing.”

The old man breathed for a while. The smoke of the rue hung under the thatch.

“My master would have liked your finding,” he said. “When he gave me the key he told me there had been pieces gone from that chest before his time, and before his master’s. The house never counted them. He said those who hold fragments are cursed, and the house would not count the curse into its own books.” His mouth moved at the corner. “So you see. Nothing is wanting. It is the oldest sentence in Zion.”

Talka said nothing to that.

The guardian’s hand came across the blanket and found his sleeve, and held it.

“Tell them I did not sleep.”

“Who?”

“Whoever reads the roll without me in it. Whoever asks. Write that I was blind from the rains, that I was a proud old fool and hid it; that is all true, write it. But I did not sleep at that door, Talka. Not one night. Tell them.”

“I will tell them,” Talka said.

The hand let go. After a time the guardian said, in a different voice, almost a boy’s, “Say me the line.”

“Which line?”

“Ours. The one Abba Sertsa beat into us on the floor with our knees burning. I cannot find the beginning of it any more. Say it for me.”

So Talka said it, on the stool by the shuttered window in the smell of rue, his hands on his knees, and the old words came up without his calling them, because they had been put in deep.

He began where the novices began, before the churches: with the years the river of Egypt did not rise. Seven years it did not rise, the line said, and the people of the river ate their seed and then their beasts, and the old men walked out into the dust so the children might have their share; and in that hunger a mother of the old line went up out of the river’s country and carried the word south, and the word was kept. Then the line came up into Lasta and named the kings who cut the houses of God out of the rock, and the masters who had kept the house at Lalibela, down to Abba Sertsa with his switch. And then, as every novice had done on that floor, kneeling, Talka gave his own fathers back to the seventh, because a boy who could not name his fathers had been beaten for it until he could.

“And I, Talka, son of Zena Markos, son of Asbe Hawaryat, son of—”

The guardian’s hand had wandered to the stool beside the bed, where the bread lay under the lid for the morning. His fingers had found the rim and were going round it, over the first grass and the two mendings and back, and round again.

“And Elleni,” he said. “You have left out Elleni, who brought you bread at the gate.”

Talka stopped.

“My father had no daughter.”

The guardian laughed. It was hardly a laugh, a breath through the nose and a shake of the chest that set him coughing, and when the cough was done he was still smiling at the roof, pleased with a joke he thought he had been told. Talka waited for the smile to go and it did not go. He put it with the fever, where it belonged. By the hearth Gebre had stopped with a dung cake in his hand, and then he laid it on the coals.

“Son of Asbe Hawaryat,” Talka said, and went on to the seventh, and finished.

The guardian lay quiet for so long that Talka thought he had gone back under. Then he said, “You will want to know about the door.”

“Only if you want to tell it.”

“Do you remember Arwe?”

Every boy in the novices’ house had remembered Arwe, in the dark.

“The serpent who was king,” the guardian said, “before there were kings. They fed him a girl at every new moon, and he ate them, and the people let him, because he was king and that was the price. And a stranger came up out of the south and saw it, and did not take a sword to him, because there was no sword that would go through that hide. He took a goat. He fed the king a goat with something in its belly that a serpent cannot keep down, and Arwe swallowed it whole. He swallowed everything whole. It lay in him and would not go down and would not come up. And he died of it. From the inside.” The old man turned his head on the hide. “What did Abba Sertsa say it meant?”

“That a stranger is a gift of God.”

“That was what he said to the abbot. What did he say to us?”

Talka found it, far back, in the old man’s dry voice with the switch tapping on the floor behind it.

“You do not kill a devourer,” he said slowly. “You give it something it cannot swallow.”

“Yes,” said the guardian. “That.” He seemed satisfied, and Talka, who could see nothing in it but a story for frightening boys into sleep, let him be satisfied.

The coals ticked. Outside the frost was coming down; Talka felt it come off the shutter boards into the side of his face.

“It was the night before the rains broke,” the guardian said. “I could still see a little then. The lamp at the sanctum door was a yellow smear, and the curtain was a dark place beside it. I had my hand on the wall and my back to the court and my face to the curtain, where a keeper’s face should be. And there was a voice at the door behind me.” His hand lifted off the blanket and lay down again. “A woman’s voice, Talka. At the door. Speaking the tongue under Ge’ez. The one on the stones.”

Talka felt the hair stand on his arms.

“No woman comes inside the precinct wall,” he said.

“I know what comes inside the wall. I kept it.” The blind eyes were wide. “She said something in that tongue, not loud, and my back was turned to the sanctum, as if God had called me. I did not choose to turn. I was turned. I stood with my face to the court and listened to her, and I did not understand one word, and I wept, and I do not know for how long.” He swallowed. “When I turned round again the curtain was where it had been. I thought I had dreamed it on my feet. The rains came that night and I could not see the lamp at all after them. That is all. I did not sleep.”

He did sleep then, all at once, with his mouth open and his hand on the rim of the lid.

Talka sat with it.

The tongue under Ge’ez. The old brothers had spoken of it when he was young, low, and never by daylight: a tongue older than the church’s, that the carriers wrote. He had seen it written only once in his life, and that was on the roof of the Mother’s house at Lalibela, in rows that leaned and branched and would not scrape away; and the hand that wrote it there had been a woman’s, and he had lit the fire under her with his own lamp.

No woman came inside the wall of Zion. No living one.

He thought of the thin yellow rind that had clung all night along the juniper’s underside and would not go over. He thought of her legs, which he had not covered; of the cloth of grey ash he had tied with his own hands and buried before the bell with not one word sung over it. It had been done so in his master’s time. Never often. Never written. Nobody had ever told him what became of them afterward, the ones it was done to; whether they went where the dead go, or whether a thing burned in the wrong fire, in the wrong house, without the words, went nowhere, and walked.

He sat until the coals were grey, and by then he believed it. She had stood at the door of Zion in the dark before the rains and turned a keeper’s back with her own tongue. He did not know what she had come for. He wondered whether he had burned her badly, and could not stop wondering.

Gebre had lit the lamp in the niche. Talka got up off the stool with his left knee cracking and held out his hand.

Gebre came. He always came. The room smelled of rue and of the bitter pot gone cold on the hearth. Talka took the right wrist, as one takes a pulse, at the place where the blood knocks, and the arm went still, and then let itself be drawn out of the sleeve and turned to the light.

The line ran across the palm as it had since the night of the bell, from the root of the first finger toward the heel: her signs, all of them, in their place. He counted them. At Timkat, past the last of them, there had been one low mark that rose and fell. There were more now. The one had become three, laid one under another, and under the three a long flat stroke that ran on to the very edge of the palm and did not end.

He made it out slowly, by the shape of it and nothing else. Water, and water under it, and a line beneath that went on past where he could follow.

*[ — — — — — ] sea*

He put his thumb on it. It did not smear. Gebre’s pulse jumped under his fingers and went on, and Gebre looked at the floor two paces to the left of Talka’s feet.

Talka let him go. He took his cloak from the peg, and from the blanket, where it had fallen out of the old man’s lap, the first of the palm rings, and went out.

The stelae stood in the field beyond the church in the last of the dark, some upright, some leaning on their neighbours, some lying where they had fallen. The tallest of all lay on the ground in great pieces, carved into storeys with false windows and false doors that opened on nothing. The cold had come down hard. It smelled of frost and of the dead dung fires of the town and of the dust that lay over everything at Aksum, even the frost. Talka walked among the stones with his hand-cross in one fist and the palm ring in the other, his knee singing, until he came to a thing he had passed every day since he came and never stopped at: a broad low block, squared and dressed, with sockets cut in its top where a throne had stood once, before anyone now living knew whose.

He sat on its step and waited for the light, because there was nothing else to wait for.

It came grey, and then yellow, low along the ground from the east, and lay across the face of the block, and he saw for the first time that the face was written. Three bands of writing, one above another, the length of the stone.

The top band was Greek. He had copied Greek letters once, as a novice’s exercise, and could still pick out a word in three: *king*; *of the Aksumites*; a run of peoples’ names he did not know. The middle band was Ge’ez, the old upright Ge’ez without its vowels, and that he could read, slowly, with his lips. *King of Aksum and of Himyar*, it said, and more of the same, the old kings’ long breath of conquests.

And in both bands, at the same place in the sentence, the masons had been at it with a chisel.

He put out his thumb and found it. A patch the size of a palm where the polished face was gone, pocked and rough as a scab, and over the scar a cross cut deep and clean. The pocked place was cooler than the polished stone round it. He knew what had been there. The masters had taught it to every novice of his years, as a warning: what the old kings called themselves before the faith came up from the coast. *Son of Mahrem, who is not conquered by the enemy.* The war god, who had fathered kings. The masons of the first Christian king had cut him out of his own stone and cut the cross over the wound.

The lowest band was older than either. The light had reached it now, raking along the ground, and the rime in its grooves was going to water in the sun, and the wet grooves stood out dark against the dry face; and he could see what was cut there.

The signs leaned. They branched, and some hooked back on themselves, and none of them wore a vowel.

Talka could not read one of them. He did not need to. He had stood under the painters’ scaffold in Bet Maryam in the year after the burning, his neck aching, telling them where to lay the ochre, and had watched these same shapes go under the paint row by row and come up through it again before the season was out. They were the shapes on his roof. They were her shapes.

And at the same place in the sentence as the two scars above, where the chisel had gone in the Greek and in the Ge’ez, the third band had no scar. The masons had cut out the god in every tongue they could read. This one nobody could read, even then. So they had left his name standing, because they did not know it was his name.

*The tongue under Ge’ez. The one on the stones.*

So the old man had not dreamed it on his feet. The pilgrims who carried her in had said she came down the north road. She had come through Aksum, then, with the boy at her side, before ever she came to Lalibela; and she had spoken at the door of Zion in the oldest tongue on its stones, and those stones had answered to it for a thousand years with no one to hear them.

He found that his thumb was resting in the grooves of the unscarred name, in the wet. He took it off as if it were hot.

The sun climbed. The dark in the grooves paled at the edges, then along their length, and in the time it takes to sing two psalms the lowest band was only a rough place on an old stone again, a little paler than the Ge’ez above it. He had not moved. When it was gone he got up and went back across the field with the palm ring crushed out of its shape in his fist.

The guardian was awake when he ducked in under the lintel, breathing high and quick, his hands cold. Talka knew that breathing. He had sat beside it at a great many beds.

The morning’s bread had come; it lay on the threshold in its cloth. He sat down on the stool with it because his hands wanted something to do, and drew the knife, and cut the heel off first, and got up and took it out and laid it on the stone beside the door, where the frost lay white. Then he came back and lifted the lid and put the rest under it.

The guardian had turned his head at the sound of the knife. He was smiling.

“You still cut the heel first.”

“Every morning of my life,” Talka said.

He took the cold hands between his own. The rue smoke had gone thin and sweet. Outside, beyond the wall, a horn sounded at the gate, and horses went out of it at a trot, northward, and the sound of them went away up the road with the finding.

The guardian’s eyes were open on the roof-poles. His lips moved, and Talka bent to them.

“He smelled of our incense,” the guardian said. “Lalibela’s.”

“Who did?”

The hands in his did not answer. The breathing went up once more, high, and caught, and did not come down; and Talka held the hands until they were no colder than his own, and then a while longer.

Through the low door he could see the stone, and on the stone the heel of the loaf, with the rime already climbing its cut face.
