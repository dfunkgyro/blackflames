# CHAPTER 5

## ZOSER

*Naples, and the road north — January to Ash Wednesday, 1326*

They ran out the same plank to put them ashore.

Zoser knew it by the tallow. Someone had greased its underside again for the quay, and it went out over the gap between hull and stone with the long wet squeal he had heard in his sleep every night since Sicily, and settled in the last of the sun: a board, a thing for walking on. It was the seventh day after Timkat. The mate jerked his chin. Down.

The priest went first, because the priest went first. Halfway down he stopped with his palm flat beneath his ribs and stood for three breaths while the gulls screamed and a porter on the quay looked up and laughed. Then he went on down, upright, and did not put out a hand to the rope. Zoser came behind with the book-satchel and the King of Kings’ letter, and did not look at the brown water between the boards and the stone.

Get him to Rome alive. That was the whole of it now.

Desta had carried this man up the escarpment above the salt like a sack of barley. Desta had looked once at Zoser’s scrip on that deck, and then walked to the end of this board and off it, so that the search would stop before it reached the boy. A man does not do that for nothing. Then Zoser would have to be something: whatever Desta had seen when he looked. He would begin by walking a sick old priest the length of a strange country in winter, with no soldier, no sword, and no tongue the country spoke.

His foot came down on stone, and his knees did not believe it. A woman with a basket of eels pulled her small son behind her skirts and crossed herself.

⁂

The quay stank of fish guts trodden into the cracks, of pitch boiling in a cauldron by the boats, of dung, and of something frying in old oil under the wall.

A word followed them along the mole. *Saraceni.* A boy on a bollard said it and grinned; a man mending a net spat.

The tollman sat by the harbour gate with a ledger and a brazier. He had a fur collar and a clerk’s hands: the second finger stained to the knuckle with ink, a callus where the pen lay, the nails pared short. He looked at the priest’s filthy shamma and white beard, and then past them, at Angeska.

She had gone forward without being told, the priest’s wife, small in her salt-stiff shawl. He could not follow her. She spoke the friar’s Latin, only faster and without the chant, so that it went past him like water past a stone; then a quick slurred tongue to a man from the gatehouse, and the traders’ Greek of Alexandria to a boatman, without a pause between, as a scribe moves from black ink to red without lifting his hand.

“Tell him who we are,” the priest said in Amharic. “The embassy of the King of Kings.”

“I have told him,” she said, and went on.

Zoser watched the tollman’s pen. He could sound Latin letters off a page, though he could not weigh them, and he read the line upside down as it came:

> MAVRI  III  ———  gr. vi

Three short strokes side by side. Then a figure for the toll. No names. The man sanded it, and the three of them were in the ledger of Naples, and they were three strokes.

The boatman said something low to Angeska and touched his own mouth. Zoser caught one word: *moria*.

“There is a sickness,” Angeska said in Amharic, “in the villages up the coast road. People say. They have shut a gate or two.”

“The captain’s map goes by the coast,” said the priest.

“Then the captain’s map goes through the sickness.” She did not ask to see it. “There is the old road inland, by Benevento. It is longer, and the bridges are stone.”

*How does she know the bridges are stone?* Then: she had spent thirty days at the mate’s elbow, and three weeks among the factors at Alexandria, asking. She asked everyone everything. That was what she was for.

Behind them the mate had come down the plank with a long bundle in oilcloth under his arm, bound with cord, as long as a man’s arm and a half, with a hard curve at one end, and a flat packet in waxed linen tucked against it. A rider in a grey cloak waited by the chandler’s, mounted, his horse blowing steam. The mate handed both up. The rider put the packet inside his coat, laid the bundle across his saddlebow, and turned north up the street.

Zoser knew the length of it. He knew the curve of the grip under the cloth. He watched Desta’s sword go up the street on a stranger’s saddle until a cart came between.

At the rail above, the captain stood drying his hands on a cloth, one finger at a time. He was not watching the sword. He was watching Zoser.

⁂

Three doors shut on them before dark. At the fourth, in an alley that smelled of tanning and the sea, an old woman with no teeth took Angeska’s coins and let them sleep in a loft above her fish-store among nets that had not been dry since summer. Brine and old scales; a tallow dip whose smoke tasted of mutton fat.

The priest lay down on the nets under the eaves without eating. His wife put bread by his hand, and after a while took it back and wrapped it.

Zoser sat on the boards beside him, where the roof came down low, close enough to hear the old man’s breath catch and go on, and took the pouch out of his shamma.

He had not opened it since the plank. Now he drew the thong, and the smell came out, and it was so exactly Desta, all at once, in this loft at the end of the world, that he had to shut his eyes. Roasted barley. Roasted chickpea. Desta’s *kolo*, no spice in it: the plain brown smell of a clay pan over a fire of dung and juniper, of a hillside at noon, of a big hand held out sideways without looking, palm up, full.

The great fast would come on the road, and at the end of it *Fasika*, and on the night of *Fasika* at home every man broke the fast from his own pan with whoever sat beside him. He would want something from home in his hand that night. He would want it to be this.

So he counted.

He laid the grains out on his knee in rows, as Desta had taught him to lay out the stars on the roof at Alexandria. *Don’t just look. Touch it, like you’re counting your brothers.* Twelve barley. Seven chickpea, split. Then he counted what was left in the pouch, a pinch at a time, and lost his place among the barley somewhere past two hundred, and began again, and came to a different number.

He found that he was smiling.

“You see,” he whispered to the dark. “I am no better at it than you.”

An arm’s length away on the nets, the priest’s breathing did not change.

Three grains a night, then, until the fast; then none, and the rest for *Fasika*. He ate three. The barley cracked between his back teeth, smoke and earth, and went down into him and lay there warm.

Across the loft Angeska sat against the wall with her hands in her lap. She had watched him count. When he looked up she did not look away. She nodded once, as if he had told her something, and pinched out the light.

⁂

The road to Benevento climbed out of the plain between bare vines and walls of black stone, and the cold came down to meet it.

It lay in the valley bottoms all day like water in a jar, and the low white sun went over it and did nothing. The villages smelled of frozen dung and wet smoke, and their people came to their doors to stare, and then shut the doors.

They went at the priest’s pace: a mile, and a rest; then less. He would stop in the middle of the road with his hand beneath his ribs and his eyes on the horizon, never bending, and Zoser and Angeska would look at the horizon too, so that no carter could say one of the Moors was ill.

She took them by roads that were not on the map. At a fork below a village on a spur, where the dotted line went right, she went left without slowing, and an hour later the left road came down to a stone bridge over a green river.

*The mate*, Zoser told himself. *The factors.* She had asked.

⁂

They came to Benevento on the fourth day as the gate was closing.

The gate was an arch, the greatest piece of cut stone Zoser had ever stood under, set into the city wall: a gilded initial on a page of common hand. Across the top ran a line of Latin letters unlike any he had seen. The friar’s book had been all hands joined, letters leaning arm in arm. These stood apart, square and deep and alone, cut to the first joint of his thumb, with a dot between each word and the next. He sounded what he could reach. *O. P. T. I. M. O.* He did not know what they said.

The gatekeeper was turning a key in the wicket door beside the arch, black iron as long as Zoser’s forearm, and it went round in the lock with one dry crack. Angeska spoke to him, and the key stopped, and he went to fetch someone.

Under the vault the road was dry, sheltered from the frost, and the dust lay pale and fine as flour, and smelled of cold stone and the mules that had gone through.

Angeska crouched. She let her hem lie in the dust. She put out one finger and wrote.

Zoser was not looking at the letters. He was looking at the finger, and at the dust lifting a little on either side of the stroke, and something in him turned over like a sleeper. Dust, and a finger moving in it. A smell came with it that was not here, smoke and some kitchen herb, and a floor, and the edge of a hand coming down. He reached for it. It went the way a word goes that was on the tongue a moment ago, and there was nothing behind it but the arch and the cold and the woman crouched in the road.

“*Clavis*,” she said.

The word lay in the dust in the friar’s letters, joined and leaning.

“*Clavis*.” She tipped her head at the iron in the wicket. “Key.”

“*Clavis*,” said Zoser.

“You will not keep it by ear.” She said it without unkindness. “I have watched you since Alexandria. You hold everything a man says in your own tongue, or in Arabic, the very words, and which hand he lifted when he said them. What a man says to you in Latin goes through you like rain through thatch. You said a whole Creed on that deck, and I do not believe you understood one word in ten. You keep it only because you wrote it down.”

He thought of the last leaves of his psalter, the friar’s sounds in Ge’ez letters, line under line, a year of call and answer on a mat at night until the drum of it lived in his mouth. The friar’s boy had read off the page and Zoser had given it back. Nothing the boy had merely said to him in passing had ever stayed.

“It came off a page,” he said. “Not off a tongue.”

“Then you will not hear it. You will see it. One word a night, a word you can use.” She stood and brushed her hands together. “It is all I can give you. It is not much, against what is coming.”

“What is coming?”

“Rome,” she said, and the gatekeeper came back with a man in a fur hat, and she went to them.

Zoser went through the wicket last. He looked back once. A cart wheel had already gone through the word and taken the middle out of it.

⁂

In the pilgrims’ hall that night, which stank of wet wool and a hundred feet, he made ink.

The sea had taken his pens and his horn. He scraped soot from under a tallow lamp with the edge of a shell and ground it in the shell’s hollow with water and spit, as the old men of the scriptorium did when the oak-gall ran out, and chewed a splinter until it would take ink.

The psalter had dried crooked, stained in brown tide-lines. He turned to the first leaf, inside the board, where there was no psalm.

The letter was still there, softened by the sea: one sign in the corner, crooked and too large, in charcoal, by a child’s hand that had pressed hard enough to dent the vellum. አ. He had made it his first week at Lalibela, with the ash of the road still in his hair, when the teacher asked whether he knew any letters, and he knew one. Zoser had never asked himself where he learned it. A child knows what he knows.

He held the splinter over the page and waited for the finger in the dust to come nearer. It did not.

He wrote the word beside the አ, close under it: the Latin first, as she had made it, C, L, A, V, I, S; under that the same sound in the letters of his own tongue, so he would know it when it came to him as sound; then, smaller, *key*. The soot went on brown and dried black, and smelled of mutton.

The next night, at Montesarchio on the old road west, Angeska held out her hand for the psalter before she would give him another word. “Which?”

He opened his mouth. It was gone. There was only the shape where it had been. He looked at the leaf.

“*Clavis*,” he read.

“Not a new one,” she said, “until you hold this one,” and gave him back the book.

⁂

In the night the snow came down off the mountain behind the town, and in the morning the priest could not rise.

He lay on the inn’s straw with his knees drawn up and said he would walk when the road was fit. The road was not fit for eight days. The innkeeper, a man who smelled of wine-lees and goat, raised the price of the straw each morning the snow lay, and Angeska paid it, and Zoser watched the purse at her girdle go slack. On the fourth day she sold her silver ear-drops for half their weight.

Each night she held out her hand. Each night Zoser looked at the leaf and read the word from it, and each night she gave the book back.

⁂

The great fast began on the Monday, two days out of Montesarchio, on the road down to Capua, where the old stone way of the emperors ran across the plain between black poplars as if drawn with a rule.

No meat, no milk, no egg; nothing from dawn to the ninth hour, then one meal. A traveller, the abbot had taught them, might keep the *Hudade* gently, for the body that walks must be fed; and so Zoser and Angeska kept it gently. At the ninth hour they sat against a milestone furred with wet moss and ate dry bread, and a handful of boiled beans gone cold in a cloth, and a raw onion that Zoser cut with his knife. The smell of it leapt out sharp in the cold air, sharp enough to make his eyes run, over the wet stone at his back and the thin smoke of a poplar fire where someone was burning prunings in a field. He did not open Desta’s pouch. That was for *Fasika* now.

The priest sat a little way off, upright on a stone.

Zoser had begun to count the old man’s meals too. A mouthful of bread at Montesarchio, chewed a long time and spat into his hand when he thought no one saw. Broth, once. He had eaten like a fasting man since Alexandria.

Zoser went to him with the bread. “It is past the ninth hour, Qess.”

“I know what hour it is.”

The old man did not look at the bread. Zoser’s hand went to the pouch, and stopped on it. Fifty-five days to *Fasika*, and he had meant to open it next on that night and no other.

For him, he drew the thong. He poured three grains into his palm beside the bread, and held that out too. Barley and chickpea. There was nothing in it a man could not eat in the fast.

Something moved at the side of the priest’s mouth and went still. “That is the soldier’s.”

“He would give it.”

“I know he would give it.” The priest’s hand came off his knee and lay flat on his belly. “Put it away.”

“Even the hermits of Waldebba eat once,” Angeska said mildly, from the milestone. “After the ninth hour. It is the rule, husband. You taught it to the boys yourself.”

“Then I am not a hermit of Waldebba.” The priest lifted his chin toward the north, where the poplars ran together into a grey point. “A man who is going to stand before the Pope should go to him empty.”

Zoser had seen the old man stand through the whole Passion on Good Friday and refuse a stool. Now he was going to the Pope of Rome for the church of the King of Kings, and he would go purified, as Moses went up the mountain. Zoser felt it go into him, a reluctant warmth, the thing he had felt as a boy at the back of Bet Medhani Alem when the old man’s voice rose in the reading.

“He keeps it harder than any of them,” Angeska said. She was looking at her husband’s hand on his belly. “He always has.”

The priest did not answer. After a while he got up without help and began to walk.

Zoser put the grains back in the pouch, and counted them in.

⁂

Capua shut every door it had.

A patrol held the bridge over its wide brown river, four men in quilted coats round a fire of wet vine-stocks that smoked more than it burned. Angeska talked. They searched anyway. Hands went down Zoser’s sides and over the sash and stopped on the scrip, squeezed it once, and moved on, bored. He breathed again.

Then they came to the priest.

A man took the satchel from his shoulder and up-ended it on the stones of the bridge, and another put both hands on the old man’s ribs and ran them down. When the hands reached his belly the priest made no sound. He stood with his eyes on the sky above the river, and his whole body went still, as a hare goes still; and then the shaking started in his hands and he could not stop it. One of them pressed a little harder, to see.

Angeska put a coin in the corporal’s palm, and another, and the hands went away. The purse was nearly flat now.

They crossed at dusk with snow beginning, small dry flakes that lay on the shamma like salt. At the last inn by the river a man stood in the lit doorway with his arm across it and said a word Zoser had heard on the road and had not wanted to understand. *Heretici.* He did not need it translated. The arm said it.

“He says we are heretics,” Angeska said pleasantly, smiling at the man, “or Saracens. He is not certain which. He says it makes no difference to the beds.”

They slept in a church porch outside the walls, with the snow driving in under the arch and the stink of the town ditches thick in the cold. Zoser lay between the priest and the wind. In the night the old man began to shake again, his teeth clicking, his palms pressed flat on his belly, and Zoser turned and put a hand on his shoulder.

The priest struck it off. He did not wake for it, or he did not seem to. He drew himself in tighter, his back to the wall and both arms across his belly, and said one word in his sleep, in Greek, that Zoser did not know.

Zoser lay awake until the snow stopped, and did not touch him again.

⁂

Three days out of Capua, below the mountain of Cassino, the bridge had gone in the autumn floods. Its two stumps stood up out of the stream like broken teeth with ice on them.

The carters’ ford ran a little upstream, wide and shallow over stones. The water was grey-green and quick and smoked in the frost, and ice had grown out from both banks in plates as thin as a fingernail. It smelled of snow and iron.

Angeska hitched her skirts into her girdle and went in. Zoser saw the cold take her: she stopped one breath with the water at her knees, and her face went grey, and then she went on, and came up the far bank, and did not look back.

The priest stood at the edge and looked at the water.

His lips were the colour of ash. He was sixty-four, he had eaten almost nothing since the fast began and little enough before it, his hands had not been steady since the bridge at Capua, and Zoser knew as plainly as he knew his own name that if the old man went in on his own feet, the stream would roll him under the ice.

“Qess.”

The priest did not look at him.

Zoser thought of a cliff of red rock above a white plain, and a big man stooping without a word. He did not ask. Desta had not asked. He hung the satchel round his neck, and turned his back, and bent, and took the old man by one wrist and one knee as he had seen it done, and swung.

The priest was lighter than he had thought, a frame of sticks inside a wet shamma that smelled of old smoke and sweat and something sour beneath. He came up across Zoser’s shoulders like a sack, and lay there rigid with outrage.

“Put me down.”

Zoser went into the water.

The cold took his feet off at the ankle; he was walking on stumps. Every stone had to be found, and tried, and trusted, with the current leaning on his legs like a man shouldering through a crowd.

“Put me down, I said.”

He went on. The water came up to his thighs, and the shock of it there took the sight from his eyes; and against the outside of his right thigh, under the shamma, under the sash, the scrip lay warm. It was not the warmth of his body; his body had none left there. It lay along his leg as warm as a loaf from the oven, and it did not cool.

Then the bottom shelved under him, and the stream came up over his belly to his chest, and the cold found the old cut below his left collarbone first, as cold always did, as if it had been left open for it: a barleycorn of ice laid along a childhood hurt from the famine road, before ever it reached his heart.

The old man’s belly was pressed to the back of his neck. His breath came short and hot by Zoser’s ear. In the middle of the ford, with the stream roaring round them, he said something into Zoser’s nape, very low.

“Qess?”

A pause. The current shoved. Zoser found a stone, and tried it, and it held.

“Clay,” said the priest through his teeth, as if each word were being drawn out of him with tongs, “carrying the potter.”

Zoser nearly went down.

He had no breath to laugh with. But something went through him that did a laugh’s work, and his foot slid and found another stone. On his shoulders the old man had gone rigid again, as if he had said something filthy in church and was waiting for the roof to fall. Zoser could feel what it had cost, the way a mule feels a load shift. He said nothing back. Anything he said would have cost more.

The water went down to his knees, to his calves. His feet came up out of the stream and broke the ice at the edge, and he climbed the bank on his hands and his stumps, and knelt, and let the old man down onto the frozen grass as Desta had set him down on the rock: as a man sets down a jar of honey.

He stayed on all fours, his teeth going like a sistrum.

Something heavy came down over his shoulders.

It was the priest’s cloak, the coarse brown wool of the country that Angeska had bought him at Naples, still warm from the old man’s body. A hand pulled its edges together under Zoser’s chin, roughly, as one closes a sack, and was gone.

Zoser looked up. The priest stood over him in his wet shamma with his arms bare to the frost, his beard stiff, his hand already back in its place beneath his ribs. He did not say anything. Then he turned and walked up toward the mountain, slowly, without help, his back very straight.

Zoser’s hand had gone of itself to Desta’s pouch, and through the wet leather he counted the days since the plank on the grains, one under his thumb for each, touching them as Desta had taught him, like counting his brothers; he lost his place in the third ten and began again, and came out both times on the same number. It was the fortieth day, the day that at home the dead are let go.

Zoser knelt on the bank in the old man’s cloak, in the smell of sheep and smoke and the sour thing under it, and did not get up for a long time.

⁂

The monks of Cassino kept a guest hall for pilgrims in the town under the mountain, long and low, where a lay brother with a scalded forearm ladled bean soup from a cauldron and the whole place smelled of beans and woodsmoke and steaming wool.

The brother at the gate was old and quick. He did not shut the door or cross himself. He lifted his lantern and looked at each of them in turn, the priest, the woman, the boy, as a man checks goods against a bill. Then he asked Angeska something, and in it Zoser heard *Mauri*, which he knew now, and a word like *Ianuens*-.

Angeska answered in a few words, and the brother stood aside.

“He asks whether we are the Ethiopians,” she said in Amharic. “I told him we were. They have room.”

There was a Copt by the hearth. Zoser saw the blue cross tattooed inside his wrist first: a narrow bald man with a pilgrim’s leaden badge, eating alone at the end of the bench no one else would use.

“Peace to you, brother,” he said in Arabic. “Abyssinia?”

“Lalibela.”

“Then we are cousins at the end of the bench. Bishoy, of Akhmim, going to see the bones of the apostles before my own are ready.” He lowered his voice. “So it is you.”

“What is me?”

“What the old brother asked her at the door. ‘Are these the Moors the Genoese captain spoke of?’” Bishoy spooned his soup. “A rider came through a fortnight since, the captain’s man, a fellow in a grey cloak riding hard for Rome and beyond it. He had a long thing in oilcloth across his saddle and would not let the brothers carry it in for him; he ate with it across his knees. He told them at table that his captain’s Moors would come up this road after him. Two men and a woman, black Christians. He described you well.” He looked at Zoser over the spoon. “Your wife did not say?”

“She is not my wife.”

A long thing in oilcloth. Zoser had walked behind it for five months. It had sat at this bench, across a stranger’s knees, a fortnight ago, and gone on north ahead of them, to wherever it was being carried.

Down the hall Angeska was helping the old priest out of his wet shamma into a dry one the lay brother had found. She did not turn.

*Ask for me in Genoa.* The captain had stood at the rail at Naples drying his hands. A captain sent word of an embassy as he sent word of a cargo, and Latins talked of everything at table. That was all it was. And she had shortened the brother’s question as she shortened everything that might frighten the old man, as she had softened the tollman. It was what she was for.

His hand had gone to the scrip. He took it away.

“Your name,” said Bishoy. “Forgive an old man. You did not say it.”

“Zoser.”

Bishoy put down his spoon. “Say it again.”

Zoser said it again, and the Copt repeated it softly, the way a moneychanger tries a coin between his teeth. “Who gave you that?”

“I don’t know. It was my name when I came to Lalibela.”

“It is no saint’s. It is not Greek, not Arabic. Up the river past Akhmim there are stones older than Moses, temples half in the sand, covered with pictures instead of letters, birds and eyes and little men walking, and no one living can read a line. My grandfather said the old names were still in there, that the stones kept them when no mouth would.” He looked at Zoser a long moment. “Yours is older than our letters, I think. It is a name the old stones keep.”

“What does it mean?”

“I told you. No one living can read them.” Bishoy drank from his bowl and wiped his mouth. “Eat your soup, cousin. It’s beans. It’s the fast.”

That night, by a tallow lamp that burned as straight as a reed, Zoser opened the psalter to the first leaf and did not look at it.

“*Clavis*,” he said. “Key.”

Across the hall Angeska turned her head. She did not smile. She held out her hand for the book, and looked at the word a while by the lamp, and gave it back to him unwritten.

“In Rome,” she said. “There will be doors enough in Rome.”

⁂

They came to Rome five days later, two of them on the tail of a salt-carter’s wagon that Bishoy paid for and would not be thanked for, by the old road from the south, in sleet, and the first thing Zoser knew of the city was the smell.

It came out across a mile of waste ground to meet them: smoke lying on the city like a lid, and inside it a river gone bad, and tanneries, and the fat-stink of ten thousand tallow candles. Through gaps in the brown walls he saw vines and frozen mud, columns with nothing on their heads, broken marble lying white under the sleet like bones after a battle.

He had imagined Rome all his life. He had imagined it gold.

The priest walked ahead in the brown cloak, which he had taken back at Cassino without a word. Just inside the wall stood the greatest church Zoser had ever seen, a black cross on the highest roof, its bells ringing slow and heavy.

“There,” the priest said softly. “You see it, boy?”

Three guards stood under the arch in leather and old mail, and on each man’s forehead was a smudge of grey ash. Zoser took it for dirt, until he saw the same grey print on all three, and on a woman going in past them with a basket.

The chief of them had a great iron bunch of keys on a ring at his belt, and stood with his thumb hooked through it, watching them come.

The priest went straight to him without waiting for his wife, and pointed past the guard, through the arch, at the great church with its bells, and said in Amharic, in the voice that had filled Bet Medhani Alem to its rock roof: “We are the embassy of the King of Kings of Ethiopia, and we have come to the Pope.”

Whatever the guard made of the Amharic, Zoser saw him understand the arm, and the church, and the last word, which needs no tongue. He looked where the old man pointed, and back at the filthy shamma and the ash-coloured lips.

Then he laughed, easily, almost kindly, the way a man laughs at a child who asks where the sun sleeps.

“The Pope?” said the guard, still laughing, and jerked his thumb not at the church but away past it, north and west, over the roofs and the smoke and the broken columns, beyond the mountains. “He’s in Avignon.”

The priest stood with his arm still raised toward the church.

Zoser did not know the word. He knew the thumb. He knew the laughter. He turned to Angeska.

She stood a little behind him in the sleet, her shawl over her head and her hands folded at her waist, looking at the guard. She did not look surprised.
