# CHAPTER 15

## ZOSER

*Castel Sant'Angelo — the same night*

*The stub.*

It lay in the old man's palm and beat.

A small dark thing, no longer than the top joint of a finger, wet with what had come up with it. Every few breaths it lifted against the lines of the palm, very slightly, and settled again. The old man knelt in the middle of the cell with his hand held out in front of him and stared at it, and the hand did not shake. Everything else in him shook.

The smell reached Zoser a breath later. Bile first, yellow and bitter, the smell the bucket corner had kept all Holy Week. Then, under the bile, something he knew and could not at first place in this room: green, sour, a little sweet. A thorn fence at home on the morning after the first rains, when the herd boys break the wet branches to drive the goats. Wet acacia.

On Angeska's ledge the candle had gone below its last nick. What was left of it stood in a pool of its own fat, and the flame was small and low and smelled of mutton, and it gave the cell less light than the torch in the passage gave the ceiling through the grille. Zoser knelt on the cold stone on the north side of the old man, where he had come when the retching began, and did not remember coming.

Angeska stood over her husband. She had come out of her corner to him an hour ago, when he went to his knees, and had stayed. Her shadow lay across him and across the hand, and she looked down at what was in it, and her face did not move.

"Why didn't you tell me?" she said, in Amharic.

The old man did not lift his eyes from his palm. There was bile in his beard, and on his lower lip a thread of it that caught the stub's light.

"I was ashamed," he said. He swallowed, and it hurt him; Zoser saw it go down his throat. "And afraid that if you knew, you would stop loving me."

"You may have already ensured that."

She said it quietly. It came out of her level, a thing set down. Zoser heard a wife's wound in it, plainly, and knew he was hearing what was not his to hear, and that he ought to have been on the other side of a door. There was no door. He looked at the floor between his knees. The grime there was black, and it shone.

When he looked up she was kneeling.

She had taken the hem of her shawl in her right hand, and she wiped her husband's mouth with it. From each corner of the lips outward, and then the beard, where the bile had run down into the white; and then she folded the hem over on itself and did it again with the clean side. She did not hurry. Her fingers were steady. The old man shut his eyes and let her, with his face lifted a little toward her hand, and the thing went on beating in his open palm between them.

⁂

"What," said Zoser, "what is this?"

His voice came out of him thin and too high. He had heard the whole of it in the last hour: the sanctum, the guardian's back, the wood. He had heard it, and he had not had it in front of him. Now it lay a forearm from his knee, and the word *wood* would not go onto it.

"I stole it," the old man said.

He said it to his palm. Then he drew a long breath through his nose, and the old voice came up in him from somewhere, the one he had read the lessons with at the Easter vigil for forty years, and he gave it from the step of the altar, each clause set down whole.

"It was cut on the mountain where the fire spoke, before Moses came down. Some say the fire touched it."

Zoser leaned to see.

It was not black. He had thought it black, in that light. It was dark brown, the brown of old wood that has been oiled by hands for a long time, and its grain ran close and straight; and in the grain, where the stub's light reached it, there were threads of gold. Fine, broken, branching, gold filaments in its grain like veins. When it beat, the light moved in them.

And there were signs on it.

He was a scribe; his eye went to them before it went anywhere else. They were cut along the grain, or had grown into it, he could not tell which: small, crowded, a line of them and part of another, and he could not make out one. Each time his eye came to rest on a sign it was not where his eye had put it. They went along the wet wood as if they resented being seen.

He did not want to touch it. Every part of him that was a body wanted to be on the other side of the cell, with his back to the wall.

The old man held it out.

"Take it." The hand came across the space between them, palm up, and now it did shake. "Take it, and don't let it be found again."

Zoser did not lift his hands. So the old man took his wrist with the other hand, an old man's grip, harder than it looked, and turned it over and tipped the thing into his palm.

It was warm. It was warmer than the hand it had come out of. It was slick with bile and with the old man's spit, and it beat against the hollow of his palm. Zoser held it away from his body at the full length of his arm, out over the floor, with his fingers spread so that as little of him as possible touched it, and the smell came up off it, bile and wet thorn.

"No," he said. "Give it to your wife. She should carry it."

He said it in Amharic, so that she would hear it said. He meant both things at once and could not have pulled them apart. He did not want it, this thing that had lived a year in the old man's gut and had come up his throat in the hall with golden eyes. And he wanted it where it belonged, if it belonged anywhere on earth: in her hands. She had carried all three of them from Alexandria. She had counted the coins and bought the bread and rendered every word, and found a way where there was none. She would know where to put it.

The old man shook his head.

"No one would believe her." He had his breath back, and he used it plainly, with no lesson in it now. "They would say she bewitched me. They would burn her too."

Zoser looked at him.

In the Cardinal's hall the Sicilian had turned on his stool and asked, in his own slow Arabic, *Is this true, father?* And the old man had looked at his wife, and not at the boy who had just sold him with a lie, and said *Yes. I ordered it. Write that I ordered it.* He had put himself between her and the fire with one word, before Latins, with his name in the minute. That time Zoser had chosen the road and the old man had walked it.

This time he had chosen it himself. It was the second time.

Zoser turned his eyes to Angeska.

She was kneeling where she had knelt to wipe her husband's mouth, the shawl's hem still in her fingers. She was looking at the thing in Zoser's hand. Her lips parted, once, and closed.

That was all. She said nothing.

Zoser lowered his arm. On the floor between the three of them lay the cloth the last crumb had been wrapped in, empty now and smelling of her cloves, and he laid the wood on it and took his hand away and wiped the hand down his shamma, again, and again, and it did not come clean of the warmth.

⁂

It beat on the cloth. He watched it beat.

He had not let himself go back over the plank since the friary except one way. He went back now, one leaf at a time, reading each against the next, as he had been taught to collate: the catchword at the foot of each leaf against the first word of the leaf that followed.

The cell stank of bile. He breathed through his mouth and did not look away from the cloth.

The deck. The rain that was not yet rain. Humbert going down the line with the candle cupped in his palm, and never once looking at a face. Zoser had seen that and not understood it: the eyes always on the flame, and the question put afterward, mildly, a thing to be said while the flame looked. Natures. Keys. What did a captain of the Latins care what a soldier of the King's baggage believed about the Word made flesh? He had not been asking. He had been hunting, and the question was what a hunter says to keep the beast still.

The flame at Desta's face, leaning. Not to Desta. Past him, down the line: toward the place where Zoser knelt, and the barrel beyond.

At the friary, with the strip open on his knee, he had read only the first half of that line. *Toward the place where Zoser knelt.* He had built on it, carefully, every stone square. The candle leaned to him; Desta saw it; Desta walked to stop it. *He died because the light wanted me.* He had pinched out the night-lamp on the strength of that sentence and had kept a forearm of air between his chest and every flame since. It had been a good reading. It had been his.

But then Humbert had come and held the candle to him, and it had done nothing. It had stood straight up over him and not stirred. And then it had gone over again, slowly, past him, to the end of the line. To the barrel. To the old man the sailors had drawn out from between the cask and the rail and knelt on the deck where the light fell. And it had not leaned there. It had bowed to him. To his belly, under the knotted sash.

Zoser had seen it bow to the belly and read *an old man's paunch*.

*He was early.* Not mockery; or not only. Desta had stood up before the candle came to the man it wanted. To the captain that was all Desta's death was: a man going out of his turn.

*You will not be my forty, old man.* On the escarpment, to the priest across his shoulders. *You're too young to be my forty*, on the roof at Alexandria, to the boy. He had kept both of them out of his count. Then he had stood up on a wet board and made the fortieth himself.

For this. Zoser looked at it on the cloth. For a finger's length of wood in a priest's belly.

Desta had died for the old man's theft.

The rest came after it, leaf on leaf, and every one of them had been bound in the wrong order and now lay in the right one. The fast that Rome and Angeska had both called piety; the bread left on the board; the lamb at Cairo pushed away with a great noise about the lamb. The arms locked over the belly all the night before the friary search, while Zoser sat with the strip in his shamma believing the search was for him. The lamp in the doorpost that had laid itself over when the old man passed it on his way to the privy. The hall, where every candle had gone to the old man first, at his *yes*, and only then come round to Zoser. The face turned away from the gursha at Fasika: not pride. The shaking on the deck, older than the deck. The cramps. The bile in the bucket corner. The dungeon. Every road shut in their faces from Naples to this cell. And the long standing at the door between their two rooms at the friary, in the dark, looking.

*And every night it turned in me toward him.*

And Desta's eyes.

He had them whole; he thought he would have them on his deathbed. The long look Desta had given the candle, steady, unsurprised, a man who had seen a lamp do that trick before in a room with women in it. Then the eyes coming down off the flame and along the deck, and resting, for no longer than it takes to say a name, on the scrip at Zoser's thigh. Not on his face. On the scrip.

The old man had said it tonight, wondering: *He never once looked at me on that deck. Not even when the candle did.*

No. He had looked down the deck, with his death already in his legs, to see that the boy was there, and the scrip was where the boy was; and then the eyes had come up, and Zoser had met them.

*He was looking for me, to the end.*

He believed it.

Then he was on his feet.

He did not remember standing. He stood over the old man in the middle of the cell with his fists shut at his sides, and the rage went through him from the stomach to the roots of his teeth, the same white heat that had gone through him on the deck at *He was early*; and this time there was no captain's throat in front of him. There was an old man on his knees with bile in his beard.

"He carried you up the escarpment," Zoser said. "Two hours. On his shoulders. And you knelt behind the cask with it in your belly, and you watched him walk."

"Yes."

"You watched him walk to the end of the board for your wood."

"Yes."

"You let me think it was me." His voice was not his own; it was loud, it went off the wet stones and came back. "A month. I carried it. I kept away from every lamp in Rome. I thought the light had come down the deck for me, and he saw it, and he went into the sea for me, and I have been living on that, and it was *you*. Tamrat."

He had never in his life said the name bare. Not once, in all the years of carrying the man's books and his satchel and his temper. Abba Talka would have had him beaten for it. It went across the three paces of the cell and the old man's head came up at it.

"It should have been you on that board," Zoser said.

There. It was out of him, and it could not go back. He had thought it on the deck, with his eyes on the soft hollow under the captain's jaw, and had let it go through him and out. He had never said it. He had never said anything to the old man that he could not take back.

The old man did not look away from him.

"Yes," he said. "It should. Hate me. That is right, hate me, it is your right. But take it." He put out both hands, empty, palms up, toward the cloth and the boy at once. "I am begging you. I have not begged a man since the bronze door. Take it."

⁂

The rage went out of him the way it had come, all at once, and left him standing in the stink of bile and tallow, cold to the bone.

The thing beat on the cloth.

On the road Zoser had watched Angeska take the old man's wrist, at the ford and after, when the cramps were bad: two fingers laid in the groove below the thumb, her eyes away on nothing, her lips counting. He had never done it to anyone. He knelt again, because his legs would not hold him, and he took the old man's left wrist in his right hand and found the groove with two fingers, and kept his eyes on the wood.

The old man let him.

Under his fingertips the blood came up, slow and heavy, an old man's blood. On the cloth the wood lifted.

One. The wood. Two. The wood. Zoser counted under his breath in Amharic, by ones. Six. Seven. The beat in the wrist and the beat on the cloth came up together, not near together, not one chasing the other: together. Eight. Nine.

Nine against nine. The same beat.

"A year," the old man said. He was watching Zoser's fingers on his wrist. "It has had a year to learn mine."

Zoser did not let go of him.

"Pick it up," the old man said. "Please. Hold it. I want to see you hold it."

He did not want to. He picked it up with his left hand, because his right was on the old man's wrist, and he brought it in, slowly, from the length of his arm to his own breast, and held it there in his shut fist with a hand's breadth between his knuckles and his shamma. Under the shamma the strip lay along the scar, cool. The wood was warm in his fist and wet, and the smell of it came up into his face.

Nine against nine. Ten.

At eleven the wood was late.

Not much. A grain late, a hair. Under his right fingers the old man's blood came up, and in his left fist the wood came up after it, a breath behind. Twelve. Thirteen. It stumbled; it beat twice close together and then not at all for a space, and Zoser's own breath stopped in the space.

"In the morning," the old man said, "at the bridge, they will have the whole city out. Every eye in Rome will be on the fire, and on me in it. I know how to hold a crowd's eyes, I have done it all my life. I will give them something to look at."

Fifteen. Sixteen. It was between the two beats now, between the wrist and something else, and Zoser could not tell what the something else was. Eighteen. Then he could. It was in his ears. His own heart, loud from the rage still, going hard against the inside of his head, and the wood in his fist was walking toward it.

Twenty. Twenty-two.

"Listen to me, my —"

He stopped. His mouth stayed open. Zoser saw his eyes go across his face, searching it, the long-sighted eyes that could see a deacon's lost place across a whole church; and nothing came.

"Boy," the old man said. "Boy. Listen."

Twenty-five. Twenty-six. Under Zoser's right fingers the old wrist went on at its own pace, heavy, alone.

The old man's lips moved on nothing. Then his face cleared. He said it slowly, and looked at it after he had said it.

"Zoser."

Twenty-eight. Twenty-nine.

"Let me buy you time."

Thirty.

In his fist the wood beat with his own heart. Not near it. With it, stroke for stroke, the beat in his ears and the beat in his palm one beat; and it was hot. It had not been so hot in the old man's hand. Zoser's fingers opened of themselves a little against the heat and closed again, and he did not let it fall.

The old man looked at the shut fist, and at the boy's face above it.

"Clay keeps the shape of the hand after the hand is gone," he said. "Remember that I shaped you, and forgive the hand."

Zoser knew the word. He had had it for years, at Abba Talka's table and the imam's in Cairo and on the quay at Alexandria, in a voice that did not lower itself for anyone: *a mind like wet clay*. He had had it as a joke at the ford, through clenched teeth, in the black water. And he had had it last in the Cardinal's hall, out of the old man's own mouth in the old man's strangled voice, with gold eyes over it: *Clay carrying the potter.* The thing had worn the word to say what it was.

Now the old man had taken his word back from it, and given it again, to the one it had first been thrown at.

Zoser could not speak. He let go of the old man's wrist and knelt with the hot thing in his fist against his chest, and he bowed his head, which was all he had.

⁂

He wanted to see the signs.

It was a scribe's want, and it was stronger than the fear. Cut into the grain or grown there, crowded, sliding: he could not have them in this light, a pace and a half from the stub, and he had to have them. He got up and carried the wood across the cell to Angeska's corner, and knelt by the ledge.

From habit he kept his body back. A forearm of air between his breast and the flame, kept as he had kept it all through Holy Week; his arm out at its length; the wood on his open palm, wet side up. He brought the hand in toward the stub, slowly, to the light.

At a hand's breadth the flame lay down.

It went over toward his palm from its root, its whole small length down along the air toward the wood, the way ink runs to the low corner of a tilted leaf; and it stayed there, lying, and did not gutter. The yellow went out of it from the tip downward. What lay toward his hand was blue, the hard blue of the sky over the escarpment an hour before a frost, and in that blue, for one breath, every gold thread in the wood stood out at once, lit from inside its grain.

He snatched his hand back against his chest.

The flame stood up. It shook, and smoked, and was yellow, and stank of mutton, and was a stub of tallow on a ledge. He knelt with the wood hot in his shut fist and his heart going in his ears and in his hand, the one beat.

A hand's breadth. On the deck it had come the length of the ship in the captain's palm. Here a hand's breadth. It made no difference to what it was looking for.

So. It had been the wood.

⁂

*Custode.*

The guards' word. He had heard them call it to one another across the court at the friary, of themselves, with the hard c, and had kept the sound and nothing else. The old man asleep beyond the narrow door had said it in the dark in a voice that was not his, in a broken Latin he did not have awake. And at Aksum, the old man had told them tonight, the keeper of the sanctum had risen and stood at the door with his back turned to the holy place, *as if God had called him*, while the wood went out.

A sleeper who said a word Zoser had only by its sound, in another man's voice; and a keeper whose back was turned.

Zoser held the thought a moment, with the tallow smoke in his nose and the wood hot in his fist. Then he let it lie, unread, and turned to the work.

⁂

He had until dawn, and the stub would not last to dawn.

He could push the thing out through the slit in the south wall, into the moat. The slit was a hand wide and higher than his head; he could reach it standing on the bucket. It would go into the standing water and the weed and the town's dung and lie there, beating, until some Orsini boy netting eels brought it up. *Don't let it be found again.* He could put it down his own throat, as the old man had, and carry it out of here in the only bag no searcher's hands could reach. His stomach turned over at the thought and he did not finish it.

He unbuckled the scrip from his right thigh.

It had hung there since he was seven. Its leather was soft and dark with handling and stiff in places from the sea, and the lining still hung open inside it from end to end, as it had hung since the friary, the pale ends of his cut stitches standing up along the seam.

The needle was where it had been since the captain tipped their things on the floor: pushed through the leather at the root of the strap, with a tail of thread in its eye. Men looking for steel had run their fingers over it and found nothing worth their trouble. He drew it out. The tail was not enough. He took the hem of his shamma and worried a thread out of it with his fingernails, cotton, white, a long one, and then another, and twisted them together and threaded them.

Then he tore a strip from the same hem, a finger wide and a hand long, and laid the wood in it and rolled it. It beat through the cotton. He bound the roll round twice with the end of the strip and tucked it, so that it would not come undone.

Then he put his hand inside his shamma and took out the strip.

He had not had it out of the shamma since the morning of the hall. It came out cool against his fingers, rolled on its old folds round the little cross, and he kept it low, below his knee and well back from the ledge, the way he had kept his chest. He did not unroll it. The breath for the open hand came into him of itself, the first sign of the Line, ten dawns of habit at the friary window, and he shut his lips on it hard and let it out through his nose.

He laid the two rolls in the lining side by side, on the inner face, the side that lay against his thigh: his mother's and the thief's. They were nearly of a size. In the dark no hand would know one from the other.

He set the scrip across his knees, a forearm back from the flame, and began. The leather smelled of salt still, from the sea, and of his own thigh.

The sacristan at Lalibela had laughed at him the first time, a boy of seven sewing a roll into a bag with stitches a thumbnail long, and had taken the needle from him and shown him: small, even, close, each stitch the length of the one before it, the needle going in where the last came out, so that the seam lay down flat and ruled and could not be told from the leather's own. He had re-sewn it three times since, in that stitch. He sewed it now. In through the lining and the leather, out; draw it to the same tension; in.

"And you?" he said, without looking up from the seam. "What will you carry in the morning?"

It was the first thing he had said to the old man since the begging. He did not know why that was the thing.

Behind him, in the middle of the cell, the old man was a while answering. When he answered it was not in Amharic. It was in Ge'ez, the tongue of the altar, very quietly, the voice he had read the Easter lessons with.

"God will see to it."

Zoser's needle stopped, and went on.

He sewed. The lining closed behind the needle a stitch at a time. In the roll under his left fingers the wood beat with his heart, and his heart beat in his ears, and the beat of both came up through the leather into his hand on the seam. Twelve. Fifteen. He had cut twenty-three stitches at the friary, by the night-lamp and then by the red of the brazier. He put twenty-three back.

At the twenty-third he drew the thread tight and bit it off.

The beat stopped.

It did not fade. It did not slow, or go faint, or grow far off. It stopped between one stroke and the next, the stroke simply not coming; and his heart went on alone in his ears, and his hand on the leather felt nothing at all under it.

He put his palm flat to the lining. Nothing. He pressed. Under the leather, through the cotton, two hard rolls, nearly of a size, and nothing. The silence of it was worse than the beat had been. The beat had at least been a thing he could hate. He took his hand away, and a moment later found it there again, flat on the lining, listening, and took it away, and found it there again.

He wanted to see his stitches.

That was all he thought. He had sewn them by feel in a bad light and he wanted to see that they were small and even, that the seam lay flat, that he had not shamed the sacristan. He lifted the scrip in both hands and brought it to the stub, right up to it, the seam toward the light, until the leather was a finger's width from the flame.

The flame stood straight up beside it, small and yellow.

It did not lean. It did not blue. It burned on its wick in its pool of fat, and smoked, and stank, and lit his stitches for him: twenty-three of them, small and even and close, the seam lying down flat. It did nothing.

He looked at the stitches. They were good. He took the scrip back onto his knees and buckled it to his right thigh.

By the stub's last light he drew አ with one finger in the black grime of the floor beside the ledge, a stroke down, a stroke across, the short foot kicked out, and wiped it away with his palm, as his mother had wiped it from the mud-brick floor, so no priest sees.

*Dark.*

The stub drowned.

The wick fell over into its own fat and hissed there, and the flame went small and blue at the root, an ordinary dying, and went out. The smell came after it: hot mutton fat, and the smoke of the wick, sour, filling the cell and then thinning down the draught toward the grille. The torch in the passage laid its squares on the ceiling and lit nothing below them.

They waited.

Nobody lay down. Zoser sat with his back against the west wall beside the empty ledge, where he had sewn, and did not go back to his own place along the north wall. He could hear the old man in the middle of the floor: the breath going in long and rough and coming out with a catch, a man who has been sick and is not sick now. He could hear Angeska, nearer the old man than to her own corner, breathing so quietly that he had to wait between breaths to be sure of it. He could hear himself.

His hand was on the lining. He did not remember putting it there.

The moat came in through the slit with the draught: standing water, weed, the town's dung, and something green and rotten underneath that the warm days had started. The bile was still in the cell, and the dead tallow over everything. If the wet acacia was still there it was drowned under them. He found he was breathing to find it, and could not.

Once, deep in the night, the old man said something in Ge'ez too low to be words. Once Angeska moved, and the straw on the east wall rustled, and was still. Nobody spoke to anybody.

Grey came at the slit.

It came the way it had come every morning since the hall, a strip the colour of ash-water, high in the south wall; then the bells began, not all of them, the near ones, for prime. Under the bells, on the stair, boots.

More than one pair. A torch coming up behind them, its light swinging along the passage and over the ceiling, and ahead of the torch, before anything else, the long thick sniff at the grille. The bar came off. The door came open.

Cencio stood back against the passage wall with his lantern. Three men came in past him in the Orsini livery, bends of silver and red, the rose at the shoulder, and the first of them, a sergeant with a short grey beard, held the torch high and looked round the cell until he found the old man in the middle of the floor.

He said a word in the Roman speech. Zoser did not need it rendered.

The old man spoke first, though, and not to the sergeant. To his wife, in Amharic, low, without taking his eyes off the torch.

"Tell him I wish to speak before the fire. Ask him to let me speak."

Angeska got to her feet. She said it to the sergeant in his own speech, plainly, in the voice she had used for the tolls of the Capua bridge. Zoser heard the sound of it and none of the sense.

The sergeant laughed. It was not a cruel laugh; he had heard it asked before. He said something back over his shoulder to the men behind him, and they laughed too, and then he said it again to Angeska, slower, the way the Romans spoke to foreigners.

"He says you may say what you like," Angeska said. "To the friar."

The old man nodded.

He got up. It cost him; Zoser saw it go through him. The old arms came up of themselves to cross over the belly, and stopped halfway, and came down again. There was nothing there to guard. He did not reach for the wall. He stood straight in the middle of the cell in the shamma he had kept clean for the Pope since Naples, with the bile dried in his white beard, and two of the Orsini men took him by the arms, and he let them. He did not pull. He did not look at the door.

He looked at Zoser, sitting against the west wall with one hand flat on the scrip at his thigh.

And he smiled.

"Now," the old man said, "I will give them a question."
