# PART ONE — DIVISION

# CHAPTER 1

## ZOSER

*The Santa Maria, off Sicily — winter 1325*

Two sailors set their shoulders to the plank and ran it out over the sea. Its underside had been rubbed with tallow, and it went out through the lashings with a long wet squeal, like a pig hauled to a gate, until a man’s height of it hung over the black water and the rest lay along the deck at Zoser’s knees, close enough to touch.

“It’s begun,” Zoser said.

He had not meant to say it aloud.

“It began on the wharf,” said Desta, beside him, barely moving his mouth, “when the old man paid the fare. This is only the part where we get wet.”

From behind the water barrel at the end of the line came the priest’s voice, thin and furious. “Hold your tongue, wet clay.”

Zoser held it. He held it between his teeth until he tasted iron.

They knelt along the rail where the mate had put them, in the order he had put them: the priest’s wife nearest the mast, then Desta, then Zoser, and at the end, where the rail met the great cask of the ship’s drinking water, the priest, who had crept behind the cask when the plank came up from the hold and had not crept out. The deck was wet and tarred and cold through the wool of Zoser’s shamma, and it rose and fell under him in a long, slow, sickening breath that he had not learned, in eleven days at sea, to stop counting. The ship stank of tar, of rope rotting in its own salt, of the bilge that slopped beneath them full of fish heads and men’s water and something that had died down there and been left. Forward, a lantern swung on its hook, laid its light across them, and took it back.

Against his right thigh, under the shamma, under the sash, lay the scrip. It was old leather gone soft as a cheek. Inside it, sewn into the lining with his own stitches, small and even, a scribe’s stitches, was his mother’s strip: a finger’s length of parchment rolled around something hard, which he had carried for fifteen years and had never once unrolled. He could feel it now with the inside of his leg, the way the tongue feels a tooth.

*Let no priest see it.*

There were five sailors working down the line with the bags. There was a priest behind the barrel. And there was the captain, who walked toward them along the rail with a lit candle in his hand, and Zoser did not know what the captain was.

He wanted two things. He had wanted them since the plank came up out of the hold, and he had known for as long that he could not have both. He wanted the strip to stay where it was, sewn and silent and unseen. And he wanted the four of them to see the morning.

⁂

The captain’s name was Humbert. On the wharf at Alexandria he had looked as if he were made of driftwood and salt, his face cured dark by sun, his beard a tangle of iron wire, and he still looked so. But Zoser had stopped looking at the face on the second day. He looked at hands. He had always looked at hands: the abbot’s, laid flat on the gospel at the great feasts, the hands of the old man who had lifted him out of the ashes at seven and set him down among the books, and whom every boy in that house called only *Abba*; the emperor’s officer’s, drumming on a sealed letter he could not read. A face could be set like a lamp in a window. Hands told you what a man did all day.

Humbert’s hands had done nothing all their lives but turn pages.

The one that held the candle had nails pared to clean crescents and rubbed until they shone. There was no tar in the creases of the knuckles, no rope-gall across the palm. On the forefinger sat a signet of dark iron, flat-faced, and something was cut into the face of it, small and sharp-edged, too small to make out in that swinging light. Zoser marked it as he would have marked an unread rubric in the margin, to come back to. It was a hand that should have held a pen over a book in a cold room, and it held a ship’s candle as thick as a wrist, beeswax, the colour of old teeth. Zoser could smell it above the tar, the sweet smoke of it. On this deck, in this wind, the captain’s candle smelled of church.

The wind was rising out of the west. It took the hem of Zoser’s shamma and threw it over his knee. It lifted the captain’s grey hair from his collar and laid it flat again. It made the lantern’s hook groan.

Humbert walked with his back to it. He carried the candle low, in the lee of his own body, and his free hand curved round the flame on the windward side, the palm cupped and lit red at the edges, so that the light came up between his fingers onto his chin. Behind that hand the flame stood up from the wick, steady and yellow, a little smoky at the tip.

Zoser looked at it for as long as he could bear to, and then he looked west. Where the sun had gone down red an hour before, a line now lay across the sea, ruled as straight as a scribe rules his page before the first letter. Above the line the sky was the colour of a bruise three days old. Below it the water had gone white. It was coming. It was not coming quickly enough.

“Woman,” Humbert said, in Arabic, and passed the priest’s wife as if she were part of the mast.

He spoke the Arabic of the Alexandria wharf, a merchant’s Arabic, but he spoke it as the abbot read the lessons at Lalibela: every word set down whole and separate, to be counted. He did not stop for her. He did not ask her anything. He did not hold the candle near her face, or look at it while he passed. Behind him two sailors upended her bundle, kicked a comb and a shift and a little pot that smelled of cloves about the deck with their bare feet, found nothing, and moved on.

The priest’s wife did not watch them. She did not watch the plank either, though every other face on that deck turned to it as plants turn to the sun. She knelt with her hands folded in her lap and her eyes lowered, and from under her lowered lids she watched the captain’s hands.

Zoser saw it, because he was watching the same thing, and the coincidence made him colder. He thought she must be very afraid.

“I will tell you once more how this goes,” Humbert said. He had stopped by the hatch, where the mate stood holding a lantern and a fold of papers, and he took the papers from the mate without looking at them. “One of you will tell me the nature of Christ. One of you, I think, will tell it to me wrongly. Then we will see who walks.”

A seal hung from the lowest fold on a twist of cord. Zoser had seen many seals, the emperor’s, the abbot’s, the Patriarch’s from Egypt with its crowded Coptic letters, and never one like this. It was the colour of iron, and pressed into it was a key standing upright inside a circle.

He had seen it not a hundred heartbeats since, the size of a lentil, on the flat iron face of the ring on the captain’s forefinger. The same mark, in iron and in wax. Above the seal ran six lines in a Latin chancery hand, the letters crowded and leaning like men crossing a ford. Zoser could read the letters and not the words, except one he had seen chalked on bales in the customs yard: Alexandria. Then Humbert folded the papers and held them out behind him without looking, and the mate took them, and that was all.

He came down the rail to Desta.

⁂

Desta had already lost his sword. They had taken it first of all, before the bags, before the plank, while he stood with his arms held out from his sides as if to be measured for a robe; and now the mate wore it, hilt forward, through his belt. Without it Desta seemed smaller and older. Zoser had never before noticed that his hair was more grey than black, or that one of his ears was only half an ear, the top of it gone in some old fight and healed in a smooth curl like the edge of a leaf.

Humbert held the candle a hand’s breadth from Desta’s face.

He said nothing. He asked nothing. That was the first thing Zoser saw, and could not understand: the captain had promised them a question, and he stood before the soldier with his mouth shut. And he did not look at Desta’s face. His eyes stayed on the flame in the cup of his hand. The fingers round it were perfectly still; the little finger lay crooked out from the rest, as a scribe’s little finger lies when he is about to touch the pen to the page.

Desta knelt and let himself be looked at, or let the candle be looked at; Zoser could not tell which. Down the line, at the barrel, the priest had begun to breathe through his nose in short, whistling pulls.

Then the flame leaned.

It did not flicker. Zoser had watched a thousand flames flicker in the scriptorium in winter; he knew how a flame stoops under a breath from the door and rights itself. This was not that. The flame turned. It went over at the top, slowly, as a head turns on its neck when someone in the next room says your name, and it turned not toward Desta, though Desta was nearest, but past him, over the rim of the captain’s cupped fingers and out of their shelter, down the line: toward the place where Zoser knelt, and the barrel beyond him. And there it stayed, tipped, intent, as if it were listening for something. While Zoser watched, the yellow went out of its tip. What was left there was blue, the small clean blue that lives at the root of a flame on a censer’s coal.

The wind was blowing from the other side.

Zoser stopped breathing. He felt the scrip against his thigh as if it had grown hot, though it had not; he felt every stitch he had put into its lining, and the hard roll inside the strip, and he thought, very clearly, in his mother’s voice, the voice from the floor of the church, *Let no priest see it*. And he thought: it sees.

Desta was looking at the candle.

He looked at it once, a long look, steady and unsurprised: the look you give a thing you have seen do the same trick somewhere else, a long time ago, in a room with women in it. Then his eyes came down off the flame and went along the deck, and stopped, and rested, for no longer than it takes to say a name, on the scrip at Zoser’s thigh. Not on Zoser’s face. On the scrip.

Zoser met the look as it came back up. He took it as it was given. He did not know what it meant. He knew it was meant for him.

The mate had stepped past Desta with his lantern. Behind him a sailor with a ring in his ear had tipped out Zoser’s bag on the deck, the psalter and the ink-horn and the reed pens and the flat bread gone hard as tile, and was crouching now with his hand out toward the strap of the scrip, saying something to the mate in his own tongue, and the mate was nodding.

“One,” said Desta, loudly, in Arabic: the three words of it he had made Zoser teach him on the road out of Massawa, over and over, at the noon halts, until he had them, and no others. “United. Without mixture.”

No one had asked him.

The wind dropped for a moment, as it does before a squall, and in the lull the words went the whole length of the ship. The sailor’s hand stopped a finger’s width from the strap. Humbert’s eyes came up off the candle at last, and found Desta, and Zoser saw something move in that cured driftwood face that he would have called, on any other man, disappointment.

They were the words of their own church. Zoser had chanted them since before he could read. One nature in the Word made flesh, divine and human made one in the union, without mixture, without confusion, without division: the faith of Alexandria and of Lalibela, for which their fathers had borne being called heretics by the Greeks for eight hundred years. Desta had said them as a sentry gives the watchword at a gate in the dark, all at once, not waiting to be challenged.

“Wrong,” Humbert said. “Recant.”

Desta got to his feet.

The two sailors nearest him reached for his arms, and he moved his shoulders slightly, and they found that they did not want to touch him after all. He stood a moment on the wet deck in the light of the swinging lantern, a greying man with half an ear and no sword, smelling, as he always smelled, of wet wool and old sweat and the smoke of other men’s fires, and pulled his shamma straight at the shoulder, as he did at every gate before he went in to the King’s officers. Then he turned, and he was looking at Zoser.

“I was never good at counting,” he said, in Amharic, low, so that the wind took the end of it.

His face did not change. If it was a joke he did not let it be one. Zoser, kneeling, staring up at him, felt a terrible laugh rise into his throat, and swallowed it, and it went down like a stone.

Desta stepped up onto the plank.

He walked out along it as easily as he had walked the top of the cloister wall at home on feast nights, not fast, his arms loose. The plank bent under him and sprang. At the end of it he did not look down, and he did not look back, and he did not pause long enough for anyone to call him; he simply went on walking as if there were more plank, and there was not.

The sea took him with a sound like a door shut in another house.

No one moved. The man with the ring in his ear had forgotten the scrip and gone to the rail with the rest to stare at the water, which showed nothing, not a hand, not a head, only the foam drawing together over the place like a wound closing. The plank went on trembling a little at its tip. On the deck, where Desta had knelt, lay a small leather pouch on a thong, the one he wore under his shamma and opened at night; it had come off over his head when he stood, or he had taken it off. Zoser could not remember which.

⁂

“Was he wrong?”

Zoser heard his own voice say it, in Arabic, much too loudly, across the whole deck.

Humbert had not gone to the rail. He stood where he had stood, with the candle, and his eyes were on the candle again. Its flame still lay over, still blue at the tip, pointing down the line. Past the empty patch of wet deck where Desta had knelt. Past Zoser. On.

“He was early,” Humbert said.

He said it mildly, setting down each word, as one might remark that a guest had come to the feast before the bread was baked. Zoser heard the mockery in it as clearly as if Humbert had laughed, and something went through him, hot and white, from his stomach to the roots of his teeth.

He did not get up. He thought of it, with his eyes on the soft clean hollow under Humbert’s jaw. Then he thought of the strip, and of the woman by the mast, and of the barrel, and he stayed on his knees and let the white thing go through him and out, and it left him empty and very cold.

*He was early.* He would remember the words. He knew it already, as you know at the first touch that a burn will scar.

The wind came back, harder, carrying rain that was not yet rain, only a cold spit of salt off the tops of the waves, and a smell like iron filings, the smell of the sky before hail. The lantern on its hook swung through a wider arc. The ruled line in the west had thickened into a wall.

Humbert came on down the rail and stopped in front of Zoser, and held the candle to him in the cup of his hand.

“Scribe,” he said, in Arabic. “Who holds the keys of the kingdom of heaven?”

Zoser knew the answer the Latins wanted. He had seen it given: a tall friar at the abbot’s table two winters ago, shaping a key in the air with two thin fingers and turning it, as if he were opening a door in the smoke. He knew his own church’s answer too, and it was not the same, and he knelt there with both of them in his mouth and could not choose which to spit out.

Humbert was not waiting for either. His eyes were on the flame.

Zoser made himself look at it. It was harder than standing at the door of the burning when he was seven; then he had not known enough to be afraid. He waited for the flame to bow to his thigh, to point at the hard roll in the lining as a hound’s nose points.

It did nothing.

It stood straight up over him in the shelter of the captain’s palm. It did not lean toward him and it did not lean away. It was as still as a flame painted in the margin of a book. For a long breath it seemed to Zoser that the candle did not know he was there at all, that to it he was a coil of rope, a space in the line, and he felt, absurdly, ashamed, as if he had not been worth its looking. Then, at the top, slowly, as it had before, it went over again toward the end of the line, toward the barrel, and held, and blued.

Humbert’s eyes followed it. They did not come down to the scrip. They did not come to Zoser at all. The question went unanswered, and the captain did not seem to know he had asked it.

The sailor with the ring in his ear did not come back from the rail. He stood with one hand on a shroud, looking at the water where Desta had gone in, and once, quickly, he touched his forehead and his breast. No one else came for the strap. The psalter lay open on the wet deck with the rain spotting its page. Zoser could not reach for it. He could not move.

Humbert went on to the barrel.

⁂

The priest had made himself very small. He had wedged himself into the space between the cask and the rail with his knees drawn up to his chest and his old arms wrapped round them, and his shamma pulled over his head like a woman’s veil, so that all Zoser could see of him was the hem of it trembling and his bare feet, grey and cracked, the toenails thick as horn. He smelled of the vomit he had not been able to keep in on the first night out, and of sour fear, and under that, faintly, still, of the frankincense of home. Two sailors took him by the arms and drew him out like a cork, gently enough, laughing, and knelt him on the deck where the light fell.

Humbert held the candle to him.

The flame bowed.

There was no other word for it. It did not lean now, it bowed: out of the shelter of the captain’s hand altogether, from the base, as the deacons at home bow to the tabot when it is carried out under the umbrellas, all the way over, low, lower than any flame can lie without guttering out, and it did not gutter. It bowed to the priest. It bowed to his belly, to the place under the knotted sash where an old man’s paunch hangs, and it stayed there, bent, its whole length gone the colour of a vein under the skin. A thin thread of smoke went off it sideways, against the wind, toward him.

The priest did not move. He knelt with his mouth a little open and stared at the blue flame bent across the space between them, and he did not cover his belly with his hands, and he did not pray. He knelt as an ox kneels after the first blow of the axe, before the second.

“Turn out his bag,” Humbert said.

They turned it out. It was the priest’s own bag, embroidered by his first wife with a border of crosses faded to the colour of dust. Out of it on the wet deck came his hand-cross of brass, his horsehair fly-whisk, a censer with no coals in it, three dried dates, the bread he had not eaten that morning or the morning before, and the case of tooled leather that held the letter of the King of Kings to the Bishop of Rome. A sailor unrolled a hand’s length of the letter, the black Ge’ez and the red rubrics, held it up to the lantern, laughed, and tossed it back. Zoser had copied that letter in nineteen days. The emperor’s armies were holding the eastern frontier with spears and prayers, waiting for what was in that case to buy them an ally, and it rolled across the deck and fetched up in a pool of tarry water.

The priest watched none of it.

But as the hands went into his bag, and then, when the bag was empty, into his clothes, the pocket of his sash, the folds of the shamma at his breast, under his arms, as matter-of-factly as herdsmen going through a dead sheep for the tallow, Zoser saw him begin to shake.

Zoser had seen the old man in every humour that a long road and a short temper can show: furious, cold, sick, vain as a peacock; weeping at the Liturgy. He had never seen him afraid. And this was not a new fear. That was what struck Zoser. It was not the shaking of someone surprised. It had a shape. It went through the old body in a pattern, shoulders, then hands, then jaw, like water running in a channel it has cut for itself long ago, in some other place, on some other day, under some other hands. It was a tremor older than this deck.

The sailors found nothing in his clothes. They stood back. Nobody went back up the line.

The candle still bowed. Blue to the wick.

“Now, priest,” said Humbert. He spoke more gently than he had spoken to anyone. He had turned the candle a little in his fingers so that the drips fell away from his clean hand, and he looked at the priest over it, and then not at the priest, at the flame. “How many natures are there in the one Christ, after the union?”

The priest’s mouth opened. Nothing came out of it.

⁂

Zoser looked at the plank, and at the old man’s cracked grey feet, and the arithmetic did itself in him without his leave. Their fathers’ answer was the plank. Silence was the plank, only slower; *hold your tongue, wet clay*, and then the splash, and then the woman’s turn, and his own, and some sailor’s fingers in the lining of the scrip after all, and the strip going into the sea. And the other answer, Rome’s answer, he had in his mouth already. He had had it there for a year. He could taste it, like a coin.

He looked west. The wall was nearer. Under it the sea was white all the way across.

He had learned it the year the Latins came, at the bedside of a dying friar. That was all he let himself think of it now.

“Captain,” he said. “Let me answer for him.”

Humbert’s eyes left the flame and rested on him for the first time. They were pale, Zoser saw, much paler than the face, the grey of a winter sky over the Lasta ridges; and they were interested.

“For him?”

“He is old. He has been sick since Alexandria. I am his scribe. I know what he would say.”

“Do you.” Humbert considered him, his head a little on one side. Then he smiled, and Zoser saw that his teeth were very good. “If you answer wrongly, you both walk.”

“Yes.”

“Then answer.”

Zoser drew a breath that stank of tar and wet wool and the hot sweet beeswax, and he began, in Latin.

It came out of him as a run of syllables that ended in *patres*, and then another that rose and turned and came down again, and he did not know what either of them meant. He knew only how they went. The friar had been tall, and young, no older than Zoser, with a peeling nose and a cough that lived low in his chest, and a name Zoser had never been able to say. Of the six who came up from the coast that year to tell the King of Kings that his church was a daughter who had strayed, the highlands had killed half inside a season: the fever, the cold, the bad water at the fords.

*Unum eundemque.* That he knew. One and the same. The friar had held up one finger, then laid a second beside it and pressed the two together.

After that the sense went out of it again and left only the drum. The friar had drilled him as the cantors drill the deacons at home, call and answer. *Credo in unum Deum*, the boy would say, and Zoser would give it back to him with the boy’s own fall at the end of it, until the boy smiled; and then the next line, and the next, and so through the Creed and on into the long Definition of their council at Chalcedon. Zoser had written the sounds down in Ge’ez letters on the blank last leaves of his psalter, and at night on his mat he said them over as he had once said over the hundred and fifty psalms.

The priest was staring at him. Zoser did not look at him. He looked at the candle, still bent blue toward the old man’s belly, and past the candle at the line of white water in the west.

*Deum verum et hominem verum.* That was not the quarrel. Their own fathers said as much. The quarrel was coming. He could feel it coming in the shape of the sentence, as you feel in the dark the step that is not there.

He had told the abbot that he learned it to refute them. Abba had approved, and Zoser had told himself the same thing many times since, in Abba’s voice.

*In duabus naturis.*

In two natures. The words that had broken the world. He said them clearly, so that they went the length of the deck as Desta’s had, and at the barrel he heard the priest make a sound like a foot coming down on a nail.

There. It was said. It could not be taken back.

And under the shame of it, deeper, in a place he did not like and would not have shown to any confessor, something else moved. It was hard and small and it had a taste to it. *Wet clay*, it said. Clay with no mind of its own, clay you could hold up at the imam’s table in Cairo to be laughed at in four languages; let him owe the clay his life. Let him kneel there in his own vomit and listen to his scribe buy his old life back with the words he could not find. Let him remember it every time he opened his mouth to say *clay*. Zoser was ashamed of it. It warmed him all the same, as a coal warms the hand that is closing round it.

*Inconfuse*, he said. *Immutabiliter. Indivise. Inseparabiliter.*

Without confusion, the first one; the friar had shaken two cups of water together to show him. Without division, the third. The others he had only by their weight. Desta had said *without mixture*. Zoser heard the two together, Desta’s three hard-won words of Arabic and the friar’s soft Latin, the same guards set round the same mystery; and the whole eight hundred years, the whole plank, came down to a number. One. Two. *I was never good at counting.* He nearly stopped. He did not stop.

Because the wall in the west was still too far.

He came to the end of the Definition, and he did not end. He went on into the Creed as the Latins said it, and when he reached the Spirit he gave the Spirit their added word, *Filioque*, which his own church had never said, and he gave it calmly, as if it were his.

There had been another reason under the one he gave Abba. He had not taken it out and looked at it then, and he did not take it out now. Only, when Abba found him in the library in the spring, bent over a page of Coptic, and stood behind him a long while without speaking, and then put a hand on his shoulder and said that he would go to Rome with the embassy, because Rome must be answered and a scribe with a good hand was needed, and *I did not choose lightly*, there had been in Zoser, under the fear, a feeling like a door opening onto a lit room. He had shut it at once.

He went from the Creed back into the drill, taking both parts himself in the dying boy’s voice, call and answer, *Credo in unum Deum*, and the answer, and the call again, the Latin falling out of him in shapes he knew only by their weight on the tongue. A sailor told him to shut his mouth, in Arabic, and he did not. He went into the Ave. *Gratia plena.* He knew that one. He said it to the bent blue flame.

Humbert raised one finger. The clean nail caught the lantern light, and the iron face of the signet under it.

Zoser saw the finger, and saw the white water behind it, very close now, and the black loom of a sea standing up out of the dark higher than the rail, and he raised his voice over the shrouds and went straight on into the Latins’ Our Father, a long falling string of sounds whose shape he had by heart and whose sense he had never been given, his eyes on the wave, talking, talking, paying the words out in front of him like a rope—

The sea came over the rail.

It came black, then green in the lantern light, then roaring cold. It struck Zoser across the chest and dragged him a body’s length along the deck on his back, and filled his mouth and nose with salt, and drew back hissing through the scuppers, pulling at his legs, at his shamma, at the scrip; he caught the rail with both hands and held on, and felt the strap of the scrip pull tight against his thigh like a hand gripping it, and hold.

When he could see again there was nothing to see. The lantern was out. The candle was out. Everywhere was dark and roaring and full of men’s voices shouting in their own tongue, and the ship heeled over under him as if it meant to lie down in the sea.

⁂

They did not drown. The squall passed over them and went on east toward the land, and behind it the rain came, hard and straight, and then less hard. Men ran barefoot over Zoser in the dark. Zoser lay against the rail with his arms round a stanchion and his cheek on the tar and the rain on his back and coughed up the sea.

When he could lift his head he could make out only shapes, black on a black that was a little less black where the sea broke white beyond the rail. By the barrel something was breathing in short whistling pulls, and an iron hoop creaked under two hands that would not let it go. Alive, then. By the mast there was a shape that had not been dragged at all, kneeling where it had knelt, its head turned aft, toward the place where the captain’s voice had last been.

Then the captain’s voice was above him. Zoser could see nothing of him but an outline against the paler dark, and the smell came down to him through the rain: wet wool, and hot wax gone cold, the snuffed wick still smoking somewhere near his face.

“Good,” Humbert said. “You lie like a scholar.”

He turned away to shout something forward, in a language that was not Arabic, and the men answered him.

Zoser stayed on his knees by the rail and let the words find their place in him. They knew where to go.

He found that he was weeping, and that it did not matter, because it was raining.

He heard the sailors draw in the plank: the long squeal of it coming home through the lashings, then a thump, and the scrape of it being stowed under the boat amidships, like an oar. When it was gone the rail under his hands was only a rail.

The outline came back along the deck. Something touched Zoser’s hand, and he closed his fingers on it: a fold of paper, small, dry at the edges where the rain had not yet found it. He could not see what was on it.

“You’ll want the road out of Naples,” Humbert said. “The men on the coast will rob you. The men on the road will rob you more. Take it, scholar.”

“And our soldier’s sword?” he said.

He could not see the mate, but somewhere aft in the dark he heard the sword, the scabbard knocking against wood as someone moved.

“The sword stays with me,” Humbert said. “If you need passage again—if you need anything again—ask for me in Genoa. Ask for Humbert. Anyone on the mole will know where I am.”

He said his own name as a man in Lalibela says *the abbot*.

Then his steps went forward into the dark to see to his ship.

Zoser put the map inside his shamma, unread. After a while he crawled, because he did not trust his legs, to the place where Desta had knelt, and felt along the deck for it with his hands.

The pouch was still there. The sea had not taken it. It had caught by its thong in a crack between two deck-planks and lay in a skin of water, a small thing of leather, greasy from Desta’s hands, the mouth drawn shut. Zoser freed the thong and held the pouch to his face. Through the salt and the wet leather he smelled what was in it, roasted barley and roasted chickpeas, no spice, the plain dry smell of every hillside in the highlands where people sit down at noon and hold out a handful to whoever is beside them. *Kolo.* Desta had carried it from Lalibela, and opened it every night, and held out his hand.

Zoser did not open it. He put the thong over his own head and the pouch inside his shamma, against his breastbone, where it lay cold and then less cold.

Forward, a sailor was relighting the lantern. Zoser heard the strike of the steel, three times, four, and saw the spark catch, and the light came up yellow and steady on its hook and laid itself across the wet deck: across the empty place by the rail, across the dark wet crack where the pouch had been, across Zoser’s hands. His bag was gone, and his pens, and his ink. His psalter he had found jammed against the stanchion, swollen with the sea, and he held it shut against his chest. He put his hand down over his right thigh, over the shamma, and felt through the wet wool the soft old leather of the scrip and the hard roll inside its lining, still there, still sewn, still shut, and he let out his breath.

In that light he could see the priest. The old man had not moved from the barrel. He was staring at the deck where the candle had bowed to him, his lips moving without sound. The shaking had stopped. His hands had come away from the barrel’s hoop and lay one over the other on his belly, flat, as if over a wound. He did not say *thank you*. He did not say *clay*. Zoser found that he had been waiting for one or the other, and that the waiting had a taste too.

The priest’s wife had come to sit by her husband. She put her shawl round his shoulders. Over his head, for a moment, her eyes met Zoser’s in the lantern light, and they were dry and steady and quite unafraid, and then she looked down.

Humbert was coming aft again, past him, toward the helm. He was drying his hands on a cloth as he came, one finger at a time, the nails first, the iron ring last. As he drew level with Zoser he slowed, and stopped, and stood for a moment looking down at him with the lantern behind his head, so that his face was dark and the light came round the edges of his hair.

“Your mother burned black,” he said, almost tenderly, “didn’t she, boy?”

Then he turned his head and spat over the rail, the way a man spits out a name.
