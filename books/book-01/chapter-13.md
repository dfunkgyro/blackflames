# CHAPTER 13

## ZOSER

*Castel Sant'Angelo — Passion week to Easter night, 1326*

The grey came down through the slit before any bell, and the first word of the Line was already behind Zoser's teeth.

Ten dawns he had knelt to it under the friary window, and his body had learned the hour before he had: the grey, the cold, the hand going up under the shamma to the roll, the breath drawn for the open hand. The breath was in him now. He shut his lips on it, hard, until he felt his teeth through them, and let it out through his nose.

*Tu.*

The old man's finger a finger's width from his breastbone; eight flames coming round to point at him. He had said his mother's line aloud every morning, and on the tenth morning the thing in the priest had pointed at the place he said it from. He did not need the Cardinal's Latin to know what he had done.

The strip lay flat along the scar where it had lain since the friary, unsewn, and it was cool. It had been cool all night, and he could not tell whether that was mercy or waiting. He would not read it again. He made the promise to her, not to God, and took his hand away from his chest and put it on the floor.

The floor was stone, and wet. So was everything.

He had paced the room in the dark with his hands out: three of his paces from the wall at his head to the wall where the old man lay, four from the door to the slit, the fourth one short. Now the grey showed him what his hands had found. Small squared stones, each set on its corner, row crossing row in a net, the whole wall a leaf ruled by someone who wanted every line to cross. They sweated. His palm came away from the nearest one wet, smelling of damp lime and, under the lime, of earth that had never been in the sun.

High in the wall opposite the door, the south wall, a slit a hand wide showed a strip of sky the colour of ash-water. No bridge, no river; only sky, and the smell that came in with it, standing water and weed and the town's dung: the moat. The draught came down from it across the floor, over his feet, and out past his head by the grille in the oak door, where torchlight from the passage lay in squares on the ceiling.

He lay along the north wall with the door at his head. In the south-west corner Angeska sat with her back to the west wall and her knees drawn up under her shawl, beside a ledge of stone no higher than her knee, with nothing on it. Her eyes were open. Along the east wall, on the only straw, the old man lay on his back with his arms crossed on his belly, and his breath went up from his beard in the cold and broke apart in the draught.

"I said yes," the old man said, to the ceiling.

Nobody answered. The torch in the passage spat.

"In the hall. The Sicilian asked me, and I said yes, and then I was on the floor and the boy was holding me." He turned his head on the straw, not to Zoser. To his wife. "What happened between?"

"You were ill, husband."

"Ill." He lay with it. "Before the Latins."

He did not look at Zoser. He looked at the slit, at the ash-coloured sky in it, and his thumb began to go round in the hollow of his other palm, and went round until the bell came.

⁂

On the second day the captain came with their things.

Zoser knew him before the bar was off by the keys: the ring hung from his left hand and he worked the lock with his right, and when the door came open the right thumbnail was split down its middle, as in the friary, as on the morning of the summons. He tipped a sack onto the floor of the cell, and the stink of the friary came out of it, beans and old wool, and he said three words to the wall above their heads and went out.

Everything had been opened. The old man's satchel had been turned inside out and its seams cut. Angeska's bundle had been knotted again by someone who did not know how; she found the little pot of cloves in it and put it to her nose before anything else, and closed her eyes.

There was no knife.

He went through his scrip twice. His knife was gone, the one he had cut his own stitches with by the night-lamp. The old man's razor was gone from its loop of leather. The Latins had gone through their bags for steel and found it all.

The psalter had come back. The reed had come back. The shell of soot-ink had come back with its plug of rag pushed in, and when he drew the rag the ink in it had gone thick and stiff, but it was there.

He ran his thumb along the scrip's seam at the root of the strap, and stopped.

The needle was where he had left it in the friary, pushed through the leather, a tail of thread still in its eye. Men looking for blades had gone over it with their fingers and felt nothing worth their trouble. He left it there.

He had been patted down himself in the hall, two guards running their hands over his ribs and his hips and his thighs for a hilt, and their hands had gone over the strip flat under the shamma and gone on. Nobody had put a hand on Angeska at all.

"They have taken my razor," the old man said. He was turning the empty loop over in his fingers. Then, without lifting his eyes: "Tell the boy they have taken my razor."

"He can hear you, my lord."

"Tell him anyway."

Angeska turned her face to Zoser. "They have taken his razor," she said, in exactly the voice in which she had once rendered to him the tolls of the Capua bridge.

⁂

The turnkey was called Cencio. He told them so the first evening, through the grille, slapping his own chest, *Cencio, Cencio*, and sniffed. He had a Holy Week cold. His nose was red and wet at the end and he wiped it on the back of his wrist, and every time he came to the grille they heard him before they saw him, the long thick drawing-in at the bars.

He had a candle to sell. Not sell: rent. He held it up in the torchlight, a stump of yellow tallow as thick as two fingers, and along its side, with the point of a little knife, he had cut nicks at even spaces, the width of a thumb apart. He laid his finger on the top nick and said a word, and laid it on the next and said it again, and rubbed his finger and thumb together under the grille.

"A Roman penny," said Angeska, "for each mark that burns."

She paid from the purse at her girdle, standing at the grille with her back to them: four small dark coins counted into the turnkey's palm, then one taken back and two smaller given in its place. Cencio counted them again in his palm, and sniffed, and passed the candle through the bars. She set it on the knee-high ledge in her corner, and it stayed there.

Zoser looked at it from his place along the north wall, two and a half paces off. It burned low and yellow and stinking of mutton fat, and its smoke went sideways in the draught and broke up before it reached the ceiling. An ordinary flame. He kept the width of the cell between it and his chest and did not think about why.

Cencio stayed at the grille that first evening, glad of faces, and talked. Zoser caught the broken shape of it and nothing else. Once the turnkey pointed upward, at the stone over their heads, the whole weight of the place; then he drew a sword out of the air, held it up, and slowly put it back into a sheath at his hip that was not there; and then he crossed himself, quickly, twice.

"He says the castle is the Angel's," Angeska said. "Once there was a plague in Rome, and a pope went in procession to pray it away, and on the roof of this place he saw an angel putting up its sword. And the plague stopped."

Cencio nodded at her, sniffing, pleased, and crossed himself again.

Roman talk, Zoser thought. Every church in this city had a saint's foot or a saint's tooth or an angel on its roof, and a man at the door to tell you.

⁂

Until Palm Sunday the old man did not speak to him.

He spoke about him. He lay on his straw along the east wall and said things to the ceiling, in Amharic, which they all three had; and his wife, in her corner, gave them across the cell to Zoser, word for word, without colour, and gave Zoser's answers back.

"Tell the boy the water stinks."

"He can hear you, my lord."

"Tell him anyway."

"The water stinks," said Angeska.

The jar stood by the door, at Zoser's head. He had filled the cup from it. "It comes in from the moat," he said. "Everything does."

"He says it comes in from the moat."

"Tell him I have a nose."

On the third night: "Tell the boy that his feet are in my straw."

"Your feet are in his straw."

Zoser drew his feet in against his body on the cold stone. In the morning they had gone back to where they had been, and the old man said nothing, and that was worse.

On the fourth day, over the bowls: "Tell the boy he chews like a mule."

"You chew like a mule," said Angeska.

Zoser stopped chewing.

She looked at her husband for a while, and then said in the same flat voice, "He has stopped. Now he does not chew at all," and went back to her own bowl, and Zoser saw the old man's beard lift at one side before he turned his face to the wall.

It was nearly funny. A week ago it would have been.

He knew what the grievance was: a lie put into the Sicilian's ear in Arabic, and the old man left standing before the Latins with two roads open, both chosen by someone else. He had words ready for it. *You saved her. I am sorry. You were braver than I was.* Each one came up to his teeth, and he heard it before he said it, not in his own voice but in the gold-eyed voice, out of the old man's mouth, kept and handed back. Every tender thing said near the priest had been taken in and kept. Whatever kept them lay three paces away, on the straw.

So he said nothing at all. The old man looked at him across the cell with his long-sighted eyes, and Zoser took the look for hatred and lowered his own.

"Tell the boy," the old man said on the Saturday, "that he need not look down at me from over there. It is three paces. I can see him well enough."

Angeska told him.

He went back over it at night, line by line, looking for the error. The plank. The ford. The hall. The loft at Naples, where he had sat on the boards beside the old man in the dark over Desta's grains and said the one thing he had never said again; and Angeska had been across the whole loft from him, against the far wall, asleep by then, or so her breathing told him, for he had listened to it. Nobody had been near him in that loft but the priest.

⁂

On Palm Sunday the bells began before light and did not stop.

They came in through the slit from every side, high and cracked and deep, the whole of Rome ringing for a Pope who was in Avignon. The draught brought the moat in with them, worse than before, because the days had turned warmer and the standing water had begun to work: green, sweet, rotten.

From Palm Sunday the old man did not even speak about him. He spoke to his wife, and to the wall, and on the Tuesday of that week, to the turnkey.

Cencio came for the bucket in the south-east corner with his sleeve over his nose. The old man had been bringing up bile into it in the nights, and the corner stank of it, yellow and bitter. Cencio held the bucket out from his body and talked over it, cheerful, and jerked his head at the walls.

"He says this was a tomb," Angeska said, "of an old emperor who built it for himself. And they burned the emperor and threw his ash to the wind. And the lid of his coffin, which was a beautiful stone, a pope took for his own tomb, at the Lateran."

Cencio nodded, satisfied, and went out with the bucket. Through the grille they heard his long sniff going away down the passage.

"They keep their emperors in pieces too," said the old man to the closed door, in Amharic, and to nobody in the room.

⁂

On the Wednesday, which the Latins called the day Judas sold his master, a notary of the Capitol came to the door.

They heard him on the stair: a voice complaining of the stair, of the damp, of his shoes. Then Cencio's lantern came up to the grille, and its tallow smoked, and behind it a face. A narrow man with a fur collar gone bald at the nape, holding a sheet tilted to the lantern. His hands were what Zoser looked at: the second finger of the right stained to the first knuckle with gall gone brown; a heavy seal-ring on the thumb; the nails long and clean. When the draught from the slit went through the grille past him with the moat on it, he put the back of his left hand to his nose and held it there and read through it.

It was Latin. It went fast and high through the nose, the voice of a man who read the same thing many times a week and wanted his dinner.

Zoser listened for what he owned: the few sounds he had kept from the Sicilian's mutter over the minute. Out of the stream they came, one by one.

*Tamrat.* That was his master.

*Presbyterum.* That was his master too.

*Angeskam.*

Then the notary's eye, going along his line, stopped on something. Zoser saw it stop. It rested on one place on the paper, and his lips held still for the space of a breath. Then the eye went on, and the voice went on, and there was no third name. Zoser waited for it, and it did not come, and he thought the Latins did not trouble to name a servant.

> SENTENCE OF THE JUDGES OF THE CAPITOL, READ AT THE GRILLE OF THE CUSTODY IN THE CASTLE OF THE ANGEL, ON THE WEDNESDAY OF HOLY WEEK, 1326
>
> In the name of the Lord, amen. We, the judges of the Capitol, sitting for the Senator of the City, the matter having been referred to us by the Lord Giovanni, Cardinal Deacon of the Holy Roman Church, and the witnesses heard, find that the crime of sorcery was committed openly in the presence of the said Lord, in the hall of the Friars Minor at Ripa, in the week of the Passion. Wherefore we pronounce Tamrat, *presbyterum*, of the Ethiopians, guilty of the said crime; who, *relaxatus curiae saeculari*, is *igni tradendus post octavam Paschae*.

The lantern guttered in Cencio's fist and stank. The notary turned his face from the moat for a breath and turned it back.

> Item, Angeska, called his wife, is to be held in this custody at the pleasure of the said Lord Cardinal. Given at the Capitol, on the Wednesday of Holy Week, in the year of the Lord 1326, in the ninth indiction, the Apostolic See being at Avignon.

The notary folded the paper in three, already turning, and said something to Cencio that made the turnkey laugh, and went down the stair complaining of the stair.

Cencio stayed. He set the lantern down in the passage and passed the bowls through the hatch at the foot of the door one at a time, and as each one went down on the wet stone he counted it aloud. *Una. Due. Tre.* Three bowls, a grey lump of bread on each: no cumin, no black seed, no fenugreek, bread that tasted of the mill and nothing else. Then he took his lantern and went, sniffing.

"Well?" said the old man from his wall. "What did the Latin want?"

Angeska did not answer. She took up the first bowl and crossed the cell with it in both hands and put it into his, and went back to her corner and took up her own, and sat with it on her knee, not eating. She sat very still. Zoser had seen her still before, on the wharf and in the hall. This stillness had no face in it for anybody.

"They read a great deal to say nothing," said the old man, and broke his bread.

Zoser ate. One sound of the notary's he had not owned before had stayed in him, and he did not know why that one. *Igni.* Short, with a lift at the end. He turned it over against his teeth and put it away with *custode*, among the words he had by their sound only.

⁂

On Holy Saturday they kept the vigil.

At home all Lalibela would be standing in the trenches round the churches from dusk, every soul with a taper; and at the hour the priest of Bet Medhani Alem would come out of the sanctuary with the light raised in his hand and cry the first line, and the fast of fifty-five days would break. In the cell there was the straw, and the wet net of stones, and Cencio's tallow on Angeska's ledge. They had the words. The words would do.

Toward the hour the old man sat up against his wall. It cost him; Zoser saw the cramp go through him and his arms tighten over his belly. Angeska looked at the candle, and then at her husband.

"The light, husband."

"My hands shake." He held one out across the cell to show her, and it did. "The cramps. If I hold it, I will drop it, and Cencio will charge us for the floor."

He did not get up. He did not move toward the ledge at all.

Then her eyes came to Zoser, and his body answered before he could think: it drew back against the north wall. If the priest would not hold the light, the scribe should. He could not. He knew what light did when it wanted him. He sat with his back to the wet stones, and across the cell the old man watched him not move.

*Too proud*, Zoser thought, *to hold the light in a Latin prison.* Or afraid of his own hands. And the old man's look came to its own conclusion about him, and it was not kind.

Angeska got up.

She took the candle off the ledge, holding its foot in her palm, and sat down again in her corner with it in her lap, both hands round it, a hand's breadth from her own belly. The flame shrank in the draught from the slit and stood up again, and its smoke went to pieces above her head. It was yellow. It burned. She held it without effort, and Zoser could not take his eyes from it and could not have said what he was watching for.

The old man drew breath, and gave the first line from his wall, in the tongue of the altar, in his great voice, the one he had preached to the King of Kings with.

"*Kristos tense'a em-mutan.*"

"*Be-abiy hayl we-seltan*," said Angeska, out of her corner, with the candle in her lap; and Zoser came in after her a breath late.

"*Asero le-Seytan.*"

"*Ag'azo le-Adam.*" On the name her fingers round the candle loosened, and took hold again.

"*Selam*," said the old man, and lifted his own hand toward them across the three paces of floor, palm out, where in Bet Medhani Alem he would have turned to the people.

"*Em-ye'eze-se.*" And at that, *from now on*, Zoser's hand went of itself to the pouch at his breast. It was over. Fifty-five days; it was over.

"*Kone fesseha we-selam.*"

They said it three times, as it is said. Angeska had every response in its place, with the long vowels a deacon learns at the altar, and gave them in a low clear voice; and neither of the men found it strange tonight. It was only right that someone in the room had the words whole.

Somewhere over the river, in the Borgo, a cock crowed.

⁂

They gathered at the cloth in the middle of the floor.

It was Angeska's shawl, spread on the stone: the old man at its east edge; Zoser at its north side; Angeska at its south-west corner with the candle still in her lap, giving the cloth what light there was. On it lay the last bread, kept dry in her bundle against the cloves, so that it smelled of cloves under the smell of the mill.

The old man looked at the bread, and then at Zoser, straight, for the first time in ten days.

"You chose my fear for me," he said. "You did not ask."

"There was no time."

"You had time to choose the words."

Zoser did not answer.

He had had time. He had chosen them in one breath in the Cardinal's hall, *my master ordered the softening*, and chosen well; that was the charge. He had decided what the old man would be able to bear, and put it on him before Latins, and called it saving her. The old man was right.

He drew the pouch out of his shamma.

He had not opened it since the fast began, two days out of Montesarchio, in the snow. The thong had been knotted and unknotted until it shone. He untied it. The smell came up into the cell, through the moat and the tallow and the bile in the corner: roasted barley, roasted chickpea, no spice in it, a clay pan over dung and juniper, a hillside at noon. He poured it out on the cloth beside the bread, a small brown heap, and some of the chickpeas rolled, and he stopped them with his finger.

If the thing in the priest kept everything, it would keep this. He looked at the old man across the cloth, and decided.

*Then let it keep this.*

"This is Desta's," he said. "Desta."

The candle in Angeska's hands stood still.

The draught had not stopped. It came down from the slit as it had come all night, and it moved the edge of her headcloth against her cheek; Zoser saw the cloth lift and fall. But the flame did not shrink, and it did not tear. It stood up from the wick, straight, yellow to the point, the way flames stand in a shut room, and its smoke went up in one thread and did not break. Zoser held his own breath so as not to be the thing that broke it.

Then the breath after the name was over, and the draught had the flame again and bowed it small and pulled its smoke to pieces, and it was only the candle.

Nobody said anything about it. The old man was looking at the kolo.

"He carried me up the escarpment," he said. "Above the salt. Two hours, across his shoulders, like a sack. I told him to put me down the whole way." His beard lifted. "At the top he put me against a rock and he said, *I have carried heavier barley, Qess. It complained less.*"

Angeska laughed, under her breath.

"We will give him his share," said Zoser.

So they divided it by four, and to divide it they had to count it.

Zoser counted first, aloud, in Amharic, by twos, moving the grains with one finger into four small heaps, as Desta had counted out the muleteers' grain at the escarpment camps, two to a hand, never looking up. He came to the end and said his number.

"No," said the old man.

He leaned in over the cloth and gathered the four heaps into one again with the side of his hand, and counted them himself by fives, in the old numbers, the Ge'ez ones the deacons learn for the feast-days, under his breath, sour and quick. He came to the end and said his number. It was one more.

"You lost one," said the old man. "You were always losing one."

Angeska set the candle down on the stone by her knee, a moment, and leaned over and counted. She did not say it aloud. Her lips did not even move. Her finger went through the grains so fast that Zoser could not follow it, and then she took the candle up into her lap again and said a number, and it was the same as the boy's.

The old man looked at her. She looked back. Nobody settled it.

"He would have laughed at us," Zoser said.

"At Massawa," said Zoser, "the waves. He got to thirty-nine and stopped, and said the sea had more than he could count, and they were not his to count anyway."

"He sang to a camel," said Angeska. "In the Afar country. A baggage camel that would not kneel, that screamed at every man who came near. He sat down in front of it and sang it a soldiers' song, all the verses, some not fit for a priest's wife, and he did not stop for me. It knelt in the middle of the worst one."

"I heard," said the old man. "I was on the other side of the camel."

They took Desta's heap last, and Zoser swept it into his palm with the side of his hand, every grain, and poured it back into the pouch, and drew the thong, and knotted it twice.

"For the road," he said, and put it back inside his shamma, against his breastbone.

They ate, three grains at a time, the barley cracking between the back teeth, smoke and earth, and the bread torn with it. After fifty-five days the grain made Zoser's stomach ache, and he went on.

The old man ate his share one grain at a time. Halfway through he stopped with a chickpea in his fingers and frowned at it, the frown he wore for a deacon who had lost his place.

"Every night on the road he ran his hand down the boy's strap," he said, "end to end, and tried the buckle. Like a man counting." He put the chickpea in his mouth. "I thought he wanted your bag."

Zoser's hand had gone to the strap on his own right hip. He took it away.

"And on the escarpment," the old man said, "when he put me down. I said to him, you will be the death of me yet, carrying me like barley. And he said: *You will not be my forty, old man.*" He chewed. "I thought it was insolence. It was insolence."

On the roof at Alexandria Desta had said it to Zoser the other way: *too young*. Zoser laid the two side by side and did not know what he was looking at.

"Thirty-nine he had," the old man said, idly, picking at the cloth for a grain he had dropped. "What were they called?"

Nobody knew.

Zoser had never asked. Desta had carried every one of them on the road behind his teeth, and gone off the plank with them, and there had been no one to hand them to.

He tore a piece of the bread and folded it round a pinch of his own grain, as at home, and leaned across the cloth with it held up to the old man's mouth: gursha, the mouthful given by hand, which a son gives a father at a feast to do him honour.

The old man looked at the hand, and turned his face away, to his wall.

Zoser held it there a moment more, and then ate it himself.

But it did not end the hour. Angeska told the camel again, with the worst verse in it, and the old man said the soldiers sang it differently in Gojjam, and sang it so, very quietly, in his cracked great voice, with his eyes shut; and Angeska put her face down on her knees and shook; and Zoser sat at the edge of the cloth with the taste of roasted barley in his mouth, and for the length of an hour the three of them were a family again.

⁂

The old man slept.

He had lain down where he sat, on his side at the east edge of the cloth with his arms over his belly, his face to his wall, and gone off between one word and the next. His breathing came long and rough. Angeska sat on at her corner of the cloth with the candle in her lap.

She broke a crumb from what was left of the bread and held it up to the light between finger and thumb.

"*Germen*," she said.

"*Germen*."

"A seed. And the green blade that comes up out of the seed. The Latins have one word for both." She put the crumb in her mouth. "They are not wrong."

He got out the psalter.

To write he needed the light, and the light was in her lap. He leaned toward it and stopped where he always stopped now, with a forearm of air between his breast and the flame, and held the psalter out at the length of his arms into the light, his body back out of it. Angeska watched him do it and said nothing. He loosened the ink with a drop from the water jar. It smelled of mutton fat and soot.

The first leaf. The child's አ in its corner; his own beside it; *clavis*, *ostium*, *vas* below, each in two hands, with their glosses. He wrote *germen* under *vas*, in the Latin letters, and then again in his own, ገርመን, and beside them, smaller, *seed*; and then, because she had said it meant both, *the blade that comes up*. The ink went on brown and slow.

Then he had to pay.

He had paid for *vas* with *kubet*, and since then with every word of the mountains he could find: the name of the hour-star, kolo as Desta had said it. He had been through the whole of Lasta and come out empty.

Except one.

He looked over his shoulder at the old man asleep, and then down at his own left hand, and counted on its fingers.

The plank: Desta, at the rail, *I was never good at counting*, and the old man crouched behind the water cask a few paces off. One.

The ford: the old man's own mouth, at the back of Zoser's neck, in the black water. Two.

The hall: the Cardinal's question put to the old man's face, with the old man standing there to hear it. Three.

The loft: his own voice, his own whisper, in the dark at Naples, with nobody near him but the old man asleep on the nets, an arm's length away. Four.

Four fingers. Every one of them said in the old man's hearing, and the quietest of them at an arm's length while he slept.

So he turned his back on the sleeper, and leaned the other way, toward Angeska and the light, as far as he dared and not a finger farther; and he said, very low, the one word left that was his. His mother's word for him, the one she called him when he fell and would not cry, which had gone by him downstream under the friary window and which he found now had been in his mouth the whole time. *My lion.* He said it in her tongue, with her fall in it.

Angeska did not say it back.

She had said back every word he had ever paid her. She did not say this one. Her eyes went past him, not to his face, to the old man on the floor behind him: to his belly, where his arms lay crossed.

"Keep that one," she said.

He did not know why she had said it, and he did not ask. He sat back on his heels with the psalter shut on his knee, warm through, and let the warmth go through him and out at his fingers, and for a while it did not ebb.

When it did, he looked round.

The old man had moved in his sleep. He had turned over toward the middle of the cell, and his head lay on his arm on the stone a forearm from the small of Zoser's back. His breath went long and rough and even, and stirred the edge of Zoser's shamma.

Zoser's stomach dropped through him to the floor; and he sat very still, and decided that he would not let it have the hour.

⁂

In the morning the old man was laughing.

It was the camel. Angeska had told it a third time, and changed the verse again, and he lay against his wall with his face to the slit and the Easter bells coming down through it, every bell in Rome, and laughed until he coughed and had to hold his belly, and went on laughing through that.

Cencio came past the grille. They heard him first, the long thick sniff. He stopped to look in at the noise, his lantern swinging, and grinned, and the thumb he hooked over the bar was dyed red to the knuckle; the Romans had been dyeing their Easter eggs. He said something to them in the Roman speech, kindly enough, and when nobody answered he said it again for the foreigners with his hands: the red thumb pointed at the old man; the fingers went up, wriggling, a fire climbing; then he held up both hands spread, and folded the fingers down one after another until eight were gone, and opened his fist.

*The priest burns when the octave is out.*

He sniffed, and nodded to them, and went on down the passage.

The old man had stopped laughing.

Zoser looked at Angeska. She sat in her corner with her hands folded beside the cold stump on the ledge, and she had not looked up, and nothing in her had started. She had known for four days.
