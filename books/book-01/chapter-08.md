# CHAPTER 8

## GIOVANNI

*San Francesco a Ripa, Trastevere — mid-Lent, 1326*

The bread came down from Monte Giordano in the dark, on four mules, under sacking wet with the night, and when the baskets reached the Forum the first grey light was on the columns and the crowd had been waiting since the bell.

Giovanni Orsini stood on the step of Santi Cosma e Damiano with his gloves in his belt and his hands bare to the wrist. The cold had its teeth in his knuckles already. Below him the old Forum lay under frost: broken columns, long grass white and stiff between the paving stones, and a dozen cows that some butcher of the Monti grazed there because no one had told him not to. Smoke came across the frost from the lime-kiln by the Arch of Severus. Somebody was burning marble again. He could smell it, the dry chalk-and-iron smell of a column going into the fire to come out as mortar for a pigsty, and he had stopped sending men to forbid it, because the men who burned it had to eat.

“The basket, Eminence.”

His chamberlain held it up. Giovanni turned the sacking back, and the smell came up out of it, and he watched it reach the crowd: faces lifted at the back, where they could not yet see the step. Barley and spelt and a little rye, baked black at the crust, still warm at the heart from the ovens behind his family’s towers, each round loaf stamped before the oven with the Orsini rose so that no baker’s boy could carry it off and sell it in the Campo. He took the first loaf in both hands. It was heavy. He had fought the stewards on the weight in December.

“Mabilia,” he said.

She was first, because she was always first; she slept, he suspected, under the portico of the church itself. A small woman, all knuckles and shawl, with a goitre swelling at her throat and a smell of old smoke and urine and wet wool that he no longer noticed except in the first breath. She took the loaf against her chest and kissed the rose on it, and then his hand, and he let her.

“God keep you, Eminence. The saints keep you.”

“Pray for my brothers,” he said, which he said to all of them, and she said she would, which they all said, and some of them did.

Then the next. Cola the ferryman’s widow, with two children whose names he made himself remember, Vanna and the small one, Pietruccio. A mason from the Ripa who had once cut capitals for the Aracoeli and now fed the lime-kiln, and whose hands were white to the elbow with the dust of the thing he was destroying. Old men, too many of them. Girls of twelve who held their loaves up under their chins and ate them there on the steps, fast, before anyone larger could take them. He knew the girls would be back at the doors of the Trastevere stews by Easter, and he gave them bread.

He had done this every Lent since he was a deacon: every station church of the forty days, at dawn, from the Orsini rents, with his own hands. A cousin of his at Avignon had told him, two winters since, that it was a waste of a Cardinal of the Holy Roman Church to stand in the frost passing out loaves like a baker’s wife. Giovanni had told him that the Holy Roman Church had once been a thing that happened in Rome.

Behind him the doors of the saints’ church stood open: two leaves of bronze, green and black, older than any pope, that the heathen had hung on the round hall of some god of theirs and the Church had kept, because a door is a door. Through them he could see the apse: Christ on a road of red and blue clouds, and the two brothers from Syria being led up to Him by the apostles with their crowns of martyrdom held out in covered hands. Cosmas and Damian. Physicians. They had healed for nothing and taken no silver for it, the old Lives said, and had been beheaded for their trouble. Rome was full of such men, in pictures.

“How many?” he asked.

“A hundred and forty loaves, Eminence. Two hundred came.”

“Last year?”

The chamberlain looked at the frost. “Fewer came last year.”

Giovanni nodded and gave another loaf. The winter took its share, and the summer fever, and the young went south to Naples or north to anyone who would have them, and every year the Forum held more cows and fewer Romans. He had been a boy in the year of the Jubilee, when Boniface proclaimed the great pardon and the whole of Christendom came walking to Rome to be forgiven. He remembered the bridge to Saint Peter’s with a fence of timbers run down its middle, so that the crowds could go over on one side and come back on the other without trampling one another into the river. The pilgrims had thrown coins on the altar of Saint Paul so thick that two clerks stood there day and night with rakes. Twenty-six years ago. In the porticoes of Saint Peter’s, this winter, there was grass. He had walked under them on Ash Wednesday and stepped on it.

The Pope was at Avignon. The Pope had been at Avignon or on his way to it for twenty years, and the Lateran’s roof was still black from the fire, and the Colonna were in their towers on the Quirinal waiting, and in the north the German whom the Pope had cursed was gathering his lances and his Roman friends and his talk of an emperor crowned on the Capitol. Rome was a widow. She sat in the ashes in her old silks and sold her own columns for lime, and the Holy Father sent her letters.

*For I was hungry, and ye gave me no meat.*

It came into his head with the smell of the bread, as it did every Lent at about the hundredth loaf, and he let it come. He had seen the picture of it over the doors of half the churches he had served in: the King on His throne, the sheep on His right, the goats on His left, and under the goats the mouth of the pit with its teeth, and the fire. He believed in that fire as he believed in the marble under his feet. A man who had read law at Bologna knew that every judgement had a record, and that the record was read out at the end, item by item, and he meant his own to have a hundred and forty loaves in it for every morning of the forty days, from the year he was ordained until the year he died.

He gave the last loaf to a boy who had come too late and was crying about it with his mouth shut. Then he drew his gloves on, and under the cuff of his right sleeve the letter from Avignon moved against his wrist.

He did not need to take it out. He had read it so often that the words came up through the cloth.

*Hold them as guests.*

⁂

He rode back by the island, on the white mule, with four of his men walking in front in the silver and red. The river ran high and brown and stank of the tanneries and of whatever the butchers had thrown into it overnight. On the Trastevere bank the smell changed to wet hides and pitch and the yeast of the Lenten bakeries, and bells answered one another across the water, Santa Maria, San Crisogono, Santa Cecilia, all the bells of a city that had no pope to ring them for.

“They are in the hall?” he asked.

“Since prime, Eminence,” said the captain of the guard at his stirrup. “As you ordered.”

“Standing?”

“Standing.”

“Have they asked for anything?”

“The old one asked for something. The woman said he was praying.”

Two hours, then, on their feet, on the marble, with nothing. And for thirteen days his men had been speaking a word at the door of every inn in Trastevere and the Borgo and the Ripa where they tried to lay down coin, and the shutters had come down. He had not given the order cheerfully. He had given it.

Humbert had asked. Humbert of Toulouse had the Pope’s left ear, which was the one the old man listened with, and a crusade chest that every Italian cardinal at Avignon laughed at in public and wrote to in private, and he had written to Giovanni, a Roman, in his own hand, with no clerk. In ten years as a cardinal nobody had done that. Humbert wanted these Ethiopians, or wanted what they carried, and he did not write letters about embassies. Whatever it was, it was holy enough that a Frenchman who believed in nothing but the price of ships had sealed it with his own ring.

And if it was that holy, Giovanni wanted it more.

Not for a chest. Not for Acre. For Rome. Let the King of the Ethiopians have sent the Holy Father something so great, so old, so far beyond the bones and the shrouds and the milk of the Virgin in every abbey from here to Cologne, that it could not be carried to the Rhône and set on a side-altar in a palace built on a rock among Gascons. Let it be such that the Vicar of Christ had to come to it. To the Lateran. To the doors of Saint Peter, where the grass was. Then let them see whether a pope could stay away.

He needed Humbert to come near the Pope, and he needed to be ahead of Humbert to keep what he found there. That was the knot. He had been turning it for thirteen days.

They passed under the friary arch. San Francesco a Ripa had been the poor man’s own house in Rome; there was a cell upstairs where the saint had slept with his head on a stone, and the friars kept the stone. They had been very quiet, since the Pope had decreed that Christ and His apostles had owned their own purse, and they took the rent of a cardinal’s lodging now with no complaint at all.

Giovanni dismounted and gave the reins to a boy, and went in to them.

⁂

The hall had been a refectory before he took it. It still had the friars’ long table pushed against one wall under a whitewashed Last Supper, and a pulpit for the reader set in the wall, and a floor of good grey marble that someone, a hundred years ago, had carried across the river from some ruin and laid in great slabs with joints so close a knife would not go between them. Giovanni loved that floor. He had had his chair set at the east end on a step, and on either side of it candles on iron stands, beeswax, eight of them, because he would not have tallow in the same room as his breath. Two guards in the livery stood at the door. A third stood by the window, where the morning light came in grey through horn panes and lay on the cage of his linnet.

The bird sang as he came in. It always did.

The strangers stood in the middle of the floor, where they had been told to stand. Then, as he came up the room, he saw that they had not. The woman and the boy were where they had been put. The old man had moved. He stood back by the window, out of the lit half of the room, almost against the cage, a long way from the candle-stands.

Giovanni took his chair. His notary was at the little desk below the step on the left, already cutting a quill.

Ser Matteo of Mazara was sixty or so, the colour of a dry fig, with a reed pen stuck behind his left ear and a goose quill behind his right so that the two pens stood out from his head on either side, reed and feather. He had learned his Arabic in Palermo from an old man whose master’s master had kept the registers of the diwan under the last of the Swabian emperors, when a Saracen clerk and a Latin one still sat at the same table and copied each other’s accounts. He used the reed for Arabic and the quill for Latin and had never in Giovanni’s hearing confused them. He had told Giovanni once, in the whole of a winter, one thing about his youth: that in the old diwan a clerk was paid by the line and hanged by the word.

“Ser Matteo.”

“Eminence.” The notary did not look up from his quill.

Giovanni looked at the strangers.

He had been told they were black. They were not black, or not as the word was meant; they were the brown of wet earth, of old walnut, of the Madonna in Santa Maria Maggiore whom Saint Luke had painted from life. The old man was very thin and very tall, a priest by his staff and his hand-cross, wrapped in a filthy cotton cloth that had been white once, with a brown cloak of Naples wool over it. His cheeks had fallen in. He held himself, in that cloak, as though it were a cope of gold and the floor a cathedral’s. The boy was perhaps twenty, slight, with ink on his fingers and a leather satchel on his shoulder and a little scrip hanging at his right thigh, and an intelligent, frightened face. The woman wore a dark shawl over her hair. She was thirty perhaps, and she stood with her hands folded at her waist and her eyes lowered, exactly as a woman should stand in the presence of a prince of the Church.

They smelled of three weeks of Rome without a bath-house, of wet wool and old sweat and the river, and faintly of some resinous incense he did not know, coming off the priest’s cloth as the hall warmed. They were thin. Not as thin, he thought, as thirteen days of shut doors ought to have made them. Someone had been feeding them. He filed it.

“You are welcome to Rome,” he said in Latin. “In the name of the Holy Father, whose house this is in his absence.”

The woman lifted her eyes and turned her head a little toward the priest, and spoke.

To him it was not a language. It was sound, quick and full of stops at the back of the throat, with long vowels laid between the stops, and Giovanni, who had heard Greek and Hebrew and the German of the Bavarian’s envoys, had never heard anything like it. When she had done, the old man answered in the same tongue, but louder, and with a cadence that rose and hammered, a pulpit cadence: Giovanni could hear the shape of a sermon even without a word of the sense. He raised his staff. He named the Pope; that much came through, *Papa*, and something like *Roma*, and then a title he repeated three times, rolling it in his mouth.

The woman listened to the end. Then, in Latin a canon of the Lateran would not have been ashamed of:

“My husband, Tamrat son of Habta Maryam, priest of Lalibela, thanks Your Eminence for your welcome. He brings the greeting of the King of Kings of Ethiopia, Amda Seyon, to the Holy Father, and asks when he may lay the King’s letters before him.”

“The Holy Father is in Avignon,” Giovanni said. “As you were told at the gate, I think.”

She rendered it. The old man drew himself up and spoke again, at length.

“He asks whether, in that case, Your Eminence speaks for the Holy Father.”

“In Rome,” said Giovanni, “I am what Rome has.”

He watched her carry it across. It was shorter in her tongue than in his. He would watch that, he decided. He leaned back in his chair. The linnet sang by the window behind the old man’s shoulder, a long liquid run and two notes, and the priest did not turn his head.

⁂

He kept them there three hours.

He did not decide to. He would have said, if asked, that the matter was long and that a cardinal does not ask leave of an embassy to sit while they stand. But the friary bell rang sext and the shadow of the window-bars moved across the marble from the old man’s feet to the cage-stand, and nobody brought them water, because he had not told anybody to; and once, near sext, his chamberlain came up the side of the room with a cup for him and he drank it, slowly, in their sight, plain water with a little vinegar against the Lenten phlegm, and set the cup down on the arm of his chair where they could see it.

The old priest saw it; he peered at the woman’s face when she was close, but he saw the cup at once across the length of the hall. He began to speak. His voice had been loud before; now it climbed, and the stops in it came harder, and he pointed with his staff at the cup and at the guards and at the whitewashed Christ breaking bread on the wall, and the woman stood with her hands folded and let the storm go past her and over her. When it had gone she turned to Giovanni.

“My husband says that he is grateful for Your Eminence’s hospitality, and that in his country also the fast of the forty days is kept with great strictness, and that he does not ask for bread.”

Giovanni smiled. He knew a petitioner’s complaint when he heard one. He had sat as an auditor of causes in the Curia before the hat; he had heard the grievance of every widow and abbot and disinherited nephew in Italy, and the music of a man saying *I have been wronged* is the same in all of them. The old man had been saying it with his whole body.

He looked past her, at the priest.

“I feed Rome, Father,” he said. “You are not Rome.”

She did not hesitate. She turned and spoke, and it was brief, and her voice stayed low and level; and the old priest listened and inclined his head courteously. A man who has been told to his face that he is not Rome does not incline his head. Giovanni watched the old man’s face settle, and then watched the woman’s, which did not settle, because it had never moved.

So. She was good. He had suspected it at the first exchange, from the length of things. Now he had seen it.

He let them stand.

They talked of the faith, because he wanted to see the shape of the old man’s mind before he asked him for anything. They talked of the Ethiopian priests, who married. That came from the priest himself, with pride, through her: in his country a priest took a wife before his ordination, as Peter the apostle had had a wife, for the Gospel said that Peter had a wife’s mother and the Lord had healed her of a fever. Giovanni knew the verse. He also knew the priests of Trastevere, half of whom kept a woman behind the sacristy whose children the parish called nephews. He had no illusions about the Latin clergy, and few about anything else.

He turned in his chair, deliberately, from the woman to the old man, and looked the priest in the face, and asked him directly, in Latin, as one would put a question to a witness over the head of his advocate:

“Is she your wife, Father, or your sin?”

The old man did not understand a word. He looked to the woman.

And for the space of a breath before she turned to him she did not move at all. Giovanni had been watching her eyes for two hours and he saw them now. They did not go to the old man. They went down, to the marble, as a clerk’s go to a sum he has to carry, and came up again; and in that breath he saw her take the question apart and choose which piece to hand across. Then she spoke to the priest, briefly. Her tone was mild. The priest answered at once and with satisfaction, a short answer, nodding, and put his hand on his chest. Giovanni caught the sound of a word he had heard her use of herself at the start.

“My husband says,” she said, “that I am his wife, and that God has blessed it.”

Giovanni noted the gap, and noted the old man’s thumb, which had gone, while she spoke to him, to the hollow of his other hand and was rubbing there, slowly, round and round, as if to wipe something off the palm.

“Has He,” Giovanni said.

He did not press it. A good advocate does not press a witness on the first day. He let the record lie open and went on.

They talked of Enoch. That too came from the priest, not from him. Giovanni had asked what books the Ethiopians read at their Mass, meaning nothing by it, and the old man had answered with a list as long as the hall, and somewhere in the middle of it, in the stream of stops and long vowels, Giovanni heard a name he knew: *Henok*. The priest said it with love. He said it again, louder, and raised his hand-cross, and Giovanni understood without any help that the book of Enoch was read in the churches of the Ethiopians as Scripture.

“The apostle Jude cites it,” the woman said for him. “My husband says that you read Jude in Rome, and so you have read Enoch in Rome, whether you know it or no.”

“Saint Augustine,” said Giovanni, “says that the writings under Enoch’s name were left out of the canon because of their great age. Being so old, nobody could be sure they were his. The Church keeps a book because she has decided to keep it, lady. Not because it is old.”

She rendered it. Her rendering was longer than his saying. He wondered what she had put in.

The old man laughed. It was not a pleasant laugh, and it was not a madman’s. It was the laugh of a scholar at a provincial, and Giovanni felt it go into him and stick, as it had been meant to. The priest spoke at length, counting on his fingers, and the woman counted with him under her breath.

“My husband asks how many books are in the Bible of Rome. In the Bible of the Ethiopians there are eighty-one.”

“Eighty-one.” Giovanni turned his head. “Ser Matteo. How many in ours?”

The notary did not look up from his desk. “Seventy-some, Eminence.”

“Some?”

“It depends whom one asks.” The quill moved. “And whether one counts Baruch with Jeremiah.”

“Tell your husband,” he said to the woman, “that Rome does not need to count her books. She keeps them.”

She told him. The old man spread his hands and spoke to the ceiling, and the hall rang with it, and then he was thundering, there was no other word, in a different tongue now, slower, chanted, the vowels held long, the priests’ Latin, Giovanni guessed, the tongue they pray in: and he was pointing at the Last Supper on the wall and then at himself and then down at the grey slabs carried over the river from some heathen ruin, and Giovanni did not need to know a word of it to know what it meant. *We are older than you. Our church was old when yours was a thief’s camp among these stones.*

Giovanni smiled at him through all of it. It cost him nothing. Behind the old man’s shoulder the linnet had stopped singing. Giovanni did not mark when.

When it was done, the woman said, “My husband asks pardon for his warmth. He is fasting.”

“So am I,” said Giovanni. “Now. You have brought a letter.”

⁂

The old man would not give it up.

That much needed no rendering either. The case came out of the boy’s satchel, a case of tooled leather stained along one side with something black, tar perhaps; and the priest took it from the boy and held it against his chest and spoke, and the woman said that the letter was to the Holy Father and to no other hand.

“Then let no other hand touch it,” said Giovanni. “Read it to me. In your own tongue first, and then in Latin, for the Holy Father’s record.” He let a moment pass. “Is there any copy in another tongue?”

There was. The priest did not want to say so; the boy did, without meaning to, because his eyes went to the case. The woman said that the King’s chancery had made a copy in Arabic for the road through the Sultan’s country, in case the envoys were stopped by the Sultan’s officers, and that it was of no account, being for the Saracens.

“Then it will do no harm in the archive of the Holy Father,” said Giovanni. “Ser Matteo will take charge of it.”

The notary got up, which he rarely did, and went down the hall, and held out a dry brown hand. The priest looked at him, and at the guards, and at Giovanni, and slowly drew out of the case a folded sheet of thick yellowish paper, written over in the curling Saracen hand, and put it into the hand and drew his own back quickly. Matteo carried it back to his desk and sat, and took the reed from behind his left ear.

The rest was a scroll. The boy unrolled it. He held it up, open, at the height of the woman’s eyes, with a hand at each end, and his hands were perfectly steady; and she read from it. First in that chanted tongue, the old priest’s lips moving with hers, the old man’s eyes half shut; a long time, and the hall very quiet, and the candles burning straight and tall in the still air under the vault. Then she began again at the top, and gave it in Latin.

It was a beautiful letter. The King of Kings saluted the successor of Peter in the name of the Trinity, and of Mary, and of the saints of his country, whose names she gave one by one in a long grave rope. He greeted the Christians of Egypt. He spoke of the waters of his mountains, and of craftsmen, and of pilgrims, and at the last of his envoys and the Pope. Her Latin had the long rise and fall of a papal chancery letter, and Giovanni found that he was listening to it with pleasure, and he made himself stop.

Below the step, the reed moved. Ser Matteo had the yellow sheet flat under his left palm and was reading it with his lips pressed shut, and writing, and stopping, and writing; and once, when the woman reached the waters of the mountains, he stopped altogether and looked up at her for the length of a breath, and then down, and wrote quickly.

The boy saw that. His eyes left the scroll and went to the notary’s pen and then to the woman, and stayed on her. It was not the look of a servant afraid of his mistress. It was the look he had seen on the faces of families in the Curia’s courts when sentence was being read, and they waited to hear whose name came next.

She finished. She bowed her head over the folded hands. The boy rolled the scroll.

Matteo sanded his sheet, climbed the step and put the sheet into Giovanni’s hand without a word. He smelled of gall-ink and of the fennel seed he chewed against his teeth. Then he went back down to his desk and put the reed behind his ear again and sat with his hands folded, exactly as the woman was standing.

Giovanni read.

He read it twice. The second time he read only the right-hand column, Matteo’s small Latin, the five notes one under another.

*A threat made into a hymn.*

He looked down the hall at her. She stood exactly as she had stood for three hours. The shawl had slipped a finger’s breadth from her hair, and she had not put it back.

So that was what she was. Not a wife gilding a rough old man’s words out of love or fear. A woman who took a king’s letter that threatened the Sultan with the Nile and bargained in hostages, and turned it, sentence by sentence, aloud, without a pause, into a psalm. That was not fear. Fear blurs; it drops a clause. This had been made. He had sat in the Curia’s courts and watched forgers, and he knew the difference between a man who smudges a figure and a man who cuts a new seal.

And then the last row.

He read it a third time. A woman who softened everything had hardened one thing. The King asked, if God willed, for friendship and prayers, a courtesy from the end of the world; and she had made him *require*, and require the Pope himself, and no other. Why would a woman whose whole art was to make her master smaller make him larger at that one place? He turned it over and could not make it lie flat. He looked at Matteo, and the notary looked back at him with no expression at all, and lifted one shoulder an inch.

*Why?*

Giovanni folded the sheet in three and put it into his right sleeve, against the letter from Avignon, and felt the two papers lie together there against his wrist.

“Is that the whole of the letter, lady?” he said.

“It is the whole, Eminence.”

“Word for word.”

“As near as Latin will carry it.”

“Ser Matteo,” said Giovanni, without looking away from her. “Write that the woman affirms her rendering to be the whole of the letter, word for word, as near as Latin will carry it.”

The quill scratched. The woman did not look at it. The boy did.

⁂

He did not expose her. A good advocate lets the witness stand on her own words a while and grow used to their weight. And he wanted the why before he wanted anything else. Until he had it, she was worth more to him standing in this hall telling lies than burning in the Campo telling none.

And Humbert had said, *as guests*.

“You have come a long way,” he said, “and Rome is not kind to strangers in Lent. The Holy Father’s house will lodge you. There are rooms in this friary for pilgrims. You will be our guests.”

She rendered it, and the old priest bowed, deeply, with relief that showed even under his pride; and the boy did not bow, but looked at the guards.

“My husband thanks Your Eminence,” the woman said, “for the roof.” She did not lower her eyes this time. “And asks, of Your Eminence’s charity, whether there will be bread.”

It was her own question. He knew that by her face; she had not turned to the old man before she asked it. It was the first thing she had said in that hall that was entirely hers.

“It is Lent, lady.”

“Even in Lent, Eminence, the Church gives bread to the poor. Your guards spoke of it at the door this morning: a hundred and forty loaves, at the saints’ church in the Forum.” She paused, and then said, quite gently, “Is it just to keep the King’s envoys without?”

Giovanni looked at her a long moment. The candles stood straight and tall on their stands on either side of him, and the hall had grown cold again with the sun gone off the window, and smelled of cold wax and the strangers’ wet wool.

“Justice,” he said, “is whatever the Church decrees. And the Church has decreed forty days.” He turned to the captain. “Water. They may have water. The rooms on the north side, by the dormitory stair. A man on the door, day and night. They are not prisoners. If the friars ask, they are not prisoners.”

“Eminence.”

The guards came down the hall. The old priest went first with his staff, slowly, through the lit half of the room, and as he passed between the candle-stands nearest the door he drew his cloak close around him and went by them at the full stretch of the passage, with his shoulder almost to the wall. The woman followed. The boy went last. At the door he looked back, not at Giovanni, but up the hall to the step where the notary sat, and then at the woman’s back, and the fear in his face was not for himself.

The door closed behind them. The candle-flames shrank in the wind of it, sank to beads at the wick, smoked, and climbed again straight.

⁂

The linnet was dead.

He saw it when he rose to go: a small brown heap on the floor of the cage, its feet drawn up into its breast, lying hard against the bars, in a smell of seed-husk and droppings, on the side nearest the place, by the window, where the old priest had stood for three hours. Its eye was open. The seed in its dish had not been touched since morning, and the water was clear. His chamberlain, sent for, turned the cage in his hands and said that it was the cold, Eminence, the Lenten cold, the window-horn let in the cold and he had said so at Candlemas; small birds died of it every year. Giovanni said that no doubt he was right. Then he told him to take the cage away from that window and hang it in the other corner of the hall, by the step, near the candles, and the chamberlain did, and stood waiting to be told why, and Giovanni did not tell him, because he did not know.

⁂

His chamber was above the cloister, on the warm side. The brazier had been lit an hour before and the room smelled of charcoal and of the dry sweet olibanum he burned against the damp. He sat down at the table without taking off his cappa and drew the two papers out of his sleeve and laid them side by side.

Humbert’s letter: six lines, the hand small and hurried, the key-in-the-ring pressed into its wax. He did not unfold it. He knew what it said. *Hold them as guests.* He had held them as guests.

Matteo’s sheet: five rows.

He sat with both before him a long while. Through the floor he could hear the friars below at their one Lenten meal in his hall, and the reader’s voice going on from the pulpit in the wall. Bread, beans, water. The smell of the beans came up the stair, and of the bread, the friars’ own coarse loaves, with no rose on them.

He thought of the old priest’s face settling, at *You are not Rome*, into courtesy. He thought of the boy looking back, and of Mabilia under the portico with her loaf, and of the goats over the church doors, and the mouth with the teeth. *I was a stranger, and ye took me not in.* That was in it too. He had always passed over that verse at the hundredth loaf. It is a lawyer’s habit to read the clause that applies.

They were schismatics. It was Lent. They had been given a roof.

He laid his palm flat on Matteo’s sheet, on the last row, and left it there.

The chamberlain knocked and came in with the lamp, though it was not yet dark, and set it down, and then took from his own sleeve a small square of paper folded twice and laid that on the table too.

“From the north rooms, Eminence. The lay brother who took up the water brought it down. The woman gave it him.” He hesitated. “He says she had it ready. Folded, when he came in.”

Giovanni looked at it without touching it. It was cheap paper, the kind the Ripa grocers wrap pepper in, and it had no seal, only the two folds. On the outside, in a hand finer than his chaplain’s, someone had written his name and title in full and correctly.

He opened it.

> TO THE LORD GIOVANNI, CARDINAL DEACON OF THE HOLY ROMAN CHURCH, INTO HIS OWN HAND
>
> Eminence. Today you heard the King’s letter twice: once in the Arabic, by your notary’s reed, and once from me. Hear it a third time, tonight, alone, without your Sicilian, and I will tell you which of the two was lying, and why.

He read it again. The Latin was better than his own.

He did not send for Matteo.
