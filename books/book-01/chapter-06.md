# CHAPTER 6

## CARDINAL HUMBERT

*Avignon — Candlemas, February 1326*

The Pope held out the candle, and at that moment Humbert’s heart missed a beat.

It missed the way a tired clerk misses a line: a gap where a figure should stand, and the next figure crowding in too soon to hide it. Humbert knelt through it. He kept his face as it was. He put out both his hands, palms up, as the ceremonial required, and the old man’s fingers laid the candle across them, a yard of white beeswax with the papal arms pressed into its foot in red, still soft from the brazier where the acolytes had warmed it for the blessing.

“A light to lighten the Gentiles,” said John XXII. His voice had the dry scratch of a quill.

“And the glory of thy people Israel,” said Humbert, and kissed the ring, and the hand under the ring, which smelled of ink and camphor and weighed nothing at all.

He rose. His knee cracked. He went back to his place with the candle upright before him, and did not touch his chest, though beneath his breastbone an ache had begun, dull and patient, with a promise of more.

Humbert stood fourth from the end on the Pope’s left, between a Gascon made cardinal for his uncle’s sake and an Italian made cardinal for his own. He had stood there six years. He knew the price of every place in that line as a factor knows the price of pepper in every port from Alexandria to Bruges, and he knew what it would cost to stand at the right hand itself, close enough to bend and speak into the old man’s ear without being seen to bend. It would cost a crusade. It would cost the city of Acre, taken back from the Sultan and laid in the Holy Father’s hands across two palms. And for that it would cost a sum no king in Christendom had yet been willing to pay.

The Ark of the Covenant, carried before the host, would make them pay.

It would make them weep and pay. Let the King of France see the Ark borne up the beach at Acre on the shoulders of Levites in white, and see whether he found no money then. Humbert had worked it through by night, figure by figure, in a ledger no clerk would ever copy. He wanted it before the old man died, because a new pope would have new favourites. And he wanted to live to see it, which was the part of the sum that troubled him now, with his heart stumbling on in his chest, unsure of its footing on every step since Advent.

The bells of the palace rang for the procession, and the cathedral answered from the rock above, and then all the bells of Avignon came in, more than he had ever managed to count, until the air of the hall shook with bronze. Smoke from four hundred candles hung in the gilded vault. The hall stank of hot wax and wet wool and the river damp that came up through every floor in this city from November to March.

Humbert looked at his flame. An old habit. It stood straight up on the wick, yellow, steady, and so did the flame of the Gascon beside him, and the Italian’s, and the Pope’s far down the hall. Four hundred candles, and not one of them leaned. He let out his breath and did not ask himself whether what he felt was relief.

After the procession the Chamberlain gave wine in the long room above the cloister, and the cardinals stood about with their blessed candles held behind them by their chaplains: every man attended by his own ghost.

It was the last Sunday before Lent. On Wednesday every head in this room would be crossed with ash, and for forty days there would be lampreys and salt eels, and every man would talk of his fasting as he now talked of his dinners. Today there were still sugared plums on the table, and a smell of roast capon from the kitchens below that made the younger chaplains swallow. Humbert took a cup and did not drink. His physician had forbidden wine.

“Our brother of Toulouse,” said Cardinal Saurel, “is still sailing for Acre.”

He said it to the room, not to Humbert, in the bright voice of a man who has been waiting since the hall to say it. Saurel was a Gascon of the Agenais, with a well-fed fox’s face and a fine pair of hands that he moved about while he talked, so that men watched his hands and not his eyes. Beside him stood Cardinal Malet, broad and soft, a Limousin, who laughed before a jest was finished in case it turned out to be one.

“In which ship?” said Malet. “The one built of tithes?”

“Our brother’s ship is built of the purest tithes,” said Saurel. “Six years of the tenth, from every benefice in France. A noble ship. Only, the King of France took the tenth and spent it on a war in Gascony, and the Holy Father took what was left and spent it on lances for his legate in Lombardy. So I am afraid our brother’s ship has no keel. And no planks. And no sail.” He spread his fine hands. “But a great deal of spirit.”

A few men laughed. Malet laughed longest. Humbert noted who laughed, and in what order, and who did not, and where each man looked as he laughed. There were men here whose rents he could have read off to them to the last denier, and their nephews’ concubines by name.

He smiled. He had a good smile. Women had told him so, before he took orders, and men since. It was his plumb line: it showed which way a wall would fall.

“My brother of Agen is right,” he said. “I have no ship.”

Saurel inclined his head, gracious in victory.

“But I am puzzled,” said Humbert, “that he should find the Holy Father’s passage to Acre a thing to laugh at, on the feast of the Presentation, in the Holy Father’s own house, with the Holy Father’s wine in his cup.” He said it gently, regretfully, and let his eyes rest a moment on the cup in Saurel’s fine hand. “I have been at the Camera this month. I have seen the bulls for the tenth. They speak of the passage as His Holiness’s dearest wish. I would not have thought there was anyone in this room who wished to be heard laughing at it. But I am a simple man, and Gascony has its own customs.”

The room went quiet in the curial way: still talking, but listening under its talk. The Chamberlain, at the far end, had turned his head.

Saurel’s hands had stopped moving. “I laugh at the King of France,” he said, “not at the Holy Father.”

“Then I have misheard you, and I beg your pardon,” said Humbert, “and I will tell the Chamberlain I misheard, if he asks.” He turned to Malet before Saurel could answer that. “And my brother of Limoges is quite right about the tithes. They are spent. That is why the passage cannot be paid for out of tithes. I agree with you both. We must find another way.”

“What other way?” said Malet, who could not help himself.

“God provides,” said Humbert, and drank, forgetting, from the wine he had meant not to drink.

It was sour and too hot and spiced with grains of paradise, and it went down into his chest and lit there, and his heart kicked against it, twice, a thing tied in a sack. Saurel was looking at him now with the fox’s eyes, and Humbert gave him the smile again, the whole of it, and saw it land.

“You look grey, Toulouse,” Saurel said quietly. “Lent comes early for some of us.”

“I have been fasting already,” said Humbert. “For Acre.”

He bowed to the Chamberlain on his way out, and to Saurel, a little lower than he needed to, which was an insult only Saurel would understand.

He went home through the palace gardens, because there were fewer stairs.

The peacocks were out on the frozen grass. They screamed at the bells and left their dung on every path, green and white and steaming faintly in the cold, so that a cardinal of the Holy Roman Church must walk to his supper with his skirts held up out of it. One cock stood across the path with its tail half raised, a hundred blue eyes trembling, and looked at him with its own small foolish eye. Humbert stepped round it. The cold off the Rhône came through his furs and lay on his skin, wet and close. The river was high and brown under the bridge, and it smelled of mud and of the tanneries downstream, and even here, behind the walls, it smelled of the sea it was going to.

The sea. He did not want to think of the sea.

He climbed the four steps to his door one at a time, and rested at the top.

His steward had the door open before he knocked. “There’s a man come from Naples, Eminence,” he said, low. “From the *Santa Maria*. He has a bundle for the Humbert. He would not give it to me.”

*The Humbert.* Not *His Eminence*, not *the Cardinal*. His steward said it as the men said it, a word for a chair rather than for the man in it, and Humbert felt, as he always felt at the article, a small warmth, low in the body. He had been given that name by an old man in a cold room in Toulouse, with a cup of water and a signet. There had been another name before it. He did not think it.

“Bring him to the study,” he said. “And a fire.”

⁂

The courier stood in the study with the bundle in his arms and snow melting on his shoulders, a lean man with a cropped head and the bruised look about the eyes of one who has ridden three weeks in winter and slept in barns. He smelled of horse and of the road. The bundle smelled of the sea.

That was the first thing. Humbert had taken the dipper from the ewer to fill his cup, and the smell reached him across the room before he had set it down: tar and salt and wet oilcloth, a ship’s smell, a quay’s smell. His hand stopped over the cup.

There was smoke in the smell. Black smoke, thick and greasy, with something sweet in it and something burning that was not wood. There was a noise in it too, men and women shouting all along a waterfront, a sea slapping the stones, and a hand on his collar lifting him off his feet, and his own legs kicking in the air.

He filled the cup. He set the dipper back in the ewer. He did it slowly and exactly, as he did everything.

The cup was pewter, old and soft, gone dark at the rim where mouths had been. In the bottom of it lay a nail. It was an old nail, square in the shank and black, a hand’s length long, its head beaten flat and lopsided by a smith dead longer than anyone could say, and the water lay over it so clear that the nail seemed to lie in nothing at all. Humbert drank. The water was cold from the ewer and tasted, as it always tasted, of iron: of blood, of a key in a child’s mouth. He felt it go down. His heart did not grow quieter. He had not truly expected it to.

The courier’s eyes had gone to the cup and stayed there. Humbert set it down on the table between them.

“Well, brother,” he said.

“From the master of the *Santa Maria*, for the Humbert.” The man knelt and laid the bundle at his feet. “He bade me say: four came aboard at Alexandria, as was written. One went into the sea off Sicily; the master says of his own will. Three came ashore at Naples. He gave them the road to Rome. They asked for the Pope.”

“For the Pope.” Humbert smiled. “At Rome.”

“Yes, Eminence.”

He did not ask which one. It would be written down. “Poor souls.” He found that he meant it. “Rest. Eat. Tell the kitchen you may have meat, while it is still a Sunday before Lent.”

When the man had gone he knelt on the floor by the bundle, which a cardinal does not do, and which his knees did not thank him for, and untied it himself.

It was long and heavy and wrapped in three folds of oilcloth stiff with salt. Inside the oilcloth was a sword.

It was not a Frankish sword. It was long and narrow and curved a little, a sickle’s curve, plain grey iron with no maker’s mark, the edge whetted into a shallow hollow near the hilt where a man had worked it with a stone night after night. The leather of the grip was dark, almost black, and shaped to one hand. Humbert laid his own on it, and it did not fit him; the hollows were in the wrong places. The scabbard was of hide with the hair still on it, worn bald along one side where it had ridden a man’s hip for twenty years. Under the tar it smelled of grease and another man’s sweat and the smoke of some dung fire Humbert had never sat by and never would.

A soldier’s sword. A poor soldier’s. The kind of man who sleeps with his hand on the hilt.

Under the sword lay a packet in waxed linen, thick, closed with a seal of plain iron-coloured wax and in it the key standing upright in its ring. The captain’s report. Humbert knew the shape of the captain’s reports. He put it on the table, beside the cup, and left the wax whole.

Under that lay another.

It was smaller. It had travelled inside the oilcloth wrapped in a fold of silk, the colour of saffron, as if someone had wanted it kept dry and warm the whole length of the sea. The wax was plain, red-brown, with no device pressed in it at all, only the whorls of a thumb. The superscription was in Latin, in a hand he did not know: a beautiful hand, upright and narrow, with long clean ascenders, the hand of a chancery that was not any chancery he had ever had dealings with. *To the lord Humbert, at the court of the lord Pope. From Alexandria.* And beneath that, alone, a single letter, set down by someone who expected to be known by it:

*A.*

He turned it over in his fingers. He ran his correspondents down in his head, column by column. Arnaut at Montpellier, who wrote in a scrawl. Aymar the Genoese, who could not spell. Neither of them in Alexandria. And at the foot of the column, where he had known all along the sum would come out: Avram ben Shelomo, the Jew of Alexandria, who sold cloves by the Sea Gate and packed his letters in his own goods. Avram, whom he had once paid six years to walk the markets of Cairo with a description of a woman. Avram wrote a merchant’s Latin. But a clove-dealer with news worth the sending could hire a chancery hand for a day.

The silk smelled of cloves. Under the cloves lay something thinner and sweeter and cheaper: the rose-water the dealers of Cairo sprinkle on the women they stand up for sale. Humbert smelled his mother’s market in it.

Thirty-five years. A man does not write after so long, in a hired hand, wrapped in saffron silk, to say that he has found nothing. After thirty-five years there was only one piece of news left in Cairo for a woman of sixty-three, and one kindness a clove-dealer could still do her son, which was to tell him where she was buried.

He broke the seal.

The wax cracked under his thumb with the small dry sound that every letter in this city made, a hundred times an hour, in a hundred rooms. And the door opened behind him, and his steward said, “Master Pèire, Eminence,” and Humbert slid the letter, still folded, into the sleeve of his rochet, against the inside of his wrist, and rose from his knees as if he had knelt there only to pray.

Master Pèire of Montpellier had cold hands and knew it, and warmed them at the fire before he touched anyone, which was the chief reason Humbert kept him. He was a small neat man in a doctor’s dark gown that smelled of the herbs he carried in its sleeves, rue and wormwood and something bitter, and of the urine flasks his boy carried behind him in a basket.

“Sit,” he said. “No. In the chair with the back. So.”

Humbert sat. Master Pèire took his wrist in two fingers and turned his head away, as if listening at a door. The letter lay against the other wrist. Humbert could feel its edge.

He counted with the physician. He could not help it. One, two, three, four, five, and a blank, a nothing, a held breath; and then six coming too fast, stumbling, as if it had been running to catch up. Seven, eight, nine. Ten. Eleven, and the blank again. Twelve.

“How often does it do that?” asked Master Pèire.

“Do what?”

The physician looked at him. Humbert laughed and gave it up. “Since Advent,” he said. “Every day. More on stairs. More when I lie on my left side. Today, at the candles, it stopped long enough for me to say an Ave in.”

“Did you say one?”

“I said a sum.”

Master Pèire did not smile. He pressed his thumb into the flesh above Humbert’s ankle, through the silk of the stocking, and took it away, and they both watched the dent stay where the thumb had been and fill slowly, from the edges in. He looked at Humbert’s lips and the beds of his nails, and held the flask his boy gave him up to the window, turning it, frowning, tilting it to the light and back again.

“You have drunk wine,” he said.

“A mouthful. I was provoked.”

“Then don’t be provoked.” He set down the flask. “The heart is a furnace, Eminence. Yours is old before its time. It draws badly. It fills with smoke. You must give it no more fuel than it can burn. No wine. No salt meats; God be thanked for Lent. No stairs. No disputing in consistory. Sleep with your head raised. And no journeys.”

“No journeys,” said Humbert.

“You have the look of a man planning a journey. I have seen it before. I do not like it.”

He laid the flask in its straw in the basket and settled the cloth over it before he looked round again.

“Wherever it is, send someone.”

“And if I do all this. How long?”

The physician was quiet for a while. The bells of the cathedral began to ring for Vespers. When they stopped he said, “I am not a prophet. If you are careful, years. If you are careless, months. If God is careless—” He lifted one shoulder. “A stair. A sneeze. A letter with bad news in it.”

When he had gone, Humbert sat on in the chair with the back, with his head raised as he had been told.

A letter with bad news in it. He could feel its edge against his wrist, and the crumbs of broken wax in his sleeve. He had broken the seal; he had not unfolded the sheet. Between those two things there was still a little room, and he wanted to stay in it. He paid Master Pèire for advice. He would take it.

His hand went where it always went, to the breast of his rochet, and under the silk to the cord.

There were two things on the cord. One was the signet, the iron key in its iron ring, cold and heavy and smooth with handling, the same key that stood in the captain’s wax on the table. The seal was older than he was, older than the old man who had worn it before him, older than anyone could tell him. He had been thirteen when he saw it for the first time, pressed in black wax on a letter in a cold room in Toulouse, and stood looking at it so long that the old man who owned it asked him why. *It is a key in a ring,* he had said. *Somebody is keeping it safe.* The old man had looked at him for a long time after that, and then sent for water.

The other was a key.

It was a house key, iron, as long as his finger, with a plain round bow and a bit cut in three steps. Its shank was worn bright where a woman’s thumb had turned it in a lock, for years, every night, and where his own thumb had rubbed it since, for thirty-five years, every night, until the iron there shone.

He could see her holding it. That was the trouble with the smell of the sea. He could see her on the quay at Acre in the smoke, with her hair coming down out of its veil and her hand closed white round the key of their house, as if the house were still there to be locked, as if the Saracens coming through the Accursed Tower would stop at a locked door. She was laughing. She had been laughing, because he was crying, and she wanted him to stop. A Venetian sailor had him by the collar and the belt, and his feet were in the air, and the boat was rising and falling against the stones so that the sailor had to wait for the sea to bring it up, and his mother put her free hand on his cheek in the moment before the sea came, and said his name, the old name, and said, *Go on. I’ll come after. Keep me a place.* And as the sailor swung him out over the water she pushed the key into his fist and closed his fingers on it with her own. *Keep that for me. You’ll let me in.*

Then he was in the boat. Then the boat was out on the water, and the quay was smoke, and the tower was fire, and the great noise behind them went on and on until the wind took it, and he was sitting in the bilge with forty strangers, holding the key in both hands because there was nothing else to hold.

She had not come after. She had not been ransomed. He had paid Genoese and Catalans and, last and longest, Avram ben Shelomo of Alexandria to look for her in the markets of Cairo and Damascus, and one had found a woman of the right age and the wrong face, and he had paid for her anyway, and set her free at Famagusta, and gone to his room and been sick. He had been nine. He was forty-four. She would be sixty-three, if she lived. Women lived to sixty-three in the Sultan’s houses. Women lived to eighty. He had read the lists.

He had kept her a place. The place was Acre. And his heart, which had carried him out of that city in a stranger’s hands, was stumbling now on the stairs of a borrowed house on the Rhône, as if it had at last run out of the strength to carry him back.

That was what frightened him. Not death; he had made his confession on the feast of Saint Thomas, and he had masses bought for his soul in four churches, and he did not doubt the mercy of God, who was a better accountant than any man in this city. What frightened him was dying in this chair with his head raised, in Avignon, warm, and her still standing on the quay with the key in her hand, waiting for him to come back for her, and the boat never coming.

He took his hand away from the cord. The letter had slipped a little in his sleeve. He pushed it back with two fingers, against the skin of his wrist, and left it there.

By the time the lamps were lit the sword was on the wall, and the captain’s report lay where he had put it, beside the cup, its wax whole.

He hung the sword himself. He would not let the steward do it. He stood on the stool, which was a stair of a kind, and drove the iron pins into the plaster above the press where he kept the order’s ledgers, between the window and the crucifix, and took a long time over it. He laid the sword across the pins, still in its hairy scabbard, and turned it until the dark grip lay toward the door, the first thing a man would see. Then he stepped down and looked at it: a word in a tongue nobody in Avignon could read.

He did not know the soldier’s name. He would not need it. But he was a man who kept his accounts, and he set it down in the ledger of his mind against the credit side: one brave man, of the Prester’s country, gone into the sea off Sicily, of his own will, for a reason. When Acre was taken there would be a mass for the dead of the passage. The soldier could have a candle at it. That much Humbert could promise him.

On the other side of the window, where the lamplight caught it, hung the mirror.

It was a round of polished silver no wider than two hands, set in gilt, the gift of a bishop who had wanted something; the finest thing in the room. He went and stood before it. He always did, at this hour. He told himself it was to look for the grey Saurel had seen at his lips. He did not look long at his lips.

He looked at his eyes. They were pale. They had always been pale, a winter colour, his mother’s. He smiled at the silver, and watched the smile go up into his cheeks and round his mouth and stop, as it always stopped, just under the eyes, as water stops under a door. He tried it again, warmer. It stopped in the same place. He rubbed almond oil into his hands, finger by finger, and watched them in the silver, soft and white, the hands of a man who had never carried anything heavier than a seal; and he looked at the man in the silver a while longer than he needed to, waiting for that man to want something different. Then he went to the table, sat down away from the report, and drew a clean sheet toward him instead.

He wrote the letter to Rome himself, in his own hand, so that no clerk would copy it. He cut a new pen for it, though the old one would have served.

> LETTER OF HUMBERT, CARDINAL OF THE HOLY ROMAN CHURCH, TO CARDINAL GIOVANNI ORSINI, AT ROME. AVIGNON, THE PURIFICATION OF THE VIRGIN, 1326
>
> To our most dear brother in Christ, greeting.
>
> An embassy of Ethiopians approaches, by the road from Naples, asking for the Pope. Hold them as guests. Feed them nothing.
>
> You will be thanked for it here. I shall see to that.
>
> Written in haste, on the feast of candles.

He sealed it with the key in the ring, and the wax smelled of hot honey, and he held the signet in it a long moment while it set, as if holding a door shut against a wind.

*Feed them nothing.* Orsini would think it cruelty, and do it gladly. Humbert did not think it cruelty. A hungry man is a man who notices bread, and notices the hand that gives it, and remembers. When the Ethiopians came to Avignon at last, as everyone did, they would come thin, and the first warm room and the first white loaf would be his, and they would tell him whatever he asked. A kindness, deferred. He had learned from the ransom brokers of Cairo, who kept a captive’s name back a season to raise her price, what a kindness deferred was worth.

He reached for the captain’s report.

The letter in his sleeve pressed against his wrist as he reached. He felt its edge, and the broken wax, and the cloves and the cheap rose-water came up to him faintly with the warmth of his own skin. *A.* Avram would write to the lord Humbert, not to the Cardinal; Avram had known him before the hat. Bad news kept. It would be as true in an hour as it was now, and for that hour she would still be sixty-three, and somewhere.

His hand had stopped on the captain’s wax. For thirty years every report of the order had come to him under this seal, and every one had said at its end, in one form or another, *the flame did not lean*. He had learned not to open hope quickly. At nineteen, in Toulouse, he had kept the Venetian brokers’ letters on his table for a week together, unopened, turning them over at supper; because as long as a letter is sealed, she is still on the quay.

He drank from the pewter cup, and the nail lay at the bottom, black under the clear water, and the iron taste went down into him. Then he broke the captain’s seal, the key in its ring, and spread the sheet on the table under the lamp, and his heart missed once, and caught up, and he read.
