# CHAPTER 10

## ZOSER

*Trastevere, guests under guard — Laetare week, 1326*

The lay brother brought supper up the dormitory stair at the ninth hour, and Zoser had the pot out of his hands before the guard had finished drawing the bar.

The pot was black on its belly from the fire and hot enough to bite through the cloth into his palms. He set it square in the middle of the board, under the lamp, where all three of them could reach. There was one lamp in the two rooms: at supper it stood on the board on its trestles by the narrow door, and at night it went back into its niche in the doorpost. Chickpeas, stewed soft in oil, with rosemary; the room filled with the green, resinous smell of a hillside. Behind him the brother laid out a round loaf with fennel seed pressed into its crust, a string of dried figs threaded on a grass stalk, and a jug with a cloth tied over its mouth. Then he bowed to Angeska, only to Angeska, and went out, and the bar came down.

Zoser untied the cloth. What was in the jug was white.

“Milk,” he said, and heard how young it sounded.

“Almonds,” said Angeska. She was breaking the loaf with her thumbs, and the steam came up out of it. “The Latins may not touch milk in their fast, so they grind almonds and steep them and wring them through linen until they give something that will pass for it. Our fast forbids milk too.” She set half the loaf by his hand. “Nothing in it forbids an almond.”

He poured a little into the clay cup and drank it with his eyes shut.

It was thin and cool and tasted faintly of the cloth it had been wrung through, and under the cloth it was sweet: a sweetness that came late, at the back of the tongue. The last sweet thing he had tasted was a lick of honey from the comb the abbot’s steward broke for the boys the night before the embassy rode out of Lalibela; he had not known it was the last, or he would have eaten it more slowly. Behind his shut eyes the honey and the almonds lay side by side, and the months between them were only a road.

When he opened them Angeska was watching him. She looked away first, which she never did, and pushed the figs toward him.

He ate. The fennel cracked between his teeth, clean and faintly bitter. He scooped the chickpeas with a fold of crust, as at home. His stomach, a fist since Capua, let go a finger at a time. It hurt. He went on eating.

This was the third day of bread. It had begun on the morning she came up the stair at first light with four loaves held against her chest, crumbs in the creases of her hands, and her hair smelling of an incense no friar in this house could afford, dry and sweet. *Finding us bread*, she had said; and there had been oil by evening, and the purse at her girdle, flat since the bridge at Capua, was round again. Zoser looked at the purse. Then he took another fold of crust and did not finish the thought. He drank again instead.

Just beyond the narrow door, on the floor of the inner room where the lamp’s light gave out, the priest sat with his back to the wall and his bread on his knee, untouched.

“It is their rose Sunday,” Angeska said. All morning, through the wall, they had heard the friars at their Mass, a chant that sounded glad in spite of itself. “Half the fast is done, and they sing *Laetare* and rejoice for a day. And in Avignon the Pope blesses a rose made of gold, and gives it to some king.”

In Avignon, a golden rose. In Rome, figs. Zoser ate one more, slowly, for the friars.

Angeska tapped the side of the pot with her fingernail. It gave back a dull clay note.

“*Vas*,” she said.

“*Vas*.”

“A pot. A jar. A coffin. Anything made to hold what is put in it.” She tapped again. “A man too, in their sermons, when they want him humble.”

He took the psalter from the sill. The first leaf opened by itself now: the child’s አ in its corner, crooked and too large; under it *clavis*, *key*; under that *ostium*, *door*. He wrote *vas* below them, in the Latin letters and in his own, then, smaller, *pot*. The soot-ink went on brown, dried black, and smelled of mutton fat.

Key, door, pot. Every night since Benevento she had put out her hand, and tested him, and given; and he had taken, with his eyes down.

“And what do I give you?” he said.

Angeska raised her eyebrows.

“You give, and I take. Every night.” He heard the sting in it and could not get it out. “I am not a beggar at a gate. From tonight I pay. One of mine for one of yours.”

“You have nothing I want.”

“Then you will have it anyway.” Through the barred window came the smoke of the guards’ brazier in the court, charcoal and pine pitch. It had smelled wrong every evening since Naples. “*Kubet*.”

“*Kubet*,” Angeska said, as if tasting something left too long in the pot.

“The cakes of cow dung the women slap on the house wall to dry. And the fire they make, with a branch of juniper on top so the smoke smells sweet.” His hands had come up, shaping it. “Every house in Lasta lights it at the same hour. Coming up the valley at night you smell the village before you see it. That is *kubet*.”

“A word for dung.”

“A word for the fire.”

“A word for dung on fire.” She examined her nails. She did not reach for the psalter or say it back. “Very well. You have paid. I am rich.”

“In Rome,” said the priest from his corner, “they need a word for a pot.”

They both turned. He had not moved. His face was in the dark, but his beard had lifted at one side.

“A man of Lasta knows a pot when he sees one. He does not need a Latin to hold it up and tell him.” The beard lifted again. “But if the boy pays, I pay. The muleteers on the lake road taught me a word on pots, forty years ago, when I was too young to be shocked and too vain to say so.”

“Qess,” Zoser said.

“They say: *It is never the pot that complains of the ladle. It is the neighbours.*”

For a moment nothing happened. The heat came up Zoser’s neck into his ears. A priest. At table. In Lent.

Then Angeska laughed.

She laughed aloud, her head back against the wall and her hand flat on the board, a sound so unlike any he had heard from her that he did not know it at first for hers. She put the back of her wrist to her mouth and it went on through that. He had seen her smile at tollmen and corporals, and learned what those smiles bought. This bought nothing. She laughed until her eyes ran, and wiped them with her knuckle, and looked through the narrow door at her husband, and laughed again.

In the dark beyond the door the old man said nothing. But he tore a piece from the bread on his knee, and ate it.

Zoser sat between them with the psalter open on his knee and the taste of almonds in his mouth, and did not move, in case moving ended it.

⁂

It ended with the bar.

The guard captain stood in the doorway with the dark of the stair behind him, and the stair came in with him: wet lime, cold, and his own smell of wool rained on and dried too close to a fire. Zoser had learned the captain’s hands in four days: a pikeman’s palms gone soft from holding keys, the right thumbnail split down its middle. The key-ring hung from the left, and its iron smelled, when he lifted it, like a coin held in the mouth. With the right he scratched under his jaw while he spoke, at the wall above their heads, in the Roman speech, which was the friar’s Latin with its bones broken.

Zoser caught the word for *Cardinal*. Nothing else.

On his way out the captain’s free hand went down to the board, quite idly, and broke a fig off the end of the string, and he went out chewing it. The bar came down.

“At first light,” Angeska said in Amharic, with no colour in it at all, as she rendered tolls, “the Cardinal’s men will come and search these rooms. For anything carried.”

Beyond the door the priest’s hand went to his belly and lay there flat.

Zoser’s went to his thigh, to the scrip, and came away too late. Through the doorway the old man was looking at him, his eyes two wet points in the dark. They held on Zoser’s thigh a moment, and went back to the dark.

Then the old man said, loudly, “They have been in my satchel.”

⁂

“They come in while we sleep.” The priest had the satchel open on his mat and was going through it with both hands, by what light came through the narrow door from the lamp, which Zoser had set back in its niche. “Look at it. The cross was on the top. I put it on the top.”

“It is on the top, husband,” Angeska said.

“It is on the *side*.” He shook the brass hand-cross at her. “The brother. The one who brings the pot. He looks at everything. I have seen him look at the bag.”

“The bar is on the outside. Only the guard has the key.”

“Then the guard.” He was breathing fast through his nose. “Every one of them in this city is a thief. They take the bread out of a man’s mouth and then they come in the night and put their hands in his—” He stopped. His hand had gone to his ribs. “In his things.”

Zoser watched from the narrow doorway. *Hunger*, he thought: five weeks of the fast on a winter of not eating. In the last week of the *Hudade* the old monks at Lalibela heard their names called in the rock and accused the novices of eating the candles. He had carried this man across a river. He did not know what he was looking at.

Angeska put her hand on her husband’s wrist. “Lie down. I will sit by the bag.”

After a while he lay on his side with his knees drawn up and both arms across his belly, facing the wall, and Angeska sat by his head with the satchel in her lap.

Zoser went back to his own room.

It was hardly wider than a man lying down, and four of a man long: at the far end the bench under the barred window; at this end the board on its trestles, the narrow door to the inner room, and in the corner beyond it a low door on a closet where a plank seat stood over a shaft that breathed up the stink of the friary ditch. His pallet lay along the wall facing the narrow door. In the doorpost, at hip height, a niche was cut, and in it stood the night-lamp, a little clay boat of oil with a twisted cotton wick. The doorway was so narrow that his own shoulder brushed the jamb. Anyone going from the inner room to the closet in the night went past that lamp close enough to scorch a sleeve.

He sat down on his pallet across from it and drew the scrip round onto his knee.

There were two places where the Cardinal’s men would not find the strip.

The shaft: a rag on a thread, looped round a nail under the seat. If the thread broke, the strip would go down into the friars’ filth, and no one would ever read what his mother had written, least of all himself.

The window: a crack at the foot of one bar where the mortar had fallen out. And in the morning a guard’s hand running idly along the bars, and Rome would have it.

Or.

Under his thumb, inside the soft old leather, was the hard roll. He had sewn it into the lining his first night at Lalibela, at seven, so badly that the sacristan laughed and showed him how; he had re-sewn it three times since, small and even, and never unrolled it. *Let no priest see it.* He had been her son. He had obeyed.

He could read it tonight, and know it. Then let them take the scrip, the strip, the whole of him. They could not take that.

He was ashamed of the thought, because it was not fear only. Under the fear was the small, hard, hungry thing that had warmed him on the plank while he paid out the Latins’ Creed for a man who called him clay. He wanted one thing of hers in this city of barred doors that no cardinal could put his hand on. He wanted it as he had wanted the almond milk. He knew what that made him.

He set the point of his knife under the first stitch, and lifted.

⁂

His own stitches did not want to come out. Each had to be found by touch and lifted on the tip of the blade; twice he cut leather instead. The lamp burned in its niche across from him on the friars’ cheapest oil, smelling of old olives and hot dust. In the inner room the priest’s breathing had gone long and rough.

Twelve stitches. Fifteen. The lining came away in a little mouth, and the end of the roll showed in it, pale, and across the outermost turn a brown line of ink, very faint.

Beyond the door the breathing stopped.

He drew the edge of his shamma over his knee, scrip and all, and sat still.

The old man was standing in the inner room, a pace back from the threshold. Zoser knew it by the shape that came out of the dark at the edge of the lamplight and by the sour smell of a body long fasting. The priest stood there in his shift, barefoot on the stone, one hand flat beneath his ribs, the other hanging. He was not looking at the lamp, or toward the closet he had presumably risen to go to. He was looking through the doorway at Zoser.

He stood so long that Zoser counted twenty breaths and lost the count. The old face held no anger and no question; only a kind of listening, as if someone had spoken and he was waiting to hear it again.

*He is counting the room for thieves.* Zoser felt a pity so sharp it was almost disgust, and under it something cold, because the face had turned toward him and stayed turned, and he did not know why.

Then the priest came forward, and through.

He could not keep his distance from anything in that doorway. He came sideways, his shoulder to the far post, his belly drawn in, and his hip went by the lamp within a hand’s breadth.

The flame bent.

It went over at its root, not stooping and righting itself but laid down in one slow movement, as a reed is laid to the ruled line, and it held there at a slant across the dark with its smoke going straight out from its tip. The yellow drained out of the top. What was left was the blue that iron-gall ink has for one breath after it leaves the pen, before the air blackens it.

On Zoser’s knee, under the edge of the shamma, the ink on the strip moved.

He felt it before he saw it: a stir under the cloth, a hair lifting. Where the wool had slipped, the brown line across the end of the roll had drawn itself together. It was leaning, he would have sworn to it, the whole line in the same breath, toward the doorway and the lamp.

The old man went on past. His shift brushed Zoser’s shoulder. The closet door scraped to behind him.

The flame stood up, straight and yellow in its clay boat, and a thread of smoke went up from it and broke and went up again. On Zoser’s knee the ink lay still. A brown line. Old. Faded. Nothing.

He made himself see it again. The flame had not leaned toward the closet, where the old man was going. It had laid itself across the doorway, along the one line in the room on which the passing hip and Zoser’s knee and the open lining lay one behind the other. The old man had only come between. And the ink had leaned back along that line to the lamp, as a thing answers when it is called.

He had seen a flame go over once before: the captain’s candle on the ship, leaning down the line toward the place where he knelt; Desta looking at it, and then at the scrip, and walking off the end of the plank. What the candle did afterwards he had never made mean anything. But the strip had been sewn then. Now it lay open, and the lamp had leaned, and the ink had leaned to meet it.

*Let no priest see it.* She had not meant priests only. She had meant this.

*He died because the light wanted me.*

The thought sat down in him and did not get up. He looked at the lamp’s ordinary yellow tongue and went cold from the scalp down.

He reached out and pinched the wick.

It bit his fingers. He held on until it was dead. The smell of hot oil and singed cotton came up into his face, and the dark came in from the corners and took the room.

The closet door scraped. The old man came back feeling along the wall, his bare feet dragging, and stopped.

“Who has put out the light?”

“The oil was going, Qess.”

A grunt. The feet came on. At the narrow door they stopped again, and Zoser, in the black, with his hand clamped over the open lining, felt the old man stand there a second time, a long time, in a dark too thick to see him in. Then the feet went on, down onto the mat, and after a while the breath came long and rough again.

Zoser sat with his fingers burning and waited for his heart to slow.

⁂

There was no moon. It had been dying all week, a paring in the east before dawn, and now it rose too late and too thin to matter; the nights in the friary were the inside of a shut book.

But the court had its brazier.

He took the scrip, the knife, the psalter and the shell of soot-ink down the length of the room to the window, as far from the dead lamp as the walls allowed, and knelt on the bench with his face to the bars. Below, the court was a square of dark with one red eye in it: the watch-brazier by the gate, a stone’s throw off, and beside it a guard on an upturned tub with his head nodding. Its glow came up weak and red and lay along the sill in stripes between the bars. When the wind came off the river the embers woke and the stripes brightened, enough to show the grain of the stone; when it dropped they sank to almost nothing. The smoke reached him faint and sour, charcoal and pitch, and under it the cold mud of the Tiber.

A fire that far off could not bend to anything. It could only glow.

He finished the work in the red. Eight stitches more, and the lining opened its whole length, and he drew out the roll.

It lay across his palm, wrapped round something hard, browned at the edges. Near one end he could feel a thicker place where she had folded the edge over before she rolled it. His mother’s fingers had made that fold. He did not unroll it yet. His other hand, without his leave, went up under his shamma to the place below his left collarbone.

The scar was there. One short cut, a barleycorn long, raised just enough for a fingertip to find in the dark. He had always taken it for a hurt from the famine road. In the river at Cassino the cold had found it first, a line of ice laid exactly along that barleycorn of skin, and he had thought nothing of that either.

Tonight his finger went back and forth along it, and it did not feel like a hurt. It felt like the first stroke of something. A pen set down on the ruled line, drawn a little way, and lifted. The beginning of a word, with the rest not yet written.

His mother’s shins had been written on.

He had not thought of it in fifteen years, and now he could feel it under his hand: sitting in the hollow of her knees, running his finger along the ridges on her shin as he would later run it along the lines in the abbot’s books. Short, short, long, short. Row under row. He had not known it was writing. He had thought it was her. A child does not ask what his mother is made of.

And the floor.

It came to him all at once, whole: a page falling open.

Mud brick, smoothed with the flat of a hand and swept, and dust on it, fine and pale, smelling of dry earth and the goats outside. The light came in low and gold through a doorway with no door in it. He was on his knees. She was on her knees beside him, her shoulder against his, and she smelled of smoke and fenugreek, the sweetish bitterness of the seeds she ground on the stone, which was always on her fingers. She was talking to him, as she always was, in the river tongue, her own tongue, low and quick and full of a falling music, and he was listening not to the words but to her.

She put out one finger and drew in the dust.

A stroke down. A stroke across. A short foot kicked out at the bottom. The dust lifted on either side of her finger and settled. He knew he must not breathe on it.

Then she said, in the speech of the highland houses, slowly, setting each word apart, to be kept: “The first letter is the mouth opening.”

She opened her mouth to show him. *A.* He opened his. *A.* She laughed at him, and said something in the river tongue that made her eyes go narrow and kind, and he laughed because she had.

Then she laid her palm flat over the letter and wiped it away. One movement. The dust was smooth.

“So no priest sees,” she said.

In the highland speech again. And then the river tongue came back, a long rise and fall of it, and he could hear every turn of it, the lift of her voice at the end of a question, the little catch before she laughed. Once, in the middle of it, there went by the one word he had kept, the one she called him when he fell and would not cry; he knew it as he knew his own hand, and it was gone downstream before he could close on it. The rest he could not tell the meaning of. He had known once. He had known it before he knew anything. It ran past him now like water under ice, and he knelt on the bank with his hands out.

The memory stayed whole. He could not get into it.

He was on his knees on a bench in Rome with his fingers on a scar and his eyes running. Nobody could see.

⁂

He unrolled the strip.

It came open stiffly, turn by turn, and out of the innermost turn something fell into his palm: a cross no longer than the top joint of his thumb, cut from a single pale shaving of wood, its arms flared at the ends in the old Aksumite way. It was so light he felt only its edges. He closed his hand on it, held the strip flat on the sill with the other, its top edge in the stripe of red between two bars, and leaned close, until his breath came back off the parchment onto his face.

The signs were small and close together, made by a hand that had not hurried. And he did not know them.

He knew them almost. That was worse. They leaned and branched where his own letters stood upright, and some of them hooked back on themselves like a vine on a pole; and his letters wore their vowels, little feet and rings and strokes, and these wore none. They were what his letters had been, perhaps, before anyone stood them straight and dressed them: the grandfathers’ letters, the abbot had called the writing on a worn stone the Aksum pilgrims once brought him.

They ran from left to right, like his own. At the end of the first line one sign stood a little apart from the rest, larger, as if the others were waiting for it.

He could not read in the dark between the gusts. So he waited, his finger set under the first group, and when the wind came and the embers woke he said its shape under his breath. Scribes read aloud. He had never learned to read any other way.

The hand he was sure of: the letter of his own tongue that sounded *k* had once been a hand, the old men said, and you could still see the fingers in it. Then the light sank, and he knelt in the dark with the next group under his finger and turned it over by memory. The two strokes and the hook he tried three ways on his tongue, and none of them sat, until the hook made him think of a strap over a shoulder, and the whole group of a man bent under something. The wind came. He looked again, and was sure, and chose *carry*, and said it.

The strip moved under his finger.

He snatched his hand back. The line lay brown and still. Out in the court the brazier’s glow rose and sank; nothing in it bent. In the inner room the old man’s breath went in, and caught, and went on.

He put his finger back. He told himself it had been the light.

The knotted cord stopped his heart, because he knew its sound before he had worked it out. *Z, k, r.* It was in *tezkar*, the feast on the fortieth day, when the family brings bread to the church and the priest says the dead man’s name. A knot was a thing tied so as not to forget. *Remember.* He said it, and his voice did something he had not intended, and he had to stop.

The parchment had grown warm.

Under his left thumb, where he held the strip to the sill, warmth had come up out of it, steadier than his own hand could have given it in that cold. His thumb had been wet with the sweat of the work. It was dry now. He lifted it, and put it back, and the warmth was waiting.

And there was a smell.

It came up very faint out of the open lining on the sill, where the roll had lain fifteen years. Not leather, not sweat, not charcoal. Cold dust. Old hide. Ink gone brown. The smell of the treasury at Lalibela on the one day a year the sacristan unlocked the chest of the oldest books and the boys were let stand at the door and breathe. He had loved it more than incense.

The wind came again. The roof he took for *house*, because the letter that sounded *b* had once been a house; and a house with someone under it, belonging there, was *whose*. Whose roof you are under. Whose you are.

One sign was left, standing apart at the end of the line. He waited a long while in the dark for the gust that would show it.

A stroke rising from a short bar and parting at its top into three, the middle one tallest. He had no sound for it. A sign is not a picture, the old men said, and boys who read pictures into letters read nonsense.

*End.* That would bear it: a stroke rising and dividing, a thing come to its last. Lay it down; finish with it.

*Let go.* That fitted too, and better. *Let go of what you carry.* A mother to a son.

Or *burn.*

Any of the three would bear the sign, and there was no one to tell him which. Not the abbot. Not Angeska with her seven tongues, asleep in the next room, who in all their road had never met a script she could not read. Nobody alive, perhaps, but him, on a friar’s bench at midnight, with his mother’s last words under his thumb.

He found that he liked it.

It was the small hard thing from the plank, with the same taste. He was ashamed of it, and it warmed him; and because it warmed him he wanted the strongest word, the one that would have been in her mouth on the floor of the church with the juniper stacked round her, the one nobody else could give him.

The old letters put their deed at the end, as the market speech of home did and the altar’s tongue never would. He turned the line in his head into the altar’s order, and then, in the next gust, he read it aloud, under his breath, from the first sign to the last, with each word set down whole, in the voice of the lesson.

***Burn what you carry, while you remember whose you are.***

In the inner room the priest said a word.

It came through the narrow door quite clearly, in a sleeper’s voice, thick and slow, in no tongue Zoser had ever heard him use awake: not Ge’ez, not Amharic, not the Arabic of his sermons, and not the Greek of that night in the snow, in the church porch outside Capua, either. It had the shape of the guards’ talk in the court, the broken Latin of the city.

“*Custode.*”

On the sill, under Zoser’s thumb, the line of brown signs stirred and drew together, all of it, in the same breath.

He did not move. The old man’s breathing went on, rough and even; he did not wake; he said nothing more. Slowly the ink lay down on the parchment and was ink. The warmth stayed.

*What you carry.* He had carried one thing all his life, from Lalibela to the sea and up the length of this country on his own thigh, and it lay under his hand. And she had written on it: *Burn.*

No. He said it to her, not aloud. He would remember whose he was, and keep it as well.

He rolled the strip again round the little cross along its old folds.

Then he opened the psalter at the first leaf, dipped the reed in the soot-ink, and in the margin, beside the crooked letter a child had pressed into the vellum in charcoal the week he came up the mountain with the ash of the road still in his hair, he drew the same letter again. A stroke down. A stroke across. A short foot kicked out at the bottom. It went on brown in the red light. He held his breath over it until it dried, so as not to blow on it.

Two of them, side by side, the small and the large. The mouth opening.

He did not sew the lining shut.

He put the roll inside his shamma, against his skin, where it lay warm along the old scar. He would read it again at dawn, and every dawn, until he had it as he had the psalms.

Below, the guard by the brazier woke with a jerk, looked up at the dark windows of the friary, settled his pike, and slept again.

Some while after, in the inner room, the rough breathing broke off. Zoser heard the mat creak, and a long scrape, as of a back going up a wall; then nothing, and no sound of lying down again.

⁂

They came at first light.

He had got as far as *you carry* when the bar scraped. The strip was rolled and inside his shamma before the door was fully open, and he knelt on the bench with the psalter in his lap, a scribe at his morning office.

Four of them, in the Cardinal’s livery, bends of silver and red with a rose at the shoulder, smelling of last night’s wine and this morning’s garlic. Their leader carried no pike. He had a clerk’s hands, long and dry, the second finger callused where a pen lay, a plain silver ring on the little finger, and he used them as if they were someone else’s tools: he pointed, and the men went.

They turned the pallet over and tipped out his bag. One put his head into the closet and pulled it out again, swearing.

The leader took the psalter out of Zoser’s lap and riffled it. At the first leaf he stopped, and tilted it to the window.

“*Clavis*,” he read. “*Ostium. Vas.*” He laughed, short and pleased, and said something to the man beside him, and the man laughed too. A boy’s primer; a heathen learning his words. His long finger tapped the margin beside the two አ, and Zoser stopped breathing, and the finger went on to the next leaf, and the leader tossed the book back onto the bench.

One of the men picked the scrip up off the sill.

He hefted it. He squeezed it once in his fist, as the corporal had on the bridge at Capua, the leather giving and the lining giving under it: empty, soft, an old bag with a torn inside. He dropped it on the floor.

Against Zoser’s skin the roll lay warm.

They went through into the inner room. Zoser heard Angeska’s bundle go over and the little pot of cloves roll across the stone, then the priest’s satchel. Through the narrow door he could see the old man. He sat upright on his mat with his back to the wall, as he must have sat since that hour in the night when he woke, his shift dark with sweat at the neck and under the arms, both arms locked across his belly, his eyes fixed on the wall opposite. The thumb of his right hand went round and round in the hollow of his left palm.

The leader glanced in at him: an old black man in a sweated shift, grey-lipped, holding himself. He said a few words. The men laughed.

None of them went near him.

They went out. The bar came down. It had taken less time than a psalm.

In the inner room the old man went on sitting as if the men were still coming. Then, long after, his arms came loose all at once, and he lay down on the bare floor beside the mat and was asleep before Angeska could put the blanket over him. He slept the day through, and did not wake for the bread.

Angeska came through the narrow door with the cloves in her hand and stooped for Zoser’s scrip where the man had dropped it.

The lining hung out of it, open from end to end, the pale ends of the cut stitches standing up along the seam.

She looked at it. She turned it a little in the grey light, as he had seen her turn a toll-ticket to read the hand.

Then she held it out to him, and when he took it she went back to her husband, and said nothing at all.

Every dawn of that week he read the line again.

He woke before the friars’ bell and knelt under the window until the grey came up enough to see by, smelling of Tiber mud and wet tiles and the first ovens on the Ripa. He unrolled the strip with the little cross in his palm, set his finger under the open hand, and read to the end under his breath; and every morning the parchment had warmed under his thumb before he reached the knotted cord, as if it had been waiting.

*Burn what you carry, while you remember whose you are.*

Each morning he rolled it and put it against his skin, and each morning the needle and thread stayed in the bottom of his bag.

At supper now he left the board and the lamp to the others and took his bowl down the room to the bench under the window, as far from the flame as the walls allowed. He did not let himself think why.

On Tuesday the bread came late, and the old man accused the lay brother of eating half of it on the stair. On Wednesday he ate a little, in his corner, with his back to the lamp. On Thursday Angeska held out her hand for the psalter, and Zoser shut his eyes and said “*Vas*”, and she gave the book back. “Not a new one. Not yet.” On Friday it rained, and the guards’ brazier hissed and stank of tar. On the Sunday the friars hung violet over their crosses, and through the wall their chant had gone grave again. The rose was over.

That Sunday, at the ninth hour, with the pot between them, Angeska lifted her head and said, “*Kubet*.”

She said it exactly. She had the long mountain vowel, and the way a Lasta woman stops the end short in her throat; she had his own voice in it. Zoser stared at her down the length of the room. She did not look at him. She tore the bread and set half of it on the bench by his hand, and went back to the board.

He tasted fennel, and almonds, and the dung-smoke of a valley he would not see again, all at once.

The summons came on the Wednesday after Passion Sunday, with the friary bells ringing terce.

The captain did not come in. He stood in the doorway with his split thumb hooked in the key-ring, said one word over their heads, the Roman word for the Cardinal, and jerked his chin at the stair.

Angeska put on her shawl. The old man rose without help. He had oiled his beard from the pot, and put on the clean shamma kept folded at the bottom of his satchel since Naples, for the Pope; his back, as he went to the door, was very straight. Zoser took up the case with the King’s letter. Under his shamma the strip lay warm against his skin, unsewn. He had read it at dawn, to the end.

The dormitory stair went down in a turn of stone, steep and narrow, each step worn hollow by friars’ feet, smelling of damp lime and old candle-smoke and bean-water from the kitchens below. No lamp burned on it. Grey light came through a slit in the wall at the turn, and the old man went down into it first, one hand on the stone.

On the third step below the turn he stopped.

Zoser nearly walked into his back. The old man stood with one hand on the wall. The other had come up to his face, slowly, as if to touch his beard, and stayed there.

“Qess?”

The priest took his hand away and looked at it.

The fingers were red. A thread of it ran from his left nostril, bright, over his lip and into the white of his beard; and as Zoser watched, one drop gathered at the end of a hair and fell, and struck the worn stone between the old man’s feet, and lay there round and dark and wet, the only colour on the stair.
