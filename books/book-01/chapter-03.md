# CHAPTER 3

## TAMRAT

*The Santa Maria — the same night as Chapter 1*

The cramp took him at the foot of the ladder, and Tamrat stood with one hand on the wet rung and waited for it to finish with him.

It began low on the right side, under the knot of his sash, and it closed the way a fist closes on a coin: slowly, completely, with no thought of opening. He did not bend. A priest of Lalibela does not fold himself in half before sailors, even sailors who cannot see him. He kept his back straight and breathed the tar of the rung, which came away black on his palm, and counted the rungs above his head as the deacons count the strokes of the bell at matins, until the fingers eased and he could draw a breath that did not catch.

Under the cramp it was still there. It was always still there: a weight, low and to the right, that the pain closed over and drew back from, as water closes over a stone.

Thirty days to Italy, the mate had said at Alexandria, if the wind held. Eleven were gone. Then the road; then however many days the Bishop of Rome took to admit a stranger from the end of the earth. He had only to keep what was in him hidden for that long, and keep himself alive to carry it, and then kneel in the Pope’s hall before the whole of the Latin Church and lay it at the Pope’s feet. After that no captain would turn out his bag on a deck. No hand on earth would be permitted to touch him.

The ship lifted under him and fell. Forward, a timber cried out like a beast in a byre.

He let go of the rung.

The hold stank as a pit stinks: of the bilge under the boards, fish-heads and old urine and rot, and of wet wool, and of the vomit of the first night out, which the straw had drunk and would not give back. There was one hatch, and the ladder came down from it at the after end, where he stood. The hold ran away from him under the beams to where the sailors had hung their lamp, far forward, on a nail by the foremast’s foot. It burned fish oil, and it swung on its nail with every roll of the hull, out and back; and the flame stood up inside it as a plumb-line stands, whichever way the housing went.

He watched it stand. He found that he was glad of it, and did not ask himself why.

Four places had been made in the straw the day they came aboard, in the order of their rank; he had seen to that himself. His own was aftmost, under the hatch and the air, as far from that lamp as the hold allowed; a priest does not sleep under a sailors’ light, he had told the mate, and the mate had shrugged. A pace forward lay the soldier’s, because a soldier sleeps between his master and the dark. Then his wife’s. Then, forwardmost, where the timbers sweated and the deck-seams had opened in the squall, the boy’s.

The boy was there already, under the swinging lamp, his psalter open on his knees and rain dripping through the seams beside him. He had taken the only light in the hold for himself. Tamrat noted it. The boy did not look up.

Desta’s place was empty, and the straw had kept the shape of him.

Tamrat looked away from it, and his eyes went back to it as a tongue goes back to a broken tooth: a long hollow, deep at the shoulder, deep at the hip, the head-end raised on the man’s spare shamma, rolled for a pillow. Beside it stood his sandals, mended at the heel with rawhide. The straw where his sword-hand had lain had sprung back.

Tamrat lowered himself onto his pallet as a man lowers a heavy jar, both hands braced. His wife’s shawl was still round his shoulders, where she had put it at the barrel, sodden and smelling of the cloves in her little pot. The cramp put out one finger and touched him again under the sash, gently, to remind him.

His wife came down the ladder behind him with something in her hand.

She brought the clean iron smell of the rain down with her in her wet hair, and nothing round her shoulders. She knelt by him in the dark at the after end, where the lamp did not reach, and held out the thing in her hand. It was a ship’s biscuit, grey, as thick as a roof tile and as hard, and it smelled of weevils and old flour.

“From the cask by the mast,” she said. “The sailors are all forward. No one saw.”

She said it as she might have said *from the market*. He noted that, and that it had taken her the time it took him to climb down one ladder.

“You stole it.”

“You have eaten nothing since Alexandria.” She did not lower the biscuit. “Three dates and the broth on the second day. I have counted.”

“Then you have counted wrongly. Put it away.”

“Tamrat.” She used his name as she seldom did, low and plain. “Is it still with you? The sickness?”

Under the sash the fist began to close.

“It is still with me,” he said.

She looked at him a moment longer, her face giving back nothing, as a well gives back nothing at noon. Then she laid the biscuit by his hand, where he would have to look at it, and stood.

“I will go up for air,” she said, “while the rain is less.”

A wife does not need air. A wife sits by her husband in a stinking hold because that is where her husband is. He opened his mouth to tell her so, and the fist closed, and when it opened again she was gone up the ladder into the rain without her shawl, and he had not called her back for it.

He did not touch the biscuit.

Overhead a sailor dropped a sack.

It struck the planking above his head, a dead flat blow that came through the beams into his spine, and after it, as the sack split, a long dry rattle of something running out across the deck. It was the sound of a bag being emptied.

His hands began to shake.

They began as they always began. First a looseness in the shoulders, as if the bones had been unpinned; then the hands, from the wrists out; then the jaw, so that his teeth touched and parted and touched. He pressed his hands flat on his knees, and they shook under his own weight. He heard again his brass hand-cross strike the deck and roll; his censer bounce, empty, chain and all; the dates fall soft as dung; the case of the King of Kings’ letter go rolling into the tar while a heathen with a ring in his ear held the Ge’ez up to the lantern and laughed. Then hands in his sash, at his breast, under his arms.

He shut his eyes and began the story.

*I have debated with the finest minds in Constantinople,* he told himself, in the words he always used, *and found them all wanting.* He had said it at Abba Talka’s table, at the imam’s table in Alexandria, in the Nubian sand with his lips split. It told itself by now; that was its use. His breath came back to him off his own beard, sour with the sickness of the first night out and the bile of this one.

His hands shook.

So he went further in, where he never went aloud. There had been a hall. He had been thirty-four, and strong, with letters from the Abuna, and there had been a hall with a ceiling of gold. He reached for the ceiling and found a door instead: a great door of bronze, shut, with a step before it worn hollow by the knees of other men. No. The hall. He put the Greeks into it, in their black, their beards combed to points. He gave them three days. He gave them the Word made flesh and the procession of the Spirit, and answered them out of Cyril; and when he looked among them for one face, one Greek with a name and a mouth he could watch change, there was the door again, and the step, and the cold of the step coming up through his shamma.

He began again from the hall. He told it faster.

He had told the short way of it to Desta once, at a fire in the Afar country, and Desta, chewing kolo, had said at the end only, *And then they let you go home*, which had been insolence, and which Tamrat had chosen not to hear.

Above him bare feet went across the hatch, quick and heavy, and a man called out in the Franks’ tongue. Tamrat’s eyes opened, and for a moment he was on his knees at the barrel again, and the captain stood over him with a candle as thick as a wrist, the sweet church smell of the beeswax over the tar, and the flame had bent toward him.

He let himself think that once, as a man puts his hand into a fire to get it over with. Then he looked at the thought with care.

A flame on a deck in a rising wind. The wind at sea was a liar and blew from every side at once; every boatman on the Nile knew it. The flame had bent. Very well. It had bent the way the wind was blowing, and he, at the end of the line, had happened to be where the wind was blowing to.

He held to it. It was the wind.

And the blue had been the cold.

The cramp closed, and held, and opened. Tamrat found that he was breathing in short pulls, like a man who has run, and looking down the hold at the bent head in the light.

Then there was the boy.

Tonight he had heard the boy’s voice go the length of the ship and come back off the sails, saying the Latin of their enemies. Tamrat had no Latin. He had not needed any. Every priest of the Tewahedo knew the sound of the Definition of Chalcedon as a herdsman knows the cough of a leopard in the dark; and at the two words, *duabus naturis*, he had made an old man’s sound, as if he had trodden on a nail. Later, in the Creed, had come the other word, the Latins’ word for the Spirit, the word even the Greeks spat out. The boy had said it as calmly as his own name.

A boy he had taught, beaten and corrected at Bet Medhani Alem since the child came up out of the ashes at seven. He had gone on long after anyone could have needed it, through the Creed and the prayer to the Virgin, his eyes on the captain’s candle and never once on the man he was saving, and Tamrat, kneeling in his own sick at the barrel, had known the tone. It was the voice of a young man reading the lesson better than his master, in front of the bishop, and making sure the bishop heard.

So the boy had saved him. He had bought him back from the sea with apostasy, and enjoyed the buying, and done it in front of his wife and a shipful of Franks, so that Tamrat would owe him the breath in his body every day from tonight until he died.

He would not owe it.

A gift given to bind you is a chain with a ribbon on it, and a man of rank does not kiss the hand that puts on his chain. He would thank God for his deliverance, as was right, and God alone, since it was God who had sent the wave; and the boy might take his Latin up on deck and say it to the fish.

The hull groaned from bow to stern, and far forward the lamp’s wick began to char. He smelled it: the fish-stink of the oil went sweetish and burnt, and thinned, and for a moment, under it, he smelled another lamp altogether.

Ghee, old and thick, in a clay dish. Frankincense gone cold in a cloth. Dusk, and the long shadows of the standing stones laid out across the grass like the fingers of a hand, and the great carved stelae going from gold to grey above him, and an old man’s lamp coming toward him between them, slowly, held at the height of a heart—

He put it away, as he would put a letter back into its case and tie the thong. He had been many places in forty years. A man remembers many lamps.

The fish-stink came back, plain and rank, and he was glad of it.

The shaking had gone down out of his hands into his belly. He looked at Desta’s place, at his elbow, and let himself look.

It was the Afar country he thought of, not the plank. He thought of the escarpment above the salt, where the road out of the lowlands had to climb the shoulder of the northern hills before it could come down to the sea at Massawa: a wall of red rock standing up out of the plain like the side of a jar, the stones too hot to touch by the second hour. His legs had gone from under him, as legs do at sixty-four, without consulting the man above them, and he had sat down in the path and shouted at the boy and his wife for looking at him.

Desta had not said anything. Desta had stooped, and taken him by one wrist and one knee, and swung him up across his shoulders the way the farmers carry barley up from the threshing floor; and had walked up the escarpment with him so. Two hours, Tamrat’s face against the man’s sweating neck and the Afar plain swinging below him, white with salt, and Tamrat commanding to be put down. At the top Desta had set him down against a rock, as gently as a jar of honey, and rolled his shoulders, and said: “I have carried heavier barley, Qess. It complained less.”

Then he had gone back down for the bundles, while Tamrat sat against the rock and told no one that he could not feel his feet.

He had not thanked him. A soldier carries; it is what the emperor pays for.

He found that he had put his hand down into the hollow, where the shoulder had lain. It was cold and damp to the root, and it smelled of the man, faintly, wet wool and the smoke of other men’s fires and the roasted barley he chewed at night, and under it the mould of the straw.

The cramp put out its finger. He drew his hand back.

His bag lay at the foot of his pallet, where his wife had put it, flat and sodden, emptied by the sea of everything the sailors had left in it. It smelled as wet hide smells, rank, and of the rawhide thongs at its mouth, and under those, faint and stubborn in the seams, of the frankincense it had carried down out of the mountains. Along its mouth ran the border of little crosses, stitched in a thread that had once been red and was now the colour of dust.

Ilfinesh had sewn those. The first one, Ilfinesh, God rest her. She had sewn them the winter before he went to the Abuna to be made priest, in the doorway of her father’s house, biting the thread off with her side teeth because she would not wait for a knife; and when he had told her that the crosses were crooked she had laughed at him, and left them crooked. Forty years a priest. Seventeen years since they had wrapped her in her shamma and carried her down to the church. And the other number, the one that came after those—

He stopped the sum. He stopped it as a man stops on a path at the edge of a gorge, with one foot still lifted, and puts the foot down somewhere else.

The cramp came back, and this time it did not close and open. It closed and stayed closed, and under it something turned over, low and to the right, and pressed.

He got up all at once, with his hand on the beam, and his mouth filled with water the way a mouth does before a man is sick.

“I must go up,” he said aloud, to no one. “To be sick. Over the side.”

No one had asked him. Forward, under the lamp, the boy did not lift his head.

Tamrat climbed the ladder with both hands, the tar of the rungs sticking at his palms, and put his head out into the night.

The rain had thinned to a drizzle that smelled of iron and wet pitch. Forward, the lantern burned on its hook with men moving under it. His wife stood by the foot of the mainmast, where she had knelt, with her hair flat to her skull and nothing round her shoulders, facing forward, toward the lantern and the men. She did not turn. He did not call to her.

He went aft to the lee rail, away from the light, and put both hands on the wet wood, and bent over the black water, and his own breath came back to him off the sea, sour as a sick dog’s.

When he came down again his beard was wet with rain and his hands were steady.

He went to his pallet, and past it, a single pace, and knelt down on the straw at the head of Desta’s place, where the rolled shamma lay.

He did not decide to weep. It came up from the same place as the sickness, with as little permission. The boy would hear. Let him hear, then. Let him carry that home in his good memory with the rest. Tamrat told himself he did not care, and found it very nearly true, and let it come. It came without noise, and then with a noise like a dog’s. He bent over the hollow with his forehead nearly touching the rolled shamma, and he wept for the soldier who had carried him up the side of the world like a sack of barley and walked off a plank into the black sea, and for whom no priest had said the prayers, because the only priest on the ship had been crouched behind a barrel.

*Egzio, meharene*, he said into the straw. *Lord, have mercy. Lord, have mercy on thy servant Desta.* The Ge’ez was thick in his mouth, as if he had forgotten how it went.

When it was finished he wiped his face with both palms. The straw under him smelled of salt.

He went forward, stooping under the beams, and the stink of the bilge thickened with every pace until it lay in his throat like a second cramp. Halfway, where the lamplight gave out on the straw in a ragged edge, he stopped. The boy’s end of the hold was the foulest. That was reason enough to stop. He did not look for another.

The boy sat under the lamp, separating the leaves of his psalter with the tip of a straw. It had come out of the sea swollen to twice its thickness, and he was laying a stalk between each pair of leaves so they would not dry together. His hands were very careful. At his breast, on a thong, hung a small leather pouch, greasy with handling, which Tamrat had last seen on another man.

The straw stopped. The boy looked up into the dark, to where he stood.

“The King of Kings’ letter,” Tamrat said.

“Yes, Qess.”

“It lay in the tar. In the morning you will open the case and dry it leaf by leaf, on deck, out of the spray, and let no one handle it. If the ink has run on any line, you will tell me which line before you touch it.”

“Yes, Qess.”

“And tomorrow, while it is in your head, you will write down every question the captain asked tonight, his words as near as you have them, and what each of us answered.” He made himself go on. “Each of us. The abbot will want it. So will the Pope.”

The boy’s head came up a little.

“The map he gave you,” said Tamrat. “Show it me.”

The boy drew it out of his shamma and unfolded it, and flattened it on his knee with the edge of his hand, and turned it to the lamp and to the dark where Tamrat stood.

Tamrat’s eyes could no longer read a psalter in his own lap; at a distance, in a good light, they were as sharp as they had been at twenty. It was a coast in brown ink, a firm line where the land met the sea, and inland a road dotted like stitches through a run of little towers, each named in Latin letters he would not ask the boy to read.

In the lower corner, where a scribe signs, there was a seal.

It was not pressed into wax but printed from a carved stamp in an iron-coloured ink, small and very clear: a circle, and inside the circle a key, standing upright as a key stands in a lock before it is turned.

He knew it. He had seen it on the wharf at Alexandria, cut into a signet of dark iron on the forefinger of the hand that took the pouch of stones. Tamrat noticed rings. A man who owns his ship wears gold to say so. A man who wears plain iron with another’s mark cut into it is a steward, and answers for it.

So the captain had a master. And the master had a seal, and the seal was on the road to Rome.

“Bring it,” Tamrat said. “The road belongs to the embassy, and I lead the embassy.”

The boy folded the map and got up, stooping under the beam, and came out of the light to him. The lamp behind him lit one side of his face and left the rest dark. He held the map out. He did not look at Tamrat’s eyes, and that was how Tamrat knew he had heard.

Tamrat took the map. The thing he had sat in the straw deciding not to say lay in his mouth like a stone.

“You held their words well,” he said.

The boy stood very still.

“Clay that takes a shape,” said Tamrat, “is not nothing.”

He watched it go into the lit half of the boy’s face, as he had watched the candle, not wanting to, unable to stop. The boy’s mouth opened a little, as if a hard answer, kept ready a long time, had gone out of it. Something moved at the corner of his jaw. For a moment, in the bad light, he was the child who had stood in the door of the school at Bet Medhani Alem with the ash still in his hair, waiting to be told where to sit.

The boy did not say *thank you*. He did not say anything. He lifted the pouch from his breast and drew open its mouth, and tipped it, and held out his hand with the grains lying in the flat of it: roasted barley and chickpea, the dry smell of every highland hillside at noon, rising up between them through the stink of the bilge.

The boy had not counted them. Desta had given Tamrat a whole handful at Aswan, uncounted, and Tamrat had not thought, then, what that was.

He held out his own hand, and the boy tipped the grains into it. Tamrat closed his fingers on them, and did not eat them, and did not give them back.

“Put out that lamp,” he said. “The oil is the ship’s.”

He lay on his back in the dark with the map against his breast, above the place where the cramp slept. Every timber spoke. The bilge ran aft and forward under the boards like a long breath drawn and let go, and the burnt-wick stink of the dead lamp crept back along the beams to him. At the forward end the boy lay still, too still for sleep. Beside Tamrat, Desta’s place was empty, and beyond it his wife’s, and the biscuit lay by his hand, smelling of weevils.

He did not sleep. He did the sum again, the days to Italy and the road and the Pope’s antechamber, and when he came to the end of it he did it again, and he held his hands one over the other on his belly, flat, as he had held them at the barrel, the kolo still shut in the lower one.

The rain had stopped. Through the boards of the deck, just above the hatch at his head, he heard voices.

One was a man’s, low and rough, the voice that had told them where to kneel: the mate’s. The other was his wife’s.

He had heard her Latin before: at Abba Talka’s table in the year the Latins came, while the friars let their bread go cold; in the Genoese factor’s counting-room; at the foot of this ship’s plank. He had never once listened to it. It was a servant’s Latin, his own words going out of her mouth a few at a time, slow and formal, her eyes lowered, as a woman carries a full dish across a room; and when the Frank answered she brought the answer back to him, shorter. That was what her Latin was. It was the road his words went out on.

No word of his was going out on it now. There was no one on that deck to render for. It ran softly and fast, with no pause in it for a word, the long vowels set down whole and the endings coming round and round again like a wheel, *-orum*, *-ibus*, *-atur*, the endings the boy had shouted into the wind. The mate said something short. She answered at length. In the middle of it, plain as a stone in a stream, came his own name, *Tamrat*, and then the running sound closed over it again. Then she laughed, low, as he had not heard her laugh in five months on the road.

Tamrat lay in the dark with his hands on his belly and listened to his wife speak the language of Rome, on the night Desta died, to the man who wore Desta’s sword.
